01User Needs and Design InputsAsk a precise question · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Clarify missing information before responding.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A design brief says the device should be "easy to use." It does not identify the intended users or use environment.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

Something a user needs to accomplish in a particular context.
design input
A documented requirement that guides a product's design.
intended use
The purpose for which a product is designed and represented to be used.

The ability to follow an item's history, source, requirements, or records through documented links.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  2. B. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  3. C. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Easy to use for whom?

Fictional design-planning meeting: the brief says easy to use but names neither intended users nor the use environment.

  1. Design engineer: The brief says easy to use. Which intended users and environment does that refer to?
  2. Product manager: Those details haven't been defined, so I can't turn that phrase into a reliable design input yet.
  3. Design engineer: Could we assume trained hospital staff, since that would make the requirement easier to draft?
  4. Product manager: No. That would invent a user group and potentially change the intended-use assumptions.
  5. Design engineer: What should we clarify first with the team that supplied the user need?
  6. Product manager: Who performs the task, what task they perform, and the conditions in which they do it.
  7. Design engineer: Then we can discuss measurable design inputs rather than treating easy as a test criterion.
  8. Product manager: Exactly. Keep traceability from the clarified user need to any proposed requirement.
  9. Design engineer: I'll bring those focused questions to the design meeting and leave the input marked unresolved.
  10. Product manager: I'll identify the appropriate user-research source so the team can confirm the scope before drafting acceptance criteria.
2. A readable screen under which conditions?

Separate fictional device case: a portable reader is intended for trained technicians in a bright clinic room; the brief says readable without specifying distance or display content.

  1. Human factors specialist: We know the users and room conditions. What does readable mean for this portable reader?
  2. Design engineer: The brief doesn't specify viewing distance or which displayed information the technician must distinguish.
  3. Human factors specialist: Could we ask users about the task before choosing a text size as the design input?
  4. Design engineer: Yes. A size selected without task context may not address the actual user need.
  5. Human factors specialist: Should the requirement cover every possible outdoor condition as well as the clinic?
  6. Design engineer: That isn't the supplied intended environment. Any scope expansion needs an explicit review, not an assumption.
  7. Human factors specialist: I'll clarify the viewing task, content, and distance with the intended users.
  8. Design engineer: I'll keep the proposed requirement linked to those findings for traceability.
  9. Human factors specialist: Then our next discussion can address a testable input without promising universal readability.
  10. Design engineer: Agreed. We'll document the confirmed environment and leave untested conditions outside the claim.
3. A user preference mistaken for an approved input

Separate fictional design review: one technician requested a larger carrying handle; no broader user assessment or approved design input exists.

  1. Product owner: A technician asked for a larger handle. Can we mark that as an approved design input?
  2. Systems engineer: It's a useful user comment, but we haven't assessed the need or approved a requirement.
  3. Product owner: What question would help us understand whether size is the actual problem?
  4. Systems engineer: Ask which carrying task is difficult and under what conditions the current handle causes difficulty.
  5. Product owner: Could the request reflect a personal preference rather than a need shared by every user?
  6. Systems engineer: Possibly. We shouldn't assume either interpretation before reviewing the evidence and intended-use context.
  7. Product owner: I'll preserve the original comment and request a focused follow-up instead of promising a redesign.
  8. Systems engineer: Good. Traceability should show how a reviewed need leads to an input, not just copy a suggestion.
  9. Product owner: I'll bring the clarified task to the design discussion and identify what remains unknown.
  10. Systems engineer: Then we can evaluate options against the actual need without treating one proposed solution as already authorized.

Scenario 2 · A one-handed requirement without a task

A fictional design brief requests one-handed operation but does not specify which task or why one hand is occupied. The intended users are clinic technicians. Clarify the task before proposing a design input.

Role A: Design engineer: ask which operation must be performed and under what conditions.

Role B: Product specialist: distinguish the stated request from information that still needs user research.

Possible opening: “Which task needs one-handed operation, and what is the technician doing with the other hand?”

Success checks
  • Clarify the task and use conditions.
  • Avoid expanding the intended requirement without evidence.
  • Agree how the verified need will connect to a testable design input.
Add a complication

A colleague wants to apply the requirement to every device interaction.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Vocabulary for this lesson

hazard
A potential source of harm.
harm
Injury, damage, or another adverse effect on people, property, or the environment.
FMEA
Failure mode and effects analysis, a structured method for identifying how a process or product can fail and prioritizing controls.
residual risk
The risk remaining after controls or other responses are applied.

03Verification, Validation, and Design ReviewExplain a fair comparison · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A device team has checked design outputs against specifications but has not yet completed the planned evaluation of intended user needs.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

verification
Checking whether specified requirements have been met.
validation
Confirming that an output or system meets needs for its intended use under defined conditions.
design review
A structured evaluation of a design at a defined stage.
acceptance criteria
Specific conditions a deliverable must meet to be accepted.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
  2. B. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  3. C. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.
  2. B. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  3. C. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

human factors
The study and application of knowledge about how people interact with products, tasks, and environments.
use error
An action or omission during use that leads to a different result from the intended one; context and design matter.
formative study
An evaluation used during development to identify problems and improve a design.
summative study
An evaluation conducted to assess performance against defined requirements after development work.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

05Regulatory Pathways and SubmissionsAsk for an actionable next step · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Make a request with a clear action, purpose, and realistic timing.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A presentation refers to a proposed device pathway as settled, although the regulatory team is still evaluating options.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

510(k)
US FDA premarket submission that typically demonstrates substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device.
De Novo
A US FDA classification process for certain novel devices without a legally marketed predicate.
PMA
Premarket approval: the US FDA review pathway for certain high-risk medical devices.
predicate device
A legally marketed device used as a comparison in an applicable US FDA submission.

03 · Notice the language

Ask for an actionable next step

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  2. B. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
  3. C. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  2. B. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.
  3. C. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Request a pathway assessment, not a presumed route

Fictional submission planning: a slide presents the proposed device pathway as settled, although regulatory specialists are still evaluating options.

  1. Project manager: The slide presents our proposed pathway as settled. What confirmation should I request before using it?
  2. Regulatory lead: Ask for the current assessment, supporting assumptions, and unresolved questions from the regulatory team.
  3. Project manager: Could I leave 510(k) as confirmed because it is already printed in the timeline?
  4. Regulatory lead: No. A planning label doesn't establish that route or an agency outcome.
  5. Project manager: What specific action do you need from the product team for the assessment?
  6. Regulatory lead: Please confirm the current device description and intended use before our internal review meeting.
  7. Project manager: Should the slide list De Novo and PMA as equally appropriate alternatives while review continues?
  8. Regulatory lead: Not unless assessed. We can say pathway under evaluation without inventing a ranking or conclusion.
  9. Project manager: I'll correct the status and send the requested product information for review.
  10. Regulatory lead: I'll return a scoped assessment and identify which questions need further specialist or agency clarification.
2. A similar product is not a confirmed predicate

Separate fictional regulatory exercise: a competitor's brochure describes a superficially similar device; no predicate assessment has been completed.

  1. Commercial manager: This brochure looks similar to our device. Can we identify it as our confirmed predicate?
  2. Regulatory specialist: Not from the brochure alone. We need the relevant device information and a regulatory assessment.
  3. Commercial manager: What can I request from our research team before tomorrow's strategy discussion?
  4. Regulatory specialist: Ask for the source documents supporting the comparison, including the device identity and intended-use information.
  5. Commercial manager: Would sharing the same marketing category prove suitability for a 510(k) strategy?
  6. Regulatory specialist: No. A marketing category is not a completed predicate-device analysis.
  7. Commercial manager: I'll call it a candidate for assessment rather than a selected predicate in the briefing.
  8. Regulatory specialist: Please also identify gaps instead of filling missing technical characteristics from appearance or promotional language.
  9. Commercial manager: I'll send the documented comparison questions today and request an initial review status.
  10. Regulatory specialist: I'll assess what is available and explain the next evidence needs without promising a pathway conclusion.
3. A design change needs a scoped regulatory question

Separate fictional planning call: a team proposes changing the device's intended user group; regulatory implications have not been reviewed.

  1. Development director: Can the regulatory team confirm that this user-group change has no submission implications?
  2. Regulatory strategist: Please frame it as an assessment request, not a request to endorse an assumed answer.
  3. Development director: What information would make the request focused enough for the team to act?
  4. Regulatory strategist: Provide the current and proposed user groups, intended tasks, and the related design-change description.
  5. Development director: Could we assume the original pathway remains unchanged because the hardware isn't changing?
  6. Regulatory strategist: We shouldn't infer that. The intended-use context needs review even if the hardware description stays the same.
  7. Development director: I'll request the assessment before we finalize the project plan, with the changes clearly compared.
  8. Regulatory strategist: Good. Ask for an initial scope and timing estimate rather than an immediate blanket assurance.
  9. Development director: I'll leave the pathway implications open and avoid making a 510(k), De Novo, or PMA claim.
  10. Regulatory strategist: I'll coordinate the relevant review and return the verified planning implications when the assessment is available.

Vocabulary for this lesson

complaint

Medical Device Reporting: the US FDA system for specified device-event reports; elsewhere the acronym may refer to a regulation.
malfunction
A failure of a product or system to operate as intended.

Collecting and evaluating information about a product after it enters the market.

03 · Notice the language

Transfer information and responsibility

State the current situation, relevant background, open task, and receiving role. Ask for acknowledgment or repeat-back of the critical item. Sending a message and transferring responsibility are not always the same thing.

  • The current status is ...; the outstanding item is ... .
  • The record shows ... .
  • Please confirm who has accepted ... .

Improve this: Someone should follow up on this.

See a clearer version

Please confirm which team has accepted the follow-up task.

The revised sentence asks for an identifiable receiving team instead of leaving responsibility with an unspecified 'someone'.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence checks that responsibility has been accepted?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

  1. A. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check. It asks for an identifiable person or team responsible for the task.
  2. B. I mentioned the check in my earlier email. Mentioning a task does not show that anyone accepted it.
  3. C. The check appears somewhere in the notes. A written record alone does not identify who will act.
2. Which response best checks a critical handoff detail?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

  1. A. I assume all results are final, so we can finish. It changes pending information into a final result.
  2. B. I saw the message, so no clarification is needed. Receiving a message is not the same as confirming its meaning.
  3. C. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct? This repeats the status and responsibility and invites correction.

Your goal

Distinguish completed work from pending work and explain its impact.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An incoming-material review has identified two nonconforming lots. The investigation is open and disposition has not been authorized.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

nonconformance
Failure to meet a requirement, specification, contract, standard, or approved procedure.
supplier CAPA
A supplier's corrective and preventive action process for addressing identified quality problems and recurrence risks.
process validation
Documented evidence that a process can consistently produce results meeting specified requirements.
lot traceability
Ability to follow a production lot through materials, process steps, tools, inspections, and downstream customers or products.

03 · Notice the language

Give a useful status update

Lead with the current state, then the important change or open item. Use completed-action language only for completed work. Add a next update point when it is known; do not invent a date to make the message sound complete.

  • We have completed ...; ... remains open.
  • As of ..., the status is ... .
  • The next update will cover ... .

Improve this: Everything is progressing well.

See a clearer version

Three checks are complete; two remain open and are being reviewed.

The revision gives observable progress. A positive label alone does not tell the reader what is complete or still needed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence says a review is finished?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The team has completed the review.

  1. A. The team is completing the review. 'Is completing' presents the work as in progress.
  2. B. The team plans to complete the review. 'Plans to complete' describes an intention, not completion.
  3. C. The team has completed the review. 'Has completed' states that the action is finished.
2. Which update distinguishes a plan from a confirmed fact?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it.

  1. A. The delivery plan proves that arrival will be Friday. A plan cannot prove a future arrival time.
  2. B. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it. This explicitly labels the date as planned and identifies the missing confirmation.
  3. C. Delivery is confirmed for Friday because we planned it. A plan does not establish carrier confirmation.

1. Two nonconforming lots still await disposition

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

labeling
The information supplied with or about a regulated product, as defined by applicable rules.
instructions for use
The manufacturer's information explaining the intended use and operation of a device.
off-label
A use outside a regulated product's approved or cleared labeling, as applicable to that product and jurisdiction.
clinical evidence
Information from clinical evaluation or research relevant to a health-related question.

03 · Notice the language

Disagree with a useful reason

Acknowledge the goal without pretending agreement. Name the specific problem and its implication, then ask a focused question or propose a next step. Be direct when a safety or ethical boundary requires it.

  • I understand the goal. My concern is ... .
  • That statement goes beyond ... .
  • Could we ... before confirming ...?

Improve this: That idea is ridiculous.

See a clearer version

My concern is that the proposal assumes capacity we have not confirmed.

The revised sentence identifies a testable concern about the proposal. It leaves room for a constructive response.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which response identifies a specific assumption and evidence against it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open.

  1. A. I would like another discussion before we proceed with the proposal. This requests discussion without stating the specific assumption or evidence.
  2. B. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open. It connects the concern to a specific assumption and record.
  3. C. The proposal appears optimistic, and I would prefer a slower approach. This expresses a preference, but does not identify the assumption or the record that contradicts it.
2. Which follow-up makes disagreement actionable?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan?

  1. A. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan? It names the unresolved condition and a practical next step.
  2. B. Could we confirm the plan and discuss the missing approval later? This confirms the plan before resolving the stated approval condition.
  3. C. Could we ask for general feedback before sending the next update? General feedback does not directly address the missing approval.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

1. A demonstration outside the session materials

Fictional clinical-training session: a trainer is asked to demonstrate a use beyond the approved session materials; no procedure is taught in this language exercise.

  2. Training coordinator: Could you show it briefly and say it isn't part of the standard training?
