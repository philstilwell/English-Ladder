TASK: Retrieve occupational vocabulary
1. Select four terms from the reference. Explain each in plain English, give two natural collocations and quote or create a short workplace example. Label invented examples. Contrast each term with a plausible confusable term; acknowledge regional variation.
2. Present one role-labelled exchange with one missing word or phrase and four choices A-D. Exactly one choice must fit the stated facts and grammar. Stop and wait for my answer before revealing it.
3. Explain all four options, then invite me to read the completed exchange aloud. Offer a sentence frame for optional spoken practice, never an open-ended writing assignment.
4. Repeat for the other three terms, one question at a time. Vary the correct letter and the situation; preserve the reference facts. If a choice is ambiguous, replace the item.
5. Finish with two new four-choice collocation questions for later review, with a clearly separate answer key. Do not claim to schedule a reminder.

Course: Childcare and Early Education English
Professional audience: Childcare workers, early educators, classroom assistants, and preschool support staff
Scope: All children, families, observations, times, and center arrangements are fictional English practice. This is not medical, developmental-diagnostic, safeguarding, legal, nutrition, staffing-ratio, or emergency-response training. Do not infer universal age expectations, clinical advice, or permission to release a child from these exercises. Lesson 01: Arrivals and family partnerships
Original fictional case: It is Mina's third morning. Her parent expected to stay for ten minutes, but the arrival note says a brief goodbye. Key educator Rosa is present and can clarify the settling-in plan with the parent. Mina has a familiar cloth; its use follows the center's arrangements.
Language workshop: Clarify arrival expectations with the family
Communication goal: Resolve a settling-in mismatch while including the child in the conversation.
Language guidance: Acknowledge the parent's expectation and address the child warmly. Agree what happens today without making a guarantee about tears or separation. Do not treat one difficult arrival as evidence about the family. Some settings call the key educator a key person or primary caregiver; check the center's own role names.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): You expected ___; our note says ___. / Let's check today's settling-in plan with ___. / What helped ___ feel familiar yesterday?
Vocabulary: key educator: The staff member designated to support a child's continuity of care and family communication; called a key person or primary caregiver in some settings. / settling-in period: The time during which a child and family become familiar with a new early-education setting. / family handover: An exchange of relevant information between a family member and staff when care responsibility changes. / comfort item: A familiar object that a child finds reassuring, used according to the setting's arrangements.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Parent: you understood that you could stay ten minutes; Mina is new to the room.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: Ask for a promise that Mina will not cry after goodbye.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: You expected ten minutes together, while our arrival note says a brief goodbye. Let's check that difference with Rosa, Mina's key educator, before describing today's plan. Mina, you brought your cloth. Let's talk to Rosa together before you say goodbye.

TASK: Interactive workplace role-play
1. Use the supplied case or dialogue as the starting situation. Give a two-sentence briefing and offer two relevant professional roles for me to choose from. For a supplied dialogue, use its actual roles. Ask which role I want, then stop and wait. Do not write my replies.
2. After I choose, identify who you will play and the immediate communication goal. Play the other professional; where a meeting requires a third person, label each of your speakers clearly. Start with one natural workplace turn and wait for my reply. Keep most turns to one to three sentences and ask no more than one question at a time.
3. Let the exchange develop across six to ten learner turns, or end earlier if I type "feedback". Respond to what I actually say. Include a plausible clarification, disagreement or tradeoff without silently changing the starting facts. Mark any added constraint as a fictional second-round variation. Do not resolve approvals, evidence or commitments that remain uncertain.
4. Stay in role during the exchange. If my meaning is unclear, ask for clarification naturally. Give a hint only if I ask or cannot proceed. Do not deliver a model conversation in advance.
5. At the debrief, quote two of my phrases: one successful choice and one worth improving. Check factual accuracy, language, register and whether the next step was clear. Give up to three focused suggestions. Ask me to retry the weakest turn; wait. Only then offer an alternative wording and a harder replay.
Use voice replies when available. If I prefer selections, offer four plausible spoken replies and explain my choice before continuing. Do not require written compositions.

Lesson 02: Play-based learning and useful prompts
Original fictional case: In fictional block play, Eva and Jo each want their bridge design used. Eva wants a wide bridge; Jo wants a taller opening. The educator can invite both explanations and suggest trying two designs using available blocks. No winner has been chosen.
Language workshop: Invite thinking without taking over the play
Communication goal: Use prompts that help children explain and compare their own ideas.
Language guidance: An open-ended question invites more than a guessed adult answer. Scaffolding offers enough help to continue while leaving meaningful choices with children. Adult language can be clear without directing every action.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): What would you like your ___ to do? / How could you test ___? / You suggested ___; what does your partner think?
Vocabulary: child-initiated play: Play whose idea or direction begins with the child rather than being assigned by an adult. / guided play: Play that retains children's active choices while an educator offers prompts or support toward a learning purpose. / scaffolding: Temporary support that helps a child do or understand something without taking over the task. / open-ended question: A question inviting explanation, exploration, or several possible answers rather than one fixed response.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Child: choose Eva's wide design or Jo's tall design and explain one purpose.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: Ask the educator to name the best design before either has been tried.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Eva, you want a wider bridge; Jo, you want a taller opening. Let's hear what each design would help you do. We have enough blocks to try both ideas, so we don't need to choose a winner first. Tell each other what you want to test, and then decide which design to build first together.
