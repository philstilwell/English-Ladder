Hidden Wildfire Damage: A Permanent Loss Below Our Feet

Advanced · C1+

News archive →
📅 September 13, 2026 - Hidden Wildfire Damage: A Permanent Loss Below Our Feet

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Bangor University researchers say the scale of damage from summer wildfires is far greater than what is immediately visible. Professor Davey Jones, leading a survey across Wales, explained that people often see only blackened vegetation, but the true impact lies beneath the surface. Approximately 10 centimetres of topsoil, containing thousands of years of organic matter layer and carbon, were lost in a single event. Jones emphasized that once this essential topsoil is gone, it can never truly recover. This critical soil acts as a water sponge, a carbon store, and a nutrient bank, playing multiple vital roles. Losing it is a double whammy: it worsens climate change while making the remaining habitat far more fragile. The Bangor team has been racing to collect soil samples before heavy winter rainfall washes away the remains. They compare burnt land with adjacent untouched areas, allowing them to measure changes in soil carbon, nutrients, microbial activity, water repellency and erosion risk. Capturing baseline data now is crucial, as soil and vegetation properties can change rapidly. Dr. Kara Marsden warned that early green shoots do not necessarily reflect true or full recovery. Bracken sprouts almost immediately because its rhizomes sit deep in the soil, so the burn layer wouldn't maybe have impacted those. But the heather and gorse have much shallower roots, so you know they're completely gone, and that depth of soil they depend on could take thousands of years to form again. Wildfires also release carbon, nitrogen, and sulphur into the atmosphere, directly contributing to climate change. Early evidence suggests vegetation can return, but more slowly and more fragile, leaving landscapes increasingly susceptible to future burns.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. immediately visible (adverbial phrase): able to be seen right away or on the surface, without closer inspection.
2. blackened vegetation (noun phrase): plants and trees that have been burned and turned dark, often appearing charred or scorched.
3. topsoil (noun): the uppermost layer of soil, typically rich in organic matter and nutrients, vital for plant growth.
4. critical (adjective): of extreme importance, especially in relation to the success or failure of something.
5. double whammy (noun): a situation in which two unpleasant or undesirable things happen at almost the same time, or one after the other, creating a more severe negative effect.
6. worsens (verb): makes something more severe, serious, or unpleasant; deteriorates.
7. fragile (adjective): easily broken, damaged, or destroyed; delicate and vulnerable.
8. baseline data (noun phrase): information gathered at the beginning of a study or project that can be used as a basis for comparison with future data.
9. rapidly (adverb): very quickly and at a great rate; with speed and urgency.
10. susceptible (adjective): likely or liable to be influenced or harmed by a particular thing; vulnerable.

Advanced Grammar: Participle Clauses for Consequence
Participle clauses, often starting with an '-ing' form, can concisely express an action or state that is a result or consequence of the main clause. They help to link ideas, showing a cause-and-effect relationship without using conjunctions like 'and as a result' or 'consequently', making sentences more fluid and advanced.
Example from the text: "Early evidence suggests vegetation can return, but more slowly and more fragile, leaving landscapes increasingly susceptible to future burns."
