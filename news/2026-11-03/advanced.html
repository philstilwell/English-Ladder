Collins Names Mogging Its Word of the Year

Advanced · C1+

📅 November 03, 2026 - Collins Names Mogging Its Word of the Year

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Collins English Dictionary has named mogging its word of the year. Used playfully, the expression describes outperforming or upstaging another person or thing. The term gained currency beyond its younger users in 2026, when its use rose by 210%, having spread among Generations Z and Alpha both online and in everyday speech. Collins managing director Alex Beecroft says lexicographers, specialists who research words for dictionaries, examine how frequently expressions occur. They also analyse recurring contexts to identify linguistic trends, or broader developments in how people use language. Beecroft interprets the choice as evidence that successive generations leave their mark on language. Mogging is often traced back to AMOG, an acronym formed from Alpha Male of the Group. The term has also been associated with bodybuilding forums in the mid-2010s. Far from being invariably insulting, the term can function as either a playful put-down or a compliment, depending on context. Its comparative meaning most often concerns physical appearance, with one person appearing more impressive than another. It circulates in looks maxxing communities, online groups whose members exchange advice on maximising their physical attractiveness. To reflect changes in English in the United Kingdom, Collins draws on varied material, including social media. Its 2025 choice was vibe coding, the practice of creating an app or website by describing it to artificial intelligence rather than manually writing programming code. Another expression on this year’s ten-item list is ghost job, referring to a position advertised by an employer with no intention of appointing anyone.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. upstaging (verb, -ing form): Attracting more attention or admiration than someone else, making their contribution or presence seem less impressive.
2. gained currency (verb phrase, past tense): Became more widely used or recognised, especially when referring to an expression or idea.
3. lexicographers (plural noun): Specialists who research words and their meanings to produce or revise dictionaries.
4. linguistic trends (plural noun phrase): Broader developments in how people use language, identified through recurring evidence.
5. leave their mark on (verb phrase): Influence something in a distinctive way that becomes recognisable.
6. traced back (past participle phrase): Connected through historical evidence or accounts to an earlier form or origin.
7. associated with (past participle phrase): Connected in accounts or understanding with something, without necessarily having originated there.
8. invariably (adverb): Always, without exceptions.
9. draws on (phrasal verb, present simple): Uses available material, knowledge or evidence to inform its work.
10. with no intention of (prepositional phrase): Without having a particular action as an intended outcome, rather than merely failing to achieve it.

Advanced Grammar: Emphatic qualification with “far from” and an -ing form
Use “far from” followed by an “-ing” form to emphatically reject a description. Here, “far from being invariably insulting” rejects the idea that the term is always insulting, while allowing both insulting and complimentary uses.
Example from the text: "Far from being invariably insulting, the term can function as either a playful put-down or a compliment, depending on context."
