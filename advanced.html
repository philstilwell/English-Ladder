📅 November 03, 2026 - Collins Names Mogging Its Word of the Year
Collins’ recognition of mogging highlights how generational slang acquires wider currency while retaining meanings that depend on context.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Collins English Dictionary has named mogging its word of the year. Used playfully, the expression describes outperforming or upstaging another person or thing. The term gained currency beyond its younger users in 2026, when its use rose by 210%, having spread among Generations Z and Alpha both online and in everyday speech. Collins managing director Alex Beecroft says lexicographers, specialists who research words for dictionaries, examine how frequently expressions occur. They also analyse recurring contexts to identify linguistic trends, or broader developments in how people use language. Beecroft interprets the choice as evidence that successive generations leave their mark on language. Mogging is often traced back to AMOG, an acronym formed from Alpha Male of the Group. The term has also been associated with bodybuilding forums in the mid-2010s. Far from being invariably insulting, the term can function as either a playful put-down or a compliment, depending on context. Its comparative meaning most often concerns physical appearance, with one person appearing more impressive than another. It circulates in looks maxxing communities, online groups whose members exchange advice on maximising their physical attractiveness. To reflect changes in English in the United Kingdom, Collins draws on varied material, including social media. Its 2025 choice was vibe coding, the practice of creating an app or website by describing it to artificial intelligence rather than manually writing programming code. Another expression on this year’s ten-item list is ghost job, referring to a position advertised by an employer with no intention of appointing anyone.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. upstaging (verb, -ing form): Attracting more attention or admiration than someone else, making their contribution or presence seem less impressive.
2. gained currency (verb phrase, past tense): Became more widely used or recognised, especially when referring to an expression or idea.
3. lexicographers (plural noun): Specialists who research words and their meanings to produce or revise dictionaries.
4. linguistic trends (plural noun phrase): Broader developments in how people use language, identified through recurring evidence.
5. leave their mark on (verb phrase): Influence something in a distinctive way that becomes recognisable.
6. traced back (past participle phrase): Connected through historical evidence or accounts to an earlier form or origin.
7. associated with (past participle phrase): Connected in accounts or understanding with something, without necessarily having originated there.
8. invariably (adverb): Always, without exceptions.
9. draws on (phrasal verb, present simple): Uses available material, knowledge or evidence to inform its work.
10. with no intention of (prepositional phrase): Without having a particular action as an intended outcome, rather than merely failing to achieve it.

Advanced Grammar: Emphatic qualification with “far from” and an -ing form
Use “far from” followed by an “-ing” form to emphatically reject a description. Here, “far from being invariably insulting” rejects the idea that the term is always insulting, while allowing both insulting and complimentary uses.
Example from the text: "Far from being invariably insulting, the term can function as either a playful put-down or a compliment, depending on context."

📅 November 02, 2026 - Oil Tank Vandalism Kills Birds at Broughshane Waterfowl Park
Examine how an oil spill at Broughshane Waterfowl Park connects vandalism with wildlife losses, an ongoing rescue effort and uncertainty about recovery.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Vandalism of a nearby home heating oil tank caused a spill that killed 13 birds from Broughshane Waterfowl Park. More than 20 birds were taken to the USPCA's headquarters for emergency treatment, yet the death toll continued to rise. The attraction in County Antrim remains closed while work to remove oil from its pond continues. A volunteer arriving to feed the birds noticed a kerosene smell and found the black swans coated in oil. The park houses 120 birds in its collection as well as wild birds. Gary Hayward, chairman of Broughshane Environment Wildfowl and Wildlife Trust, described the incident as a nightmare. Hayward described the USPCA and the Northern Ireland Environment Agency as instrumental in treating birds and clearing the spill. Inspectors deployed oil-absorbing barriers and other countermeasures as mitigation, seeking to limit the spill's impact. The agency identified the substance as kerosene, a home heating oil, and traced its source to a nearby tank. Although the waterway has improved, further work will continue for as long as it is deemed necessary. Hayward hopes the incident will act as a deterrent to vandalism. Having suffered extensive contamination, the birds were bathed and treated as quickly as possible. The team administered charcoal to help counter some of the toxins the birds may have ingested. Hopes that the birds were past the worst were tempered by uncertainty over how much they may have ingested. The incident has also placed rescue staff under substantial emotional and mental pressure. Birds can conceal illness effectively, meaning that outward signs may become apparent only when it is already too late. Most rescued birds are non-native species, and their return is contingent on the pond being completely cleaned.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. death toll (noun phrase): The number of deaths resulting from a particular incident, often reported as that number changes.
2. instrumental (adjective): Making an important contribution to achieving something, without necessarily being solely responsible for it.
3. countermeasures (plural noun): Actions or equipment used to oppose a specific threat or reduce its harmful effects.
4. mitigation (uncountable noun): The reduction of harm or severity, without necessarily removing the problem entirely.
5. traced (verb, past tense): Established where something originated by investigating its source or route.
6. deterrent (countable noun): Something that discourages an action by making its possible consequences seem undesirable.
7. contamination (uncountable noun): The presence of an unwanted, potentially harmful substance on an animal or in an environment.
8. ingested (verb, past participle): Taken into the body by swallowing; a formal term that does not imply deliberate consumption.
9. tempered (verb, past participle): Made less strong or confident by a competing consideration, without being completely removed.
10. conceal (verb, base form): Keep something from being apparent or easily detected, without necessarily doing so consciously.
11. non-native species (plural noun phrase): Types of animals or plants that do not naturally occur in the region concerned.
12. contingent on (adjective phrase): Dependent on a condition being fulfilled; meeting that condition does not necessarily guarantee the outcome.

Advanced Grammar: Perfect participle clauses with a shared subject
A perfect participle clause uses “having” plus a past participle to present an event or experience as preceding the main event. Both clauses share a subject: here, the birds experienced contamination before they were bathed and treated.
Example from the text: "Having suffered extensive contamination, the birds were bathed and treated as quickly as possible."

📅 November 01, 2026 - Rediscovered Beetle Shows Signs of Reproducing in Wales
The discovery of beetle larvae in Pembrokeshire suggests renewed reproduction in Wales, while highlighting the species' demanding habitat requirements and the importance of continued conservation.

Read the story

The short-necked oil beetle has been rediscovered in Pembrokeshire after an apparent absence of 60 years from Wales. Recent surveys had prompted fears of local extinction, meaning that the beetle might no longer exist in Wales. Across the UK, the species went unreported for almost 60 years after 1948, as habitat loss brought it to the brink of extinction. Last year's survey at Castlemartin recorded only dead adults, prompting concern about the species' persistence at the site. After three years of searching, experts discovered larvae, or immature beetles, in a small area of the site, suggesting that they had located part of a larger batch of young beetles. According to Natural Resources Wales, the number and distribution of the larvae corroborated this interpretation. Anne Hobbs, from the Bumblebee Conservation Trust, welcomed indications that the species was reproducing in Wales again. The beetle relies on solitary bees for its life cycle and requires both thick vegetation for cover and abundant flowers. Natural Resources Wales said these exacting habitat requirements leave only a few suitable sites in the UK. The species is classified as nationally rare in Great Britain and is listed as vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. The beetles are typically encountered between March and June in coastal grassland rich in flowers and in sand dunes, with activity greatest on warm, sunny days. Rediscoveries elsewhere in the UK included Devon in 2006 and the Isle of Coll in Scotland in 2009. Castlemartin Range remains the species' only known Welsh location and is subject to sustained monitoring by conservationists. The work forms part of Wales's flagship species recovery programme, which also seeks to conserve other species sharing similar habitats. Hobbs stressed that effective conservation must accommodate the needs of several species.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. apparent absence (noun phrase): A period when something seems to be missing, although its actual absence has not been established.
2. local extinction (noun phrase): The disappearance of a species from a particular area, even though it may continue to exist elsewhere.
3. brink of extinction (noun phrase): A situation in which a species is close to disappearing completely.
4. persistence (noun): The continued existence of a species in a location despite conditions that threaten it.
5. corroborated (verb, past tense): Provided additional support for an interpretation, making it more credible without necessarily proving it.
6. exacting (adjective): Demanding precise conditions that can be difficult to satisfy.
7. vulnerable (adjective): Assigned a conservation category indicating a significant risk of extinction.
8. sustained monitoring (noun phrase): Repeated observation maintained over time to track a situation or detect changes.
9. flagship (noun used attributively): Used to identify a particularly prominent programme that represents an organisation's wider work or priorities.
10. accommodate (verb): Take different needs into account and make provision for them.

Advanced Grammar: Supplementary participle clauses expressing inference
A supplementary “-ing” clause can express an inference from the preceding main clause. Here, “suggesting” presents an interpretation of the discovery without treating it as established fact; it comments on the finding as a whole.
Example from the text: "After three years of searching, experts discovered larvae, or immature beetles, in a small area of the site, suggesting that they had located part of a larger batch of young beetles."

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. financial headroom (noun phrase): The capacity within a budget to meet additional costs without financial difficulty.
2. eligibility threshold (noun phrase): A minimum requirement someone must meet to qualify for support or an opportunity; here, a specified period of seeking work.
3. absorb (verb): Use up part of someone's available income, leaving less for other expenses.
4. below-market rate (noun phrase): A price lower than the amount normally charged for comparable accommodation or services.
5. redistribution (noun): The use of taxation and public support to shift income or wealth between groups, especially towards those with less.
6. compounding (verb, present participle): Accumulating as interest is added to a debt and further interest is calculated on the enlarged amount.
7. waived (verb, past participle): Formally not required to be paid, although the charge would otherwise apply.
8. capped (verb, past participle): Restricted by an upper limit, without necessarily being removed.
9. balance the books (verb phrase): Keep income and expenditure in balance so that costs do not exceed available money.
10. offset (verb): Reduce or cancel the financial effect of a saving through a cost elsewhere.

Read the story

For many people in the United States, delivery drones are ceasing to be a novelty and becoming part of everyday life. Ashley Schmidt, a resident of North Garland near Dallas, now spends little time in her garden. She attributes her discomfort to the sound of drones flying overhead with her neighbours' purchases. Whereas steady traffic may merge into the surrounding hum, a drone's sudden whine can cut through it. What Torija Martinez emphasises is that drone sound can be conspicuous even when overall sound levels are relatively low. He explains that communities with little previous exposure to aircraft noise may be especially sensitive to changes in their local soundscape, the combination of sounds around them. Research he cites suggests that reactions are stronger when a sound's source seems unfamiliar, intrusive or frequent, while people who benefit from drone services may be less troubled. He warns that currently localised complaints are likely to become more widespread as the sector expands. London-based Greenjets claims that its propulsion system, which generates the force to move a drone, can reduce drone noise by at least two thirds. Its design encloses spinning blades within a pod to muffle their sound and direct it away from the ground. The system also uses slower rotor speeds and materials that reduce sound caused by vibration. Greenjets' technology has helped conservationists in Africa seeking to monitor poachers from above without revealing their presence. For larger drones used in the Russia–Ukraine war, however, speed generally takes precedence over stealth, according to Tollast. Another propeller design features twisted, loop-shaped blades without pointed tips. Amazon says its latest drone designs use custom propellers that produce almost half the perceived sound of the previous generation, with a milder tone. Wing says that, alongside designing propellers to minimise sound, it varies flight paths to limit repeated overflights of individual homes or neighbourhoods. Schmidt would prefer deliveries in her area to stop until the service no longer causes a disturbance.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. novelty (noun): Something that attracts interest because it is new or unfamiliar, often with the implication that this interest will fade.
2. conspicuous (adjective): Standing out strongly from the surroundings; here, readily attracting attention through sound.
3. soundscape (noun): The combination of sounds that characterises a particular environment.
4. intrusive (adjective): Unwelcome because it enters someone's personal space or disrupts their activities or peace.
5. localised (adjective): Restricted to particular places rather than distributed across a wider area.
6. propulsion system (noun phrase): The equipment that generates the force needed to move a vehicle.
7. muffle (verb): To make a sound less clearly audible by enclosing or covering its source.
8. takes precedence (verb phrase): Receives greater importance than another consideration when priorities are decided.
9. stealth (noun): The ability to operate without attracting attention or being detected.
10. perceived (adjective): Experienced or judged by a person, rather than described solely through physical measurements.

📅 October 22, 2026 - Britain's Missed Opportunity for a Trillion-Pound Wealth Fund
New Treasury calculations reveal that systematically investing historical North Sea oil proceeds could have endowed the UK with a sovereign wealth portfolio worth a trillion pounds today.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

For the first time, Treasury officials have modelled the staggering potential value of a British sovereign wealth portfolio built from historical North Sea proceeds. According to these calculations, had the capital been systematically retained without withdrawals, the nation would possess an endowment worth approximately one trillion pounds today. Instead, critics of 1980s economic policies argue that peak oil receipts were frittered away on pre-election tax cuts rather than preserved. Defenders counter that subsequent administrations also failed to act, pointing out that Norway only began investing its wealth in earnest in 1996. In contrast, Norway’s methodical approach has yielded a formidable war chest of $2.4 trillion from the same North Sea reserves. To address this historical gap, Chancellor John Healey recently announced the creation of a domestic Energy Wealth Fund. However, the new British entity will operate on a markedly different premise, as it intends to finance domestic environmental projects. This contrasts sharply with Norway, which is mandated to invest its vast capital abroad and has generated immense returns from global technology stocks. Analysts caution that this constrained domestic focus may cause the British entity to struggle to match Norway's exceptional financial performance. Furthermore, as North Sea production slows, the anticipated influx of new fossil fuel receipts into the British portfolio is projected to decline steadily. Ultimately, rather than expecting the initial £2 billion allocation to match such exponential growth, officials are left asking what might have been had a similar, far-sighted idea been adopted in decades past.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. staggering (adjective): Deeply shocking or astonishing, often due to an overwhelming size or amount.
2. proceeds (noun): The monetary revenue or profit obtained from a specific event, activity, or enterprise.
3. endowment (noun): A large reserve of capital invested to provide long-term financial support for an institution or nation.
4. frittered away (phrasal verb): Wasted money, time, or resources gradually on trivial or unimportant things.
5. in earnest (phrase): Occurring to a greater extent or more intensely and seriously than before.
6. war chest (noun): A substantial reserve of funds built up to be used for a challenging situation or long-term strategic goal.
7. premise (noun): A fundamental principle or foundational idea upon which an argument, theory, or operation is based.
8. mandated (verb): Officially required or commanded by authority to take a specific action.
9. constrained (adjective): Severely restricted in scope, extent, or activity.
10. influx (noun): The arrival or entry of a large amount of money, resources, or people into a place.

Advanced Grammar: Inverted mixed conditional
In formal English, conditional clauses can omit the word "if" by inverting the auxiliary verb and the subject. Here, "had the capital been systematically retained" acts as a past hypothetical condition (third conditional). It connects to a present hypothetical result ("would possess... today"), creating a mixed conditional sentence.
Example from the text: "According to these calculations, had the capital been systematically retained without withdrawals, the nation would possess an endowment worth approximately one trillion pounds today."
