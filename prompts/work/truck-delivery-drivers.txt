Lesson 01: Confirming the delivery destination
Original fictional case: Shipment 418 names Alder Books at 26 Mill Street, suite 4. The route note says rear annex. The receiver confirms that the annex belongs to suite 6; suite 4 uses the staffed front desk. Dispatch must correct the conflicting note.
Language workshop: Ask which destination a note identifies
Communication goal: Resolve conflicting location details using names and reference numbers.
Language guidance: An address can identify a building without identifying the consignee. Ask a narrow question and repeat the answer.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The label names ___, while the note says ___. / Which entrance receives deliveries for ___? / So ___ refers to ___, not ___.
Vocabulary: consignee: The person or business named to receive a shipment. / delivery window: The agreed period during which a delivery is expected or accepted. / stop sequence: The planned order of delivery locations on a route. / delivery instruction: A shipment-specific note about where or how receipt is arranged.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Receiver: suite 4 uses the staffed front desk; the annex serves suite 6.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: Dispatch asks whether all deliveries use the front desk. Limit the correction to Alder Books.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Shipment 418 names suite 4, but the route note points to the rear annex. Could you confirm which entrance receives Alder Books deliveries? I need the receiving location for this shipment, not just the building address. I'll ask dispatch to correct the note once we have that distinction clear.

Lesson 02: Checking shipment counts
Original fictional case: At Finch Office Supplies, the bill of lading lists twelve cartons. Driver and receiver count eleven. Contents have not been checked; dispatch has no location for the twelfth carton.
Language workshop: State what was actually counted
Communication goal: Report a quantity discrepancy without inventing its cause.
Language guidance: A carton count and a product count answer different questions. Name the unit and evidence.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The document lists ___ cartons; we counted ___. / That confirms ___, but not ___. / Can we note the difference against ___?
Vocabulary: bill of lading: A transport document identifying the shipment, parties, and goods; its legal effect depends on the shipment and applicable rules. / piece count: The number of separately counted packages or handling units, with the unit stated. / short shipment: A delivery containing fewer goods than the agreed or documented quantity. / packing list: An itemized description of the contents packed for an order.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Receiver: confirm eleven cartons but not their contents.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: A colleague says one missing carton means twelve missing products. Ask for the packing detail.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: The bill of lading lists twelve cartons, and we have counted eleven together. That is a one-carton difference, not a confirmed count of missing individual products. Could we record the carton shortage against this shipment reference? Dispatch will investigate the remaining carton; I don't have its location or a replacement date.

Dialogue 08: The evening desk challenges a vague status
Original fictional setting: A driver and evening dispatcher resolve an ambiguous end-of-day route entry.
Professional roles: Dispatcher / Driver
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Dispatcher: Your final stop says done, but a contents query remains open.
Driver: The physical handover is complete. The customer received six cartons, while the order query concerns contents they expected separately.
Dispatcher: Did you verify the contents yourself, or are you passing on the customer's report?
Driver: I'm passing on their report. We confirmed six cartons, but I didn't verify the expected items inside them.
Dispatcher: Then done is too broad for our handoff. We need to distinguish completed delivery from an unresolved order question.
Driver: Agreed. I'll state six cartons received by Omar at five ten, with a separate reported contents query outstanding.
Dispatcher: I can send that query to customer service. What reference should they use to connect it to the receipt?
Driver: Use shipment Q14. The receipt is already available, so this isn't a missing-upload issue like yesterday's stop.
Dispatcher: I have Q14 and its proof of delivery. I'll link the query to that shipment.
Driver: Please don't tell the customer their whole order is complete solely because the delivery receipt is available.
Dispatcher: Understood. I'll take the customer-service follow-up and keep the contents query open until they respond.
Driver: That accurately separates what happened at the door from what customer service still needs to establish.
Vocabulary in this exchange: delivery receipt: A record acknowledging delivery and any recorded qualifications about it. / proof of delivery: A record supporting a delivery event, such as named receipt or other evidence required for that service.
