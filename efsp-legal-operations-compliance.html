04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  2. B. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  3. C. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Urgent does not identify the decision

The lesson's contract intake says urgent but omits agreement type, business owner, and required decision date; this is fictional workflow practice, not legal advice.

  1. Legal operations coordinator: The contract intake says urgent. Could you clarify the agreement type before we identify the appropriate approval path?
  2. Business requester: I haven't included that or the business owner. I can see the request isn't ready for useful triage.
  3. Legal operations coordinator: Thank you. We also need the required decision date and why that timing matters.
  4. Business requester: Could you simply prioritize it first while I gather the missing information?
  5. Legal operations coordinator: We need enough context to assess priority through the actual intake process, rather than infer it from urgent.
  6. Business requester: Understood. Should I choose any familiar template to make the request more complete?
  7. Legal operations coordinator: Don't guess the template. First clarify the agreement and business need so the responsible team can route it.
  8. Business requester: I'll confirm the owner and required decision date, distinguishing a target from an external commitment.
  9. Legal operations coordinator: Good. Mark anything still unknown instead of filling the gaps with assumptions.
  10. Business requester: I'll return the clarified intake so the team can assess the request and its priority accurately.
2. A renewal or a new agreement?

Separate fictional case: a requester labels a vendor document renewal, but the intake does not identify the existing agreement or whether the proposed scope changed.

  1. Contract intake analyst: You labeled this a renewal. Could you identify the existing agreement and whether the proposed services have changed?
  2. Business owner: I haven't linked the existing agreement, and I need to check the scope against the new document.
  3. Contract intake analyst: Then we shouldn't route it solely on the assumption that it repeats the prior arrangement.
  4. Business owner: Would the same vendor name be enough to select the previous approval path?
  5. Contract intake analyst: Not by itself. The document type and changed scope may affect the appropriate triage questions.
  6. Business owner: I'll provide the existing agreement reference and identify the differences I can confirm.
  7. Contract intake analyst: Thank you. Please distinguish confirmed changes from points you want the reviewer to interpret.
  8. Business owner: Understood. I won't describe the new terms as approved just because we have used the vendor before.
  9. Contract intake analyst: Good. Once the context is clear, the responsible team can determine the applicable workflow.
  10. Business owner: I'll complete those details and keep the renewal label provisional until the document relationship is understood.
3. A signature deadline or a requested review date?

Separate fictional case: an intake form lists Friday without explaining whether it is a signature deadline, a meeting date, or a preferred review date.

  1. Legal intake specialist: The date field says Friday. Could you clarify what needs to happen by then?
  2. Commercial project lead: I meant our preferred review date, but the form doesn't explain that. It is not a confirmed signature deadline.
  3. Legal intake specialist: That distinction matters for triage. We should record the requested decision and timing accurately.
  4. Commercial project lead: Could you guarantee completion on Friday now that the date is only our preference?
  5. Legal intake specialist: No. The responsible team still needs to assess the request, scope, and capacity before confirming timing.
  6. Commercial project lead: I'll explain the meeting purpose and identify which business question we need help resolving.
  7. Legal intake specialist: Please also confirm the business owner and intended document version so the approval path isn't based on assumptions.
  8. Commercial project lead: Understood. I can supply those details without presenting the review request as a request to sign.
  9. Legal intake specialist: Good. We'll preserve Friday as your requested review date and await the team's actual response.
  10. Commercial project lead: I'll communicate that internally so nobody treats an intake date as a confirmed legal commitment.

Scenario 2 · An attachment with no counterparty identified

A fictional contract request contains a blank template and the label urgent, but no counterparty name or business owner. No external deadline is supplied. Hold an intake clarification conversation rather than choosing an approval route by guesswork.

Role A: You are the intake coordinator. Ask for agreement purpose, counterparty, business owner, and the meaning of urgent.

Role B: You are the project requester. Identify the gaps and explain whom you must consult.

Possible opening: “Could you clarify the agreement's purpose and counterparty before we determine how to route this template?”

Success checks
  • Clarify required identities and purpose specifically.
  • Do not invent an owner, deadline, or approval path.
  • Agree who will supply the missing intake context.
Add a complication

The requester wants to reuse another team's owner name because it makes the form look complete.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

Two short language checks

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  3. C. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  2. B. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  3. C. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.

06Third-Party Risk and Due DiligenceExplain a fair comparison · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Two vendors offer similar services. One handles more personal data and relies on additional subcontractors.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

third-party risk
Risk arising from relationships with outside providers or partners.
due diligence
Structured review of facts, risks, obligations, and evidence before a transaction, onboarding, investment, or decision.
data processing
Operations performed on data, such as collection, storage, transformation, or analysis.
vendor questionnaire
A structured request for information used to evaluate a supplier's capabilities or risks.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
  2. B. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  3. C. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.
  2. B. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  3. C. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
  2. B. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
  3. C. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.
  2. B. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.
  3. C. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.

08Governance, Reporting, and Risk CommitteesGive a useful status update · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Distinguish completed work from pending work and explain its impact.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A risk committee tracker shows an action as complete because its plan was approved. Implementation evidence is still outstanding.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

governance
Decision structure defining roles, rights, approvals, controls, and accountability.
risk committee
A group responsible for reviewing defined risk matters and related decisions.
risk acceptance
An authorized decision to retain a stated risk under specified conditions.
remediation
Work to correct a problem or reduce its effects; in environmental work, addressing contamination.

03 · Notice the language

Give a useful status update

Lead with the current state, then the important change or open item. Use completed-action language only for completed work. Add a next update point when it is known; do not invent a date to make the message sound complete.

  • We have completed ...; ... remains open.
  • As of ..., the status is ... .
  • The next update will cover ... .

Improve this: Everything is progressing well.

See a clearer version

Three checks are complete; two remain open and are being reviewed.

The revision gives observable progress. A positive label alone does not tell the reader what is complete or still needed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence says a review is finished?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The team has completed the review.

  1. A. The team plans to complete the review. 'Plans to complete' describes an intention, not completion.
  2. B. The team has completed the review. 'Has completed' states that the action is finished.
  3. C. The team is completing the review. 'Is completing' presents the work as in progress.
2. Which update distinguishes a plan from a confirmed fact?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it.

  1. A. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it. This explicitly labels the date as planned and identifies the missing confirmation.
  2. B. Delivery is confirmed for Friday because we planned it. A plan does not establish carrier confirmation.
  3. C. The delivery plan proves that arrival will be Friday. A plan cannot prove a future arrival time.
