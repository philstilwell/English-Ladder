TASK: Retrieve occupational vocabulary
1. Select four terms from the reference. Explain each in plain English, give two natural collocations and quote or create a short workplace example. Label invented examples. Contrast each term with a plausible confusable term; acknowledge regional variation.
2. Present one role-labelled exchange with one missing word or phrase and four choices A-D. Exactly one choice must fit the stated facts and grammar. Stop and wait for my answer before revealing it.
3. Explain all four options, then invite me to read the completed exchange aloud. Offer a sentence frame for optional spoken practice, never an open-ended writing assignment.
4. Repeat for the other three terms, one question at a time. Vary the correct letter and the situation; preserve the reference facts. If a choice is ambiguous, replace the item.
5. Finish with two new four-choice collocation questions for later review, with a clearly separate answer key. Do not claim to schedule a reminder.

Lesson 01: Client Discovery and Problem Definition
Original fictional case: Client sponsor Rita asks consultant Owen for a benchmark of support-center costs. During discovery, she explains that the executive committee must decide by 30 November whether to centralize the North and South support centers or retain them separately. The comparison must address total operating cost, response time, and service continuity, including transition effects. Staffing and customer-impact data are not yet available. Centralization has not been approved. Owen must define the decision and evidence needs before treating a peer cost comparison as a recommendation to close either site.
Language workshop: The requested study is not the decision
Communication goal: Turn a request for benchmarking into an agreed decision question with boundaries, criteria, and an owner.
Language guidance: A benchmark compares selected measures against a reference group. It does not itself decide the operating model. Different service hours, case complexity, or cost definitions can make apparently similar figures misleading unless the comparison is adjusted or qualified.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): What decision should this benchmark help you make? / The choice is centralization or continued separate operation. / The executive committee owns that decision. / The recommendation needs to support the thirtieth-of-November decision. / Centralization has not been approved. / We need to compare cost, response time, and service continuity. / Show transition costs separately from steady-state costs. / Are the peer figures based on comparable services and definitions? / We do not yet have the staffing and customer-impact data. / A lower peer cost would not by itself justify closing a site. / This phase evaluates options; it does not authorize implementation. / Which service requirements must remain intact during transition? / The benchmark is one input to the operating-model decision. / We will produce a decision-focused option comparison. / Is that the choice you need the analysis to support? / Let us record the question, criteria, owner, deadline, and evidence needs.
Vocabulary: discovery: Early inquiry to understand the client situation and decision. / problem statement: A precise description of the question or issue to address. / decision question: The choice that the analysis is intended to support. / client sponsor: The client representative supporting and directing the engagement within their role. / decision owner: The person or body authorized to make the relevant choice. / benchmark: A reference used for a defined comparison. / peer group: Organizations or units selected for comparison. / comparability: The degree to which a comparison uses sufficiently similar definitions and conditions. / operating model: How an organization arranges people, processes, systems, and responsibilities. / centralization: Concentrating specified activities or authority in a common location or structure. / decentralization: Distributing specified activities or authority across units. / baseline: The defined starting position used for comparison. / decision criterion: A factor used to evaluate alternatives. / constraint: A limit that an option must respect or explicitly address. / service continuity: Maintaining the required service through an operating change. / transition cost: A cost associated with moving from one arrangement to another. / steady-state cost: Cost after a defined transition has settled. / response time: Elapsed time to provide a specified response. / stakeholder map: A representation of relevant parties and their interests or roles. / in scope: Included in the agreed work boundaries. / out-of-scope: Excluded from the agreed work boundaries. / success measure: A defined indicator of whether the intended result has been achieved. / engagement charter: A document summarizing purpose, boundaries, roles, and arrangements. / decision deadline: The date or time by which a choice is needed.
Speaking task: Read Rita and Owen aloud, then swap roles. Stress the actual choice and the three comparison criteria. Use the answer key to correct all ten gaps. Reread the peer-comparison and transition-cost exchanges without dropping their qualifications. Deliver Owen's final engagement summary again, retaining the decision owner, deadline, evidence gaps, and boundary between analysis and implementation.
Partner: Rita: Client sponsor / Owen: Engagement lead
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: A sponsor requests a vendor-price comparison. The actual choice is whether to renew the current provider or run a new competition. The procurement committee decides by 15 January, using total cost, service performance, and transition risk.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Rita: The committee will make the choice. I can sponsor the engagement and coordinate the inputs, but I cannot approve a closure myself.
Owen: That clarifies the decision owner. We can direct the recommendation to the committee and ask you to confirm the factual inputs and engagement arrangements within your role.
Rita: Please summarize the question, boundaries, criteria, and dates so that everyone starts with the same understanding.
Owen: I will put them in the engagement charter. Then the benchmark serves the actual choice, and the team has a clear basis for deciding what evidence to collect first.
END REFERENCE

Give up to three focused suggestions. Ask me to retry the weakest turn; wait. Only then offer an alternative wording and a harder replay.
Use voice replies when available. If I prefer selections, offer four plausible spoken replies and explain my choice before continuing. Do not require written compositions.

Twenty departing customers volunteered for interviews: 12 mentioned price, 9 mentioned service delays, and 5 mentioned competitor features. Each person could mention more than one issue. Consultant Ada wants to lead with price as the cause of churn; manager Karim asks for a testable hypothesis and a structured inquiry into the alternatives. The interview sample is not established as representative. Timing, customer segments, actual prices paid, and comparable nondeparting accounts have not yet been analyzed. No causal driver has been isolated.
Language workshop: A frequent complaint is not yet the cause of churn
Communication goal: Use a working hypothesis to organize analysis while checking alternative explanations, overlapping categories, and evidence that could contradict it.
Language guidance: Twelve of twenty interviewees mentioned price: 60% of that interview group. It is not evidence that price caused 60% of all sixty departures. The price, service, and competitor counts overlap because respondents could give more than one answer.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Price is a working hypothesis, not an established churn driver. / We interviewed twenty of the sixty departing accounts. / Twelve of those twenty interviewees mentioned price. / Respondents could name more than one issue. / The categories are not mutually exclusive. / How would the proposed price effect lead to departure? / We also need to examine service experience and competitor changes. / What evidence would weaken the price hypothesis? / Did the price change precede the departure? / How do otherwise comparable nondeparting accounts differ? / Separate the patterns by relevant customer group. / Use prices actually paid, not only the published list price. / A frequent complaint does not by itself isolate a cause. / Start with questions that could change the client decision. / The new evidence may require us to change the initial explanation. / Assign a test, evidence source, and owner to each priority question.
Vocabulary: hypothesis: A proposed explanation that can be examined against evidence. / issue tree: A structured breakdown of a question into related subquestions. / hypothesis tree: A breakdown of a proposed explanation into testable supporting claims. / workstream: A coordinated part of the engagement's work. / churn: Customer or account loss, defined over a specified period and population. / churn driver: A factor contributing to customer departure. / exit interview: A conversation with someone leaving a service or organization. / multi-response question: A question allowing more than one answer category. / mention count: The number of recorded references to a specified topic. / sample: The observations included in an analysis. / sampling bias: Distortion associated with how observations enter a sample. / MECE: Mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive; a structuring aim of avoiding overlap and missing categories. / overlap: Inclusion of the same observation in more than one category. / double counting: Counting the same item more than intended in a total. / alternative explanation: A different possible account of an observed result. / disconfirming evidence: Information that weakens or contradicts a hypothesis. / confirmation bias: Favoring information that supports an existing belief. / causal mechanism: The process through which one factor could produce an effect. / segmentation: Dividing a population into defined groups for analysis. / cohort: A group sharing a defined starting characteristic or period. / price sensitivity: Responsiveness to price changes under specified conditions. / confounder: Another factor that complicates a proposed causal interpretation. / triangulation: Comparing different evidence sources or methods. / analytical priority: A question selected for attention based on decision relevance and evidence needs.

Lesson 03: Data Requests and Client Burden
Original fictional case: Consultant Mateo sends client lead Leah a 40-item request due Friday. The first analysis actually needs six monthly measures for January through June at the North and South centers: completed cases, paid staff hours, labor cost, vendor cost, reopened cases, and opening backlog. That analysis is needed for a Wednesday workshop. Existing aggregate reports may be sufficient, but definitions and period coverage must be checked. The remaining 34 items have not been prioritized. Leah cannot promise the full request. Mateo must agree owners, feasible dates, and approved transfer arrangements rather than demand raw personal records by default.
Language workshop: Forty requests, but only six are needed first
Communication goal: Prioritize a client data request, define the required measures, and agree a feasible delivery sequence without collecting unnecessary personal information.
Language guidance: A smaller first request is not a waiver of definitions or quality. Confirm what completed, reopened, and opening mean in the existing reports, along with units, cost scope, and monthly coverage. A timely file with misunderstood fields can produce a misleading comparison.
