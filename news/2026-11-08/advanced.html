📅 November 08, 2026 - Leonid Meteor Shower to Peak on 17 November

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

The Leonid meteor shower is set to begin this weekend and continue through the end of November. The shower is expected to reach its peak on the night of 17 November. The report estimates that observers might see as many as 10 to 15 meteors per hour. The shower originates in dust that Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle leaves in its wake as it orbits the Sun. Those particles can enter Earth’s atmosphere at up to 71 kilometres per second, a speed the report says makes the Leonids the fastest meteors in the sky. The Leonids’ radiant, the point from which they appear to emerge, lies near the head of the constellation Leo. That part of the sky offers a point of reference, although the meteors can appear anywhere in the sky. Experts advise seeking a safe, dark site well removed from sources of light pollution such as street lighting. The meteors can be seen without optical aids such as binoculars or a telescope. Allowing 20 to 30 minutes for your eyes to acclimatise to the dark can improve your chances of seeing the meteors. Dry though the weather is expected to be much of the time, extensive cloud is forecast in many areas. For Friday and Saturday nights, the forecast favours southern and western parts of England and Wales, where clear skies look more likely. More of the UK is likely to see clear skies by Sunday night. Fog could still form where skies stay clear for some time, obscuring the view. The report anticipates favourable viewing conditions around the peak, when the Moon will be below the horizon around midnight and its reflected light is less likely to interfere.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. as many as (quantifier phrase): Emphasises a high number; with “might see”, it highlights the upper end of an estimate.
2. originates in (verb phrase): Has its source in the material, place or process named next.
3. in its wake (prepositional phrase): Behind something as it moves away, along the path it has taken.
4. radiant (noun): The point in the sky from which the meteors in a shower appear to spread out.
5. point of reference (noun phrase): A known position that helps you work out where to look or locate something else.
6. optical aids (plural noun phrase): Instruments such as binoculars or telescopes that help people see objects that are difficult to make out unaided.
7. acclimatise to (verb phrase): Gradually become able to function under different conditions, here as the eyes become more sensitive to low light.
8. favours (verb): Indicates that one place or option has better prospects than others.
9. obscuring (verb, present participle): Making something harder or impossible to see by coming between it and the observer.
10. interfere (verb): Get in the way of an activity, as moonlight can do by making the meteors harder to see.

Advanced Grammar: Adjective fronting in concessive clauses with “though”
Put an adjective before “though” to foreground a point you acknowledge, then use the main clause to challenge what readers might expect from it. Putting “Dry” first acknowledges the dry forecast before drawing attention to extensive cloud.
Example from the text: "Dry though the weather is expected to be much of the time, extensive cloud is forecast in many areas."
