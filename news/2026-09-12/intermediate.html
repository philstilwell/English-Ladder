Museum Thefts Across Europe Become Bolder and More Frequent

Intermediate · B1–B2

News archive →
📅 September 12, 2026 - Museum Thefts Across Europe Become Bolder and More Frequent

Read the story

Art crime investigators and security experts say burglaries from galleries across Europe are becoming more brazen. When thieves broke into a French museum earlier this week and stole four paintings by Pierre-Auguste Renoir, not everyone was surprised. The European policing agency (Europol) had warned that museum thefts across Europe were growing increasingly brazen just last month. Internationally known art detective Arthur Brand predicted that more high-profile burglaries were likely after a successful robbery at the Louvre in Paris. The audacious crime at the Louvre stunned France, and the stolen jewellery worth €88m has still not been recovered. Museums are vulnerable and easy to burgle, Brand says, compared with well-secured jewellery shops and banks. Directors historically "never have enough money for security" or prefer to spend it on the next exhibition. Many of these burglaries are over in minutes and the thieves have long gone by the time police show up. Now precious metals, gemstones, and culturally significant artefacts are increasingly targeted, according to Europol's report. Precious metals can be easily melted down, leaving no trace for law enforcement, and facilitating illicit sales during the fencing process. Christopher A Marinello, the boss of Art Recovery International, says only 5-10% of stolen art is ever recovered. Experts say the trick is to create physical barriers that will delay thieves so there is time for the police to arrive or deter would-be offenders. However, security measures are expensive, and finding the money is a main challenge for smaller cities with important collections. A museum cannot be a fortress, but institutions are facing new challenges that will require new security standards.

Words and grammar

1. brazen (adjective): done in a very open and shocking way, without caring if people are shocked.
2. burglaries (noun): crimes where someone illegally enters a building to steal things.
3. audacious (adjective): showing a willingness to take surprisingly bold risks, often in a shocking or rude way.
4. stunned (verb, past participle): extremely surprised or shocked by something.
5. recovered (verb, past participle): found again after being lost or stolen.
6. vulnerable (adjective): easily hurt, attacked, or damaged.
7. targeted (verb, past participle): chosen or aimed at for a particular action.
8. illicit (adjective): not allowed by laws or rules; illegal.
9. deter (verb): to make someone decide not to do something, especially by making them feel afraid or doubtful.
10. fortress (noun): a strong building or place that is protected against attack.

Grammar Focus: The Passive Voice (Present Perfect)
The passive voice helps us describe actions where the focus is on the action itself or the receiver of the action, rather than who performed it. It's often used in news to report events without needing to name the specific person or group responsible. The present perfect passive, formed with 'has/have been + past participle,' describes an action that started in the past and continues or has an effect in the present, where the agent is unknown or less important.
Example from the text: "The audacious crime at the Louvre stunned France, and the stolen jewellery worth €88m has still not been recovered."
