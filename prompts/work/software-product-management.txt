Lesson 01: Product Discovery and Problem Framing
Original fictional case: Support lead Morgan asks product manager Li for a new Export button on the home page. Their review finds 12 relevant tickets from the past month: eight users could not find the existing action, while four found it but lacked the required permission. The records do not show how common either problem is among all users. No design change is approved. Morgan and Li agree to separate the two issues and prepare a two-day investigation of navigation and permission messaging before choosing a solution.
Language workshop: One button request, two different problems
Communication goal: Reframe a requested interface addition using supplied support evidence and agree a focused discovery task.
Language guidance: Twelve tickets are support evidence, not a census of all users. Eight describe finding the action, and four describe permission. A new button might help the first group but would not itself give the second group authorization to export.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): We should address the export friction in these tickets. / The button is one proposed solution, not the problem itself. / What were users trying to export, and from where? / Eight tickets concern finding the action; four concern permission. / The existing action may have a discoverability problem. / The permission message may need a separate review. / Adding a button would not grant authorization. / These tickets do not establish prevalence across all users. / Can users find the action during the relevant task? / We are not proposing to bypass access controls. / Let us spend two days examining these two paths. / Link each ticket to the task and navigation path. / A clearer label and a new location are different options. / No design change is approved yet. / Bring the observed paths and findings to the next review. / Choose the solution after separating the two problems.
Vocabulary: product discovery: Work to understand problems and test potential product directions. / problem statement: A concise description of a user problem and its context. / solution request: A request for a particular implementation or feature. / user need: An outcome a user needs to accomplish. / job to be done: The progress or task a user seeks in a given situation. / user segment: A group of users sharing relevant characteristics or needs. / support evidence: Information from support interactions used to investigate problems. / discoverability: How readily users can find an available function. / permission: Authorization to perform a defined action. / access barrier: A condition preventing a user from reaching or using a capability. / navigation path: The sequence of locations used to reach a function. / friction point: A step that makes a user task harder or less reliable. / usability test: Observation of representative users attempting defined tasks. / task success: Completion of a defined user task under stated conditions. / research question: A specific uncertainty the investigation aims to resolve. / assumption: An unverified premise guiding a proposal or decision. / hypothesis: A proposed explanation or prediction to evaluate. / prototype: A representation used to explore or test a design. / desirability: Whether a solution addresses something users value. / feasibility: Whether a solution can be built and operated under constraints. / viability: Whether a solution can support the relevant business needs. / opportunity: A problem or possibility that could create product value. / discovery scope: The defined boundaries of the investigation. / evidence gap: Missing information needed to support a conclusion.
Speaking task: Read turns 1-10 in pairs. Stress finding and permission as different barriers. Switch roles for turns 11-20. Pause after the two-day limit and the no-approval statement. Check the Download exchange, then read it with six and four clearly distinguished.
Partner: Morgan: Support lead / Li: Product manager
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: Ten tickets are reviewed. Six users could not find Download. Four found it but lacked permission. No design change is approved, and the records do not represent all users.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Morgan: Once we have the observations, we can decide whether to test a revised label, a new location, or another design. We do not have to build every option.
Li: A prototype may help compare the relevant direction before implementation. The choice should follow what we learn, not merely the most visible suggestion in the initial request.
Morgan: I will prepare the ticket groups and coordinate the review. Our status will say that we have agreed the investigation, not approved a new home-page feature.
Li: Good. Record the problem statement with both barriers and the remaining uncertainty. That gives Support a clear next step and gives the product team a useful basis for choosing a solution.

Lesson 02: Roadmaps and Prioritization
Original fictional case: Product owner Nora and commercial sponsor Samir review three features. A improves setup recovery and directly supports the agreed activation goal. B provides audit export needed for a recorded October 31 customer commitment. C refreshes the home page; no fixed commitment or activation evidence is supplied for it. Each feature needs one of two available delivery slots, and splitting them is not feasible this cycle. The agreed rule is to protect fixed commitments first, then the activation goal. Nora proposes B and A, with C reviewed again in two weeks.
Language workshop: Three urgent requests, two delivery slots
