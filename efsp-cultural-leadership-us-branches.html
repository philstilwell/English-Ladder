01Check meaning before assuming agreementAsk a precise question · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Clarify missing information before responding.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

In a branch meeting, two colleagues say the launch plan is "fine." One later emails a concern about staffing. The manager had recorded agreement.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

assumption
Something treated as true for planning or reasoning, though it may need verification.
clarification
A question or explanation that makes meaning more precise.
expectation
A stated or assumed understanding of what should happen or be delivered.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

Improve this: Could you explain what does this mean?

See a clearer version

Could you explain what this means?

After an introductory phrase such as 'Could you explain', the embedded question uses statement word order: what this means.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: the review starts.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

05 · Say it

Practice with a partner

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You need to know exactly what information is missing. Give one answer only after your partner asks a focused question; then ask them to confirm their understanding.

Try a harder second round

The other person uses an ambiguous word such as 'ready', 'approved', or 'urgent'. Clarify it before continuing.

Studying alone? Give both sides of the conversation aloud. Pause before answering the follow-up, then repeat with fewer notes.

02Challenge ideas respectfullyDisagree with a useful reason · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

State a concern respectfully and propose a practical route forward.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A colleague says, "This proposal makes no sense." The group goes quiet. You want to hear the concern without accepting personal attacks.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

objection
A stated concern or reason for not accepting a proposal.
behavioral example
A specific description of what a person did or said.
boundary
A limit on acceptable conduct, responsibility, access, or scope.

03 · Notice the language

Disagree with a useful reason

Acknowledge the goal without pretending agreement. Name the specific problem and its implication, then ask a focused question or propose a next step. Be direct when a safety or ethical boundary requires it.

  • I understand the goal. My concern is ... .
  • That statement goes beyond ... .
  • Could we ... before confirming ...?

Improve this: That idea is ridiculous.

See a clearer version

My concern is that the proposal assumes capacity we have not confirmed.

The revised sentence identifies a testable concern about the proposal. It leaves room for a constructive response.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which response challenges an assumption without attacking a person?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open.

  1. A. Only an irresponsible person would suggest this. This attacks a person rather than examining the proposal.
  2. B. Your team never thinks about anything properly. This makes a broad personal judgment without a specific example.
  3. C. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open. It connects the concern to a specific assumption and record.
2. Which follow-up makes disagreement actionable?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan?

  1. A. I will say yes even though the approval is still missing. This hides the concern and risks misrepresenting approval.
  2. B. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan? It names the unresolved condition and a practical next step.
  3. C. I disagree, and there is nothing more to discuss. This offers no route to resolve the concern.

Partner's role and follow-up

You want to move quickly and initially overlook a relevant condition. Ask why it matters, then consider a practical route forward that respects the real constraint.

Try a harder second round

The other person repeats the request more firmly. Restate the specific concern calmly and explain the appropriate next route.

03Make authority and participation clearHelp a group reach a clear next step · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Include relevant voices and clarify the decision process.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Your team wants more direction, but the manager has been answering every question. A new hire has stopped offering ideas.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

decision rights
The allocation of authority to recommend, approve, or make particular decisions.
role clarity
A shared understanding of a person's responsibilities and authority.
facilitation
Guiding a discussion so people can contribute, understand differences, and reach useful next steps.

03 · Notice the language

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Improve this: Nobody objected, so everyone agrees.

See a clearer version

Before I record agreement, does anyone have a concern or need more time?

The revised question gives people a way to raise a concern. Silence can have several meanings, especially in unfamiliar or unequal-power situations.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
  2. B. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
  3. C. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.
  2. B. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.
  3. C. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.

04Negotiate realistic commitmentsOffer a workable tradeoff · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Negotiate scope or timing while making conditions explicit.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Headquarters asks for a report on Friday. The local team can produce verified figures on Monday or a clearly labeled estimate on Friday.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

commitment
An explicit promise or accepted responsibility to do something.
tradeoff
A choice that improves one objective while giving up something on another.
deadline
The latest agreed or required time for completing an action.

03 · Notice the language

Offer a workable tradeoff

Acknowledge the goal and explain the available choices. Connect an offer to its actual conditions. A useful response makes room for discussion without promising approval or resources you do not control.

  • We can ..., provided that ... .
  • Would you prefer ... or ...?
  • I can take that proposal for review; I cannot yet confirm ... .

Improve this: If you will approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

See a clearer version

If you approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

For an ordinary future condition, the if-clause uses present tense. The other clause can express the future consequence or possibility.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence presents a normal future condition clearly?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule.

  1. A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule. The if-clause uses present tense; the main clause states the future response.
  2. B. If the scope will change, we review yesterday's schedule. The tense pattern and time reference do not express the intended future condition.
  3. C. If the scope changed tomorrow, we reviewed the schedule yesterday. The conflicting time references make the condition unclear.
2. Which response negotiates a new request without silently promising extra work?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

  1. A. We have included it, although its scope has not been reviewed. It treats unreviewed work as already included.
  2. B. We guarantee the original date without assessing the addition. It promises an outcome without examining the consequences.
  3. C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope. This offers a useful next step while keeping the approval and scope question open.

05 · Say it

Practice with a partner

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You want the preferred outcome but can accept one tradeoff. Ask for two options, then state which condition you can accept and which still needs approval.

Try a harder second round

The preferred option is unavailable. Offer an alternative and clearly state any approval still needed.

Studying alone? Give both sides of the conversation aloud. Pause before answering the follow-up, then repeat with fewer notes.

06Give specific, useful feedbackGive feedback people can use · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Describe observed behavior, its effect, and a useful next step.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

feedback
Information about performance intended to support understanding and improvement.
expectation
A stated or assumed understanding of what should happen or be delivered.
support plan
Agreed actions and resources intended to help a person or team meet an identified need.

03 · Notice the language

Give feedback people can use

Use a specific example instead of a personality label. Explain its effect, invite the person's perspective, and agree on a practical improvement. Focus on communication that can be changed, not accent or national identity.

  • In [specific situation], I noticed ... .
  • The effect was ... . What was happening from your perspective?
  • Could we agree to ... and review it ...?

Improve this: You are always careless.

See a clearer version

The last two reports omitted the agreed summary section.

The revision identifies observable work. 'Always' and a personality judgment make the feedback harder to verify or act on.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which feedback begins with observable behavior?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The last two updates omitted the agreed completion date.

  1. A. You are the kind of person who ignores details. This labels a person rather than an action.
  2. B. The last two updates omitted the agreed completion date. The recipient can check the specific examples and discuss how to improve them.
  3. C. You obviously do not care about our work. This assumes motivation rather than describing behavior.
2. Which follow-up invites useful information before agreeing on support?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. What is making this part of the task difficult?

  1. A. What is making this part of the task difficult? An open question may reveal a barrier the speaker has not considered.
  2. B. There cannot be any reasonable explanation. This closes off relevant information.
  3. C. You should already know what I am thinking. This relies on unstated expectations.

Your goal

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

operating agreement
An agreed description of how people will coordinate work and responsibilities.
workload
The amount and type of work assigned or required over a period.
stakeholder engagement
The involvement and interaction of people affected by an activity or decision.

03 · Notice the language

Make a complex point clear

Start with the reader's question. Explain one unfamiliar term with familiar words, then give a relevant example or contrast. Check understanding without asking only 'Do you understand?'.

  • In this context, ... means ... .
  • The difference is that ... .
  • How would you explain that distinction to a colleague?

Improve this: We need to operationalize the implementation.

See a clearer version

We need to put the plan into practice.

The revised sentence names the action in familiar words. Specialist vocabulary is useful when it adds precision, but repeating abstract nouns can hide the meaning.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
  2. B. The backlog is an important operational backlog. Repeating the same term does not define it.
  3. C. The backlog must be reviewed at the backlog meeting. This describes a meeting but leaves the term unexplained.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. I have explained the next step, so shall we finish? Finishing the conversation gives no evidence of understanding.
  2. B. You understand everything now, don't you? This leading question makes it easy to agree without showing understanding.
  3. C. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.
  • I'm sorry for ... . The correct information is ... .
  • Let me check that the revised explanation addresses ... .

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

See a clearer version

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry you did not read it correctly. This shifts blame to the reader.
  2. B. I'm sorry if you chose to misunderstand it. This attributes an unverified motive to the listener.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.

05 · Say it

Practice with a partner

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You are frustrated because an earlier message created an expectation. Explain that expectation. Ask your partner to distinguish the correction from anything still uncertain.

Try a harder second round

The listener remains disappointed after the correction. Acknowledge the impact and restate the available next step calmly.

Studying alone? Give both sides of the conversation aloud. Pause before answering the follow-up, then repeat with fewer notes.

06 · Write it

A message someone can act on

Write a 70-110 word message for the person who needs to act on this case. State the purpose, preserve the relevant facts, and make the next action or unresolved question clear. Use a subject line and an appropriate opening. Do not invent a deadline, finding, or approval.

Compare with a model response
I interrupted you and missed part of your concern. I'm sorry. Could we return to it now? At our next meeting, I'll let you finish before I respond.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. For your written version, add a subject line, opening, and relevant context without inventing facts.

Notice: Acknowledge a communication problem and give a corrected message. Underline the wording that does this. Then identify one detail from the case that the response preserves.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

Put it together

Your final workplace challenge

Choose a case you have not rehearsed today. Give a one-minute response, answer two follow-up questions, then write a 70-110 word message. Have your partner introduce the harder second-round challenge. Review the four criteria used in the lessons and repeat the part that needs improvement.
