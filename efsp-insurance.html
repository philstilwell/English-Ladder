01Underwriting and Risk SelectionAsk a precise question · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Clarify missing information before responding.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An underwriting submission describes a business activity broadly but omits its operating locations and loss history.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

underwriting
Evaluation of risk and terms used to decide whether and on what conditions to issue insurance, extend credit, or accept an investment exposure.
risk appetite
The amount and types of risk an organization is prepared to pursue or retain.
exposure
The extent to which people, assets, or operations are subject to a hazard or potential loss.
loss history
A record of prior claims or losses for an insured risk.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Which operations and locations are included?

Fictional underwriting intake: a submission says business services but omits operating locations and loss history. No coverage or acceptance decision is made.

  1. Underwriting assistant: The submission says business services. Could you clarify the activities and operating locations included?
  2. Broker account manager: The description is broad, and I haven't obtained the location schedule or loss history yet.
  3. Underwriting assistant: Those gaps affect the exposure information available for review. Who can provide the verified details?
  4. Broker account manager: I'll ask the client's authorized contact rather than assume all work happens at the registered address.
  5. Underwriting assistant: Thank you. Please distinguish current operations from any planned activities when obtaining the description.
  6. Broker account manager: Does requesting those details mean the risk already fits your underwriting appetite?
  7. Underwriting assistant: No. An information request isn't acceptance, a quote, or a coverage decision.
  8. Broker account manager: I'll keep that clear with the client and ask what loss-history records are available.
  9. Underwriting assistant: If any period is missing, identify it explicitly instead of describing the history as claim-free.
  10. Broker account manager: I'll return the verified activity and location details with any remaining gaps clearly marked for review.
2. Storage or manufacturing?

Separate fictional submission: a facility is described as a warehouse, but an attachment mentions assembly work; the extent of that activity is unspecified.

  1. Commercial underwriter: The form says warehouse, while the attachment mentions assembly. What activity actually occurs at the facility?
  2. Broker placement specialist: I need to clarify that with the client. The attachment doesn't describe the assembly process.
  3. Commercial underwriter: Please ask whether it's a current operation and which location the description concerns.
  4. Broker placement specialist: Would calling it minor assembly be enough to keep the exposure summary brief?
  5. Commercial underwriter: Not unless the client supplies facts supporting that description. Minor is too vague by itself.
  6. Broker placement specialist: I'll request a factual activity description and avoid choosing a label based on expected risk appetite.
  7. Commercial underwriter: Good. We need accurate information before considering how the submission fits our review criteria.
  8. Broker placement specialist: Should I tell the client that clarifying the activity guarantees a more favorable underwriting outcome?
  9. Commercial underwriter: No outcome is guaranteed. The purpose is a complete, accurate submission for assessment.
  10. Broker placement specialist: I'll explain that distinction and return with confirmed operations rather than an unsupported risk characterization.
3. A blank loss field is not a clean history

Separate fictional renewal submission: the loss-history field is blank; the broker has not established whether records are unavailable or no losses were reported.

  1. Underwriting coordinator: The loss-history field is blank. Does that mean no reported losses, or information not yet supplied?
  2. Broker service lead: We haven't established which. The client returned the form without completing that section.
  3. Underwriting coordinator: Please clarify the status and the period covered before we use it in the underwriting review.
  4. Broker service lead: Could I enter none temporarily so the application moves through the intake screen?
  5. Underwriting coordinator: No. That would turn missing information into a factual claim about the client's loss history.
  6. Broker service lead: I'll request the relevant records and identify any period the client cannot currently document.
  7. Underwriting coordinator: Thank you. Also avoid assuming a blank field establishes low exposure or automatic eligibility.
  8. Broker service lead: Understood. Is the immediate goal clarification, rather than asking the client to predict our decision?
  9. Underwriting coordinator: Exactly. We need evidence and an accurate status, not assurances about the eventual underwriting outcome.
  10. Broker service lead: I'll return a verified answer or explicitly state what remains missing for the review.

Scenario 2 · Is the third site operating or planned?

A fictional submission lists three operating sites, but a separate note calls one site planned for next year. Its current operating status is unverified. Clarify the location schedule before underwriting review.

Role A: Underwriting analyst: ask whether the third site is current or proposed.

Role B: Broker coordinator: agree to obtain the client's verified status without removing the site on assumption.

Possible opening: “Could you clarify whether the third location is operating now or is only planned?”

Success checks
  • Distinguish current operations from plans.
  • Preserve the unresolved site information.
  • Avoid promising an underwriting outcome.
Add a complication

The broker asks whether excluding it would guarantee acceptance.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

  2. B. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.

Answer and explanations

  2. B. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.
  3. C. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A renewal quote has a lower premium but a higher deductible. The client asks whether it is the same protection for less money.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

renewal
Continuation of a policy, contract, or subscription for a further period.
premium
The price charged for insurance coverage over a stated period.
deductible
The amount a policyholder bears before the insurer pays covered amounts as the policy specifies.
reinsurance
Insurance purchased by an insurer to transfer an agreed part of its risk.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  2. B. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
  3. C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  2. B. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.
  3. C. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An unusual claim pattern prompts concern. A note calls the customer fraudulent before an investigation has established the facts.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

fraud indicator
An observation that may warrant investigation but does not by itself prove fraud.
SIU
Special investigation unit: an insurance team examining claims or concerns that need specialist investigation.
material misrepresentation
A false or misleading statement significant to a decision under applicable law or contract terms.
investigation
A systematic effort to establish facts and understand an event or concern.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  2. B. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  3. C. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  2. B. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  3. C. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.

1. More claims and costlier claims

Fictional actuarial explanation: comparable exposure periods show both increased claim frequency and increased average claim severity; no pricing decision is supplied.

  1. Portfolio manager: Claim costs increased. Can you explain how both frequency and severity contributed in this report?
  2. Actuarial analyst: Frequency concerns how often claims occur relative to the exposure basis; severity concerns average cost per claim.
  3. Portfolio manager: So more claims and more expensive claims are different drivers, even when total costs rise together.
  4. Actuarial analyst: Exactly. The report says both increased on its comparable basis, rather than attributing everything to one driver.
  5. Portfolio manager: Does higher severity mean every individual claim became more expensive than every claim last year?
  6. Actuarial analyst: No. An average doesn't describe each claim, and the mix can affect that summary.
  7. Portfolio manager: I'll explain the two drivers separately and include the comparison period and exposure basis.
  8. Actuarial analyst: Good. Keep any pricing recommendation separate from this descriptive explanation of the observed claim experience.
  9. Portfolio manager: Should reserve adequacy be declared from those two increases alone during the meeting?
  10. Actuarial analyst: No. Reserve assessment requires its own analysis and assumptions, including relevant loss-development information.
2. Paid so far is not the final claim cost

Separate fictional actuarial briefing: a recent claim cohort has paid amounts recorded, but some claims remain open and development assumptions need review.

  1. Finance reporting manager: The paid total is available. Why doesn't the actuarial report call it the final cost?
  2. Reserving analyst: Some claims remain open, so amounts paid so far don't establish their eventual total cost.
  3. Finance reporting manager: Is loss development the way reported claim costs change as more information becomes available?
  4. Reserving analyst: Yes, broadly. The specific analysis and assumptions need to be explained for this cohort.
  5. Finance reporting manager: Then a current payment total is an observation, while an ultimate-cost estimate includes assessed future development.
  6. Reserving analyst: Correct. We should label those figures separately instead of presenting an estimate as settled fact.
  7. Finance reporting manager: Could the lower paid total for recent claims prove their severity is lower than older claims?
  8. Reserving analyst: Not without accounting for maturity and comparable definitions. Recent claims have had less time to develop.
  9. Finance reporting manager: I'll request the matched comparison and preserve the estimate's assumptions in the briefing.
  10. Reserving analyst: Good. That clarifies why paid amounts alone don't determine final cost or reserve adequacy.
3. Claim counts need an exposure denominator

  1. Pricing trainee: Portfolio B has thirty claims and A has twenty. Is B's frequency higher?

  3. Pricing trainee: So we compare claims per policy-year, rather than only the raw number of claims.
  4. Actuarial tutor: Exactly. These figures give twenty per thousand for A and ten per thousand for B.
  5. Pricing trainee: B has more claims in total but a lower frequency relative to the supplied exposure.
  6. Actuarial tutor: Correct. That distinction matters whenever portfolio sizes differ in an actuarial comparison.

  8. Actuarial tutor: No. We haven't supplied claim amounts or the other information needed for pricing analysis.
  9. Pricing trainee: I'll state the frequency result only, using the same denominator and clearly defined period.
  10. Actuarial tutor: Good. Keep frequency, severity, and any reserve or pricing conclusions distinct instead of extending one calculation.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

market conduct
How an insurer or financial firm treats customers and carries out market-facing activities.
complaint
An expression of dissatisfaction requiring attention under a relevant process.
timely communication
Providing information early enough to meet the relevant need or required process.
corrective action
Action taken to fix a current problem and prevent recurrence.

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.
  • I'm sorry for ... . The correct information is ... .
  • Let me check that the revised explanation addresses ... .

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

See a clearer version

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry the process took longer than expected. This acknowledges duration, but does not take responsibility for the unclear wording.
  2. B. I'm sorry we could not meet the original date. This acknowledges a missed date, not the communication problem in the question.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.
