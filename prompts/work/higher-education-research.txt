Lesson 01: Research Questions and Study Design
Original fictional case: Doctoral researcher Imani and supervisor Leon revise a proposal asking whether optional tutoring works. Available records cover first-year students in one introductory biology course for one semester: 32 of 40 tutoring participants completed the course, compared with 36 of 60 nonparticipants. Tutoring was self-selected, not randomly assigned. The groups may differ in prior preparation or motivation, which these records do not measure. The immediate analysis can describe the association with completion in this cohort. A future causal study would need a suitable design; it has not been conducted.
Language workshop: What exactly would it mean for tutoring to work?
Communication goal: Specify a research question and distinguish an observed association from a causal effect.
Language guidance: 32/40 is 80%; 36/60 is 60%. The difference is 20 percentage points. Relative to 60%, the difference is approximately 33.3%, not 20%. Neither calculation removes the design limitation created by self-selection.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): "What outcome would count as working?" / "The records cover first-year students in this course." / "The observation window is one semester." / "We are measuring course completion." / "Tutoring participation was optional." / "Thirty-two of forty participants completed, or 80%." / "Thirty-six of sixty nonparticipants completed, or 60%." / "That is a 20-percentage-point difference." / "This is an observed association, not an isolated causal effect." / "Students chose whether to participate." / "Prior preparation and motivation are not measured here." / "Those are possible group differences, not confirmed causes." / "The current result concerns this course cohort." / "A causal question needs a suitable future design." / "The descriptive contrast remains worth reporting." / "The proposal should match its question to the available evidence."
Vocabulary: research question: A specific question a study is designed to answer. / target population: The group to which a research question refers. / study sample: The observed group included in the study. / cohort: A defined group observed over a specified period. / outcome variable: The measured result of interest. / exposure: A condition or experience whose relationship with an outcome is studied. / comparison group: The group used as a reference for a contrast. / observational study: A study observing conditions without assigning the exposure. / self-selection: Entry into a group through participants' own choices. / random assignment: Allocation to study conditions using a random process. / random sampling: Selection of units from a population by a random process. / association: An observed relationship between variables. / causal effect: A change attributable to an intervention or exposure. / confounder: A factor influencing both exposure and outcome that can distort their apparent causal relationship. / baseline difference: A group difference present before the studied exposure or outcome. / operational definition: A precise specification of how a concept is measured. / completion rate: The proportion meeting the defined completion outcome. / denominator: The base count used in a proportion. / percentage-point difference: The arithmetic difference between percentages. / relative difference: A difference expressed in relation to a reference value. / internal validity: The credibility of the study's inference within its setting. / external validity: The extent to which findings apply beyond the studied setting. / estimand: The precise quantity a study seeks to estimate. / study protocol: A documented plan for the study's conduct and analysis.
Speaking task: Read turns 5-8, stressing each denominator and the words percentage points. Repeat turns 9-14, distinguishing self-selection, random sampling, and random assignment. Switch roles for the transfer and read the three completed calculations with their explanations.
Partner: Imani: Doctoral researcher / Leon: Research supervisor
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: In a self-selected workshop cohort, 18 of 30 attendees and 20 of 40 nonattendees completed a task. Prior experience was not measured. The records cover one course only.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Leon: Let us keep the causal question for the next study. For this paper, can you put the course, semester, and two counts into the abstract?
Imani: The future causal effect question needs a suitable design and explicit assumptions. We should discuss that design as a proposed next step, not imply that it has already been implemented.
Leon: Revise the objective to describe completion by tutoring participation in this cohort. Then make the design limitation visible in the abstract as well.
Imani: I will update the study protocol and the proposal wording together. The useful result is a bounded comparison with clear counts, while the causal and broader-population questions remain open.

Lesson 02: Grant Proposals and Specific Aims
Original fictional case: Research lead Sora and grants adviser Ben revise a proposal for a small learning-study pilot. The draft promises to establish that a new workshop improves academic attainment. The actual pilot tests recruitment and follow-up procedures, not efficacy. A completed preparatory pilot recruited 30 students in four weeks and retained 24 at follow-up. The team had specified two local progression criteria: recruit at least 30 in four weeks and retain at least 85% of those recruited. Both must be met for automatic progression to the next planning stage. Only recruitment passed; a redesign or explicit review is needed.
Language workshop: A feasibility pilot cannot promise a definitive answer

Lesson 08: Academic Presentations and Conferences
Original fictional case: Presenter Noor answers a conference question about a memory-strategy study. All 24 volunteers completed one laboratory task in a fixed before-then-after order. Mean response time was 600 milliseconds before training and 540 afterward, a 60-millisecond or 10% reduction. There was no untrained comparison group, so practice and order effects are not isolated from training. No confidence interval or significance test is supplied for this exercise. An audience member asks whether the study proves a general memory theory. During the follow-up discussion, Noor must answer concisely, stating observations, limits, and the next test without inventing evidence.
Language workshop: Answer the challenge without claiming universal proof
Communication goal: Give a concise evidence-based conference answer, distinguish task performance from mechanism, and handle an unresolved question.
Language guidance: The mean fell from 600 to 540 milliseconds: 60/600 equals 10%. Lower response time is faster performance on this task. That observation alone does not identify the mechanism, isolate training from practice, or establish a universal memory theory.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): "No, this study does not prove the general theory." / "Mean response time fell from 600 to 540 milliseconds." / "That is a 10% reduction from the initial mean." / "All 24 volunteers completed the task." / "This was one laboratory task." / "Everyone completed the same before-then-after sequence." / "Practice and order effects remain possible explanations." / "We have not isolated training from those effects." / "Faster responses do not establish the proposed memory mechanism." / "I do not have a verified interval or test result to report here." / "The observation is worth testing under stronger controls." / "A controlled follow-up should address the alternative explanations." / "Broader populations and tasks need separate evidence." / "Are you asking about the observed change or the proposed mechanism?" / "The short answer is that the finding is limited but informative." / "The next study would test the explanation, not guarantee confirmation."
Vocabulary: conference presentation: A spoken account of research for a scholarly audience. / audience question: A query raised by someone attending a talk. / central finding: The main observation or result being reported. / take-home message: The concise main point an audience should retain. / response time: The elapsed time to produce a response in a task. / millisecond: One thousandth of a second. / within-participant comparison: A comparison of observations from the same participants. / fixed order: The same sequence of conditions for all participants. / practice effect: A performance change associated with repeated task exposure. / order effect: A change associated with the sequence of conditions. / comparison arm: A study group or condition serving as a reference. / counterbalancing: Varying condition order across participants under a design. / mechanism: The process proposed to explain an observed effect. / theoretical claim: An assertion about a broader explanatory account. / generalizability: The extent to which results apply beyond the study context. / statistical uncertainty: Uncertainty associated with estimation from data. / confidence interval: An interval produced by a method with stated long-run coverage. / significance test: A statistical assessment under a specified null model. / independent replication: A new study testing a finding with independent evidence. / boundary condition: A circumstance limiting when a finding or explanation applies. / controlled follow-up: A later study designed to address relevant alternative explanations. / clarifying question: A question used to resolve ambiguity in what is being asked. / time allocation: The speaking time available for a response. / unresolved question: An issue not answered by the current evidence.
Speaking task: Read turns 1-8, giving the direct answer before the sample and design qualification. Repeat turns 11-14 without inventing a confidence interval; keep the follow-up study in the future tense. Switch roles for the transfer and read eighty milliseconds and ten percent as different descriptions of the same change.
Partner: Audience member: Conference participant / Noor: Presenter
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: A fixed-order task study records mean times of 800 milliseconds before training and 720 afterward in 16 volunteers. There is no untrained comparison group, and no interval or significance result is supplied.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Audience member: The chair is asking us to wrap up. Could you give us the main result in one sentence, with that qualification?
Noor: The take-home message is this: response time fell ten percent in twenty-four volunteers, but we have not separated the training effect from practice and order effects.
Audience member: Thank you. That distinguishes the observed change from a general explanation. The planned test sounds like the important next step.
Noor: Exactly. The unresolved question is what produced the change and how broadly it applies. We will keep those questions explicit rather than promise that the next study must confirm our preferred explanation.
