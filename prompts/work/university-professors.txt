Lesson 02: Leading a rigorous and inclusive seminar
Original fictional case: In a seminar, student Leo says a campus survey proves that using the library causes higher grades because frequent library users report higher grades. The survey records an association and supplies no causal design. Professor Okafor asks Leo to distinguish the observation from the inference and acknowledge prior preparation as a possible alternative explanation. No participant has established the actual cause.
Language workshop: Challenge the causal claim, not the speaker
Communication goal: Guide disagreement, test an inference, and make room for a reasoned response.

Dialogue 02: Challenge the causal claim, not the speaker
Original fictional setting: In a seminar, student Leo says a campus survey proves that using the library causes higher grades because frequent library users report higher grades. The survey records an association and supplies no causal design. Professor Okafor asks Leo to distinguish the observation from the inference and acknowledge prior preparation as a possible alternative explanation. No participant has established the actual cause.
Professional roles: Leo / Professor Okafor
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Leo: The survey proves that studying in the library improves grades. Frequent users reported higher grades than students who rarely go there.
Professor Okafor: Let us examine that causal claim. Does the survey establish a cause, or does it report a relationship?
Leo: It reports the relationship. I used proves because the difference seemed strong, but I do not know how students were selected.
Professor Okafor: Then start with association. A strong relationship can be worth discussing without settling the causal explanation.
Leo: Students who were already well prepared might be more likely to use the library and also receive higher grades.
Professor Okafor: That is an alternative explanation to examine. We should not announce it as the answer merely because it is plausible.
Leo: So prior preparation might affect our interpretation of the relationship, but we would need relevant evidence to assess it.
Professor Okafor: Yes. It could be a confounding variable. Naming a possibility helps define the question rather than resolving it automatically.
Leo: I can revise my sentence to say that frequent library use is associated with higher reported grades in this survey.
Professor Okafor: That is a qualified conclusion. It keeps the finding while avoiding a causal promise the study design has not supplied.
Leo: I worried that changing my wording would make it sound as though my entire contribution was wrong and should be withdrawn.
Professor Okafor: Revising an inference is part of rigorous discussion. We are examining the reasoning, not making a judgment about you.
Leo: Another student suggested that grades might influence where people choose to study. That is another direction we could investigate.
Professor Okafor: Good. Let us use that as a probing question about direction, rather than treating the first plausible story as settled.
Leo: Could you restate the distinction once more before we invite the next response? I want to be sure I have it.
Professor Okafor: The revoicing is this: the reported grades differ with library use, but the cause of that difference remains unestablished here.
Leo: That gives others something specific to challenge. They can question the interpretation without dismissing the people who completed the survey.
Professor Okafor: Exactly. Our discussion norm is to challenge claims with reasons and make room for a response.
Leo: I will keep the association in my summary and mark the causal explanation as an open question.
Professor Okafor: I will include that in the discussion synthesis, then invite another contribution after a short pause so more participants have time to formulate it.

Professor Evans: Let us set a short meeting agenda: first distinguish the quantities, then use the two groups to check the idea.
Noor: I compared the number of people and did not look at how many people were eligible in each group.
Professor Evans: That identifies the learning bottleneck. You are using an absolute count to answer a question about a proportion.

Noor: For B, sixty out of two hundred gives thirty percent, even though sixty is the larger number of participants.
Professor Evans: Good. That is the worked example: B has the larger count, while A has the higher participation rate.
Noor: I can see why putting both raw counts on a chart does not by itself answer which group had the higher proportion.
Professor Evans: That is the purpose of normalization here. We compare each count with its own eligible population rather than treating the groups as equal in size.
Noor: Would it be accurate to say B had more participants but a lower share of its eligible population?
Professor Evans: Yes. That sentence separates the absolute count from the proportional measure and keeps both facts intact.
Noor: I initially thought the feedback meant sixty was the wrong number, but the number was right and my interpretation was wrong.
Professor Evans: That is a conceptual misunderstanding rather than a transcription error. Understanding the distinction will help with other unequal-size groups.
Noor: Can we check it once more without looking at the completed percentages, using the same supplied figures?
Professor Evans: Certainly. That retrieval practice will show whether you can reconstruct the reasoning instead of recognizing an answer that is already written.
Noor: A is forty percent and B is thirty percent. I will identify the eligible population whenever I report a participation rate.
Professor Evans: That completes the concept check. Use the distinction in your next practice, and bring any remaining specific question to a follow-up meeting.

Dialogue 08: A recommendation is not implementation approval
Original fictional setting: A fictional department committee discusses increasing a seminar enrollment cap from 20 to 30. Professor Grant supports access; Professor Silva asks about feedback workload and room capacity. A motion is amended to recommend a one-term pilot only if staffing and a suitable room are confirmed. The committee votes for that recommendation. Under the supplied local process, a separate faculty board must approve implementation; that approval has not occurred.
Professional roles: Professor Grant / Professor Silva
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Professor Grant: I support raising the seminar cap from twenty to thirty. More students want access, and the current limit leaves some out.
Professor Silva: I support that goal too. Before we settle the curriculum proposal, we need to examine the teaching and room assumptions.
Professor Grant: My concern is that resource questions will delay the idea indefinitely, even though the demand is already visible.
Professor Silva: Let us make them specific. The feedback workload increases with enrollment, and the current staffing plan has not addressed that increase.
Professor Grant: We could recommend a limited trial rather than a permanent change, provided the teaching support is confirmed.
Professor Silva: That would be a pilot offering. We should state the duration and conditions clearly instead of leaving them in informal discussion.
Professor Grant: I propose one term, subject to confirmed staffing and a room suitable for thirty participants.
Professor Silva: That is a motion amendment to the original proposal. We need the committee to know exactly which version it is voting on.
Professor Grant: The revised wording still supports increased access, but it does not assume that a larger room and staff already exist.
Professor Silva: Correct. It makes the resource assumption visible and gives the responsible colleagues concrete information to verify.
Professor Grant: Under our department process, does this committee authorize the pilot once the vote is complete?
Professor Silva: No. The faculty board has decision authority for implementation; this committee sends a recommendation under the local process.
Professor Grant: Then the minutes should not say the enrollment increase is approved for next term merely because this vote passes.
Professor Silva: Exactly. The meeting minutes need to record the amended wording and distinguish our recommendation from the board's eventual decision.
Professor Grant: The committee has voted for the amended proposal. I will tell colleagues it is a one-term pilot recommendation with conditions.
Professor Silva: Call it a conditional recommendation, and keep staffing and room confirmation in the same sentence as the proposed increase.
Professor Grant: We can submit the recommendation after identifying the responsible contacts for those checks, without announcing a completed launch.
Professor Silva: Yes. Implementation approval remains pending until the faculty board actually makes that decision.
Professor Grant: If the pilot is approved, we should specify when the department evaluates workload and student participation.
Professor Silva: Include a review checkpoint in the proposal, so a limited trial does not quietly become a permanent change without the appropriate review.
Vocabulary in this exchange: seminar: Academic session centered on discussion and analysis. / curriculum proposal: Suggested change to a program or course structure. / feedback workload: Time and effort required to respond to student work. / resource assumption: Premise about available staffing, space, or support. / motion amendment: Proposed change to the motion under discussion. / meeting minutes: Formal record of discussion and decisions.
