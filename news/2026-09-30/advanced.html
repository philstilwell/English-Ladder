Bailey: Rigorous Testing Must Precede AI Regulation

Advanced · C1+

📅 September 30, 2026 - Bailey: Rigorous Testing Must Precede AI Regulation

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Andrew Bailey, Governor of the Bank of England, believes regulating artificial intelligence (AI) "is not the right place to start." Instead, he advocates for "rigorous" testing to find vulnerabilities and establishing safeguards to contain risk. Writing his first article for Substack, Bailey highlighted that the risks associated with AI are "real and increasingly significant." He stated that the development of AI should not be prohibited, "on the contrary, the benefits are immense," but emphasized the need for a system of intervention and clear boundaries. The debate surrounding AI's rapid development and its implications for humanity has recently intensified. Leaders from major AI firms like Anthropic and OpenAI have called for a slowdown and an internationally co-ordinated approach to assessing risks and implementing safeguards. OpenAI specifically chose not to release its latest AI model due to safety concerns. Initially, President Donald Trump rejected the idea of a slowdown, asserting the US lead in the "AI race." However, after a summit with leading AI firms, Trump announced that executives had signed a "morally binding" document to serve as a "form of protection" from potential AI risks. This agreement places responsibility for ensuring the safety of their technology squarely on the companies themselves. In the UK, Bailey noted "important work" underway, including the AI Security Institute, which contributes to forming a set of standards. While acknowledging progress, he stressed that "the pace of progress must accelerate" but also advised proceeding "with a degree of humility." Bailey clarified that failures during testing, where "models will behave unexpectedly," are not evidence of testing failure but rather proof of its necessity. He acknowledged that a more formal regulatory framework might emerge over time, yet maintained that immediate regulation "is not, in my view, the right place to start."

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. advocates (verb): To publicly recommend or support a particular cause or policy.
2. prohibited (adjective): Forbidden by a rule or law.
3. on the contrary (phrase): Introducing a statement that contradicts something just said; quite the opposite.
4. implications (noun): The possible results or effects of an action or a decision.
5. co-ordinated (adjective): Organized so that all the parts work together effectively.
6. assessing (verb, present participle): Evaluating or estimating the nature, ability, or quality of something.
7. asserting (verb, present participle): Stating a fact or belief confidently and forcefully.
8. morally binding (adjective phrase): Creating an obligation based on ethical principles rather than legal requirements.
9. squarely (adverb): Directly and firmly; without doubt or equivocation.
10. humility (noun): The quality of having a modest or low view of one's own importance.

Advanced Grammar: Expressing Obligation and Recommendation with Modals
In English, modal verbs like "must" and "should" are used to express different degrees of obligation, necessity, or recommendation. "Must" conveys a strong obligation or something considered essential and unavoidable. "Should" suggests a strong recommendation, advice, or what is generally considered right, but implies less forceful compulsion than "must." The choice of modal can subtly shift the speaker's emphasis on certainty or the imperative nature of an action.
Example from the text: "While acknowledging progress, he stressed that "the pace of progress must accelerate" but also advised proceeding "with a degree of humility.""
