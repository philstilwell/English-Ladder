📅 November 09, 2026 - Hazel Dormice May Be Recovering in England and Wales

Read the story

Dormouse numbers may be recovering in England and Wales after years of decline. The hazel dormouse is the only dormouse species in the UK, and it now lives mainly in southern England and parts of Wales. The National Dormouse Monitoring Programme has been tracking dormouse populations at sites in England and Wales since 1998. The programme found that dormouse numbers have fallen by almost 60% since 2000. More recent results suggest that numbers rose by nearly 10% between 2020 and 2025, although researchers say the estimate is uncertain. This apparent rise came during a period when springs and summers were warmer and drier than average. In cold, wet weather or when food is scarce, dormice can enter torpor, a short period of deep sleep that saves energy. Researchers think warmer summers may mean less time in torpor and leave dormice with more energy to feed and breed. The animals may also have a better chance to build up fat reserves before winter. One ecologist who worked on the survey says it is still unclear how dormice will respond to warmer, wetter winters. People working to save wildlife say looking after the woodlands and hedgerows where dormice live is essential to their long-term survival.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. decline (noun): A fall in the number or amount of something over time.
2. tracking (verb, -ing form): Regularly checking something to see how it changes over time.
3. estimate (noun): A number based on the information available that may differ from the exact number.
4. scarce (adjective): Hard to find or available only in small amounts.
5. breed (verb): To produce young, when talking about animals.
6. build up (phrasal verb): To gradually increase the amount of something.
7. respond to (verb phrase): To react or change when something happens.
8. hedgerows (plural noun): Long rows of bushes or small trees, often along the edges of fields.
9. long-term survival (noun phrase): The ability of a group or species to keep living for many years into the future.

Grammar Focus: Present perfect continuous with “since”
Use the present perfect continuous for an activity that started in the past and is still happening. Use “has” or “have” followed by “been” and the “-ing” form of the verb. Use “since” with the point in time when the activity began.
Example from the text: "The National Dormouse Monitoring Programme has been tracking dormouse populations at sites in England and Wales since 1998."
