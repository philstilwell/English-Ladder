Lesson 01: Resolving a request discrepancy
Original fictional case: Morgan arrives for a wrist X-ray. The request names the left wrist, but Morgan describes an injury to the right wrist. Radiographer Alex has not acquired any images. Alex verifies identity using the approved identifiers, records the discrepancy, and contacts the requesting team through the department process. This case does not authorize Alex to rewrite the request.
Language workshop: The request says left; the patient says right
Communication goal: Pause an examination and verify conflicting information without suggesting an answer.
Language guidance: The patient account identifies a conflict; it does not independently authorize a different examination. Verification and request amendment are different actions.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Please tell me which wrist was injured. / The request names the left side, while you describe the right. / I need to clarify this before taking the images. / I am checking the information, not asking you to resolve the paperwork. / I will contact the team that requested the examination. / Please state your details rather than agreeing with my suggestion. / I cannot silently change the request on that basis. / We will proceed once the discrepancy is appropriately resolved. / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.
Vocabulary: laterality: The specified right or left side of an examination. / imaging request: Authorized request describing the intended imaging examination. / patient identifier: Approved identifying detail used to verify a person's identity. / examination site: Anatomical location intended for the examination. / requesting clinician: Clinician who initiated the imaging request. / clinical indication: Clinical question or reason supporting an examination. / requisition: Document or electronic order requesting a service. / worklist: Electronic list of scheduled or requested imaging examinations. / accession number: Identifier assigned to a particular imaging examination. / identity discrepancy: Conflict between identifying details or records. / side marker: Marker identifying anatomical side on a radiographic image. / pre-examination check: Verification performed before carrying out the examination. / order clarification: Authorized clarification of an ambiguous or conflicting request. / acquisition: Process of obtaining the imaging data. / anatomical landmark: Identifiable body feature used to orient an examination. / request amendment: Authorized documented change to an examination request. / verification pause: Temporary stop while relevant information is checked. / resolved discrepancy: Conflict clarified through the appropriate documented process. / qualified interpreter: Trained language professional supporting accurate communication between speakers. / teach-back: Checking an explanation by inviting the listener to restate its meaning. / patient preference: A person's stated wishes about care or communication. / clinical handoff: Transfer of relevant care information and responsibility between professionals. / documented plan: Agreed next steps recorded for the people involved. / source attribution: Identification of who reported, observed, or established a fact.
Speaking task: Read the corrected exchange, stressing left and right without blaming either source. Swap roles. Repeat the verification and authorization distinction without adding an examination. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied words. Check every explanation, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Alex: Radiographer / Morgan: Patient
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: An ankle request omits the side. Jo reports a left-ankle injury. No images have been acquired, and the authorized team is being contacted.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Alex: Any authorized request amendment must follow the proper process so the record remains clear.
Morgan: All right. I would rather have the correct examination than hurry past a mismatch.
Alex: Exactly. Acquisition has not started. I will explain the verified plan once the team responds.
Morgan: So we are waiting for a resolved discrepancy, not assuming that either account can simply be ignored.
END REFERENCE

Lesson 02: Adapting positioning instructions
Original fictional case: Casey cannot comfortably raise the left shoulder for the planned position. Radiographer Jamie stops the attempted movement and checks the limitation. An adapted examination requires the appropriate departmental approach and, where needed, clinical advice. Casey has not refused all imaging; Casey has said this particular movement hurts. No alternative projection is prescribed by the exercise.
Language workshop: One movement at a time
Communication goal: Give sequenced movement instructions and respond to a limitation without coercion.

An adapted examination requires the appropriate departmental approach and, where needed, clinical advice. Casey has not refused all imaging; Casey has said this particular movement hurts. Professional roles: Jamie / Casey
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Jamie: I will explain one movement at a time. Please tell me before moving if a position is painful or difficult.
Casey: Raising this left shoulder hurts. I can turn a little, but I cannot lift the arm that high.
Jamie: Stop there. This is pain-limited movement, and we should not force the position just to follow the first instruction.
Casey: I do want the examination. I am worried you will think I am refusing to cooperate.
Jamie: I will record the actual mobility limitation, not describe you as refusing every part of the examination.
Casey: Thank you. It is easier when I know exactly which movement you are asking me to try.
Jamie: I can give a shorter movement cue and pause between steps. We can check what is comfortable before proceeding.
Casey: Could you explain what the position is intended to show without asking me to force it?
Jamie: The requested projection is a particular view. I will check the appropriate approach when that positioning is difficult.
Casey: Would a cushion or some support help, or does that need to be checked as well?
Jamie: Any support aid must be appropriate for this examination.
Casey: I would also like you to ask before moving my arm, because unexpected movement makes me tense.
Jamie: Of course. Physical assistance needs your agreement and a suitable assessment. May I help support it while you stay still?
Casey: Yes, supporting it is comfortable. Please do not lift it farther without checking with me first.
Jamie: Understood. I will keep your range of motion limitation clear when I discuss the examination with the team.
Casey: Does changing the way I sit automatically mean you can take a different view instead?
Jamie: No. An adapted technique must fit the clinical question and our authorized process, rather than be an improvised substitute.
Casey: Once that is settled, could we practice any breathing instruction before the image is taken?
Jamie: Yes, if a breath-hold instruction is appropriate, we can rehearse it and check that the wording is manageable for you.
Casey: Clear positioning instructions and a pause give me a chance to tell you what I can actually do.

Rowan: I have heard the word radiation, but I do not know what kind this examination uses.
Taylor: X-rays use ionizing radiation. The team can explain what that means for the proposed examination and your circumstances.
Rowan: Will my uncertain answer still be visible when the person reviewing the examination reads the record?
Taylor: Yes. The screening response must reflect what you said, not a more convenient definite answer.
Rowan: Could the reason the examination is needed also matter to that discussion?
Taylor: Yes. Justification concerns whether the expected benefit warrants the examination in its clinical context.
Rowan: And I would like my companion included only after we agree what can be discussed with them.
Taylor: I will respect that privacy preference within the applicable rules and explain any relevant limits.
Rowan: Who will tell me what the review means for the next step?
Taylor: We will arrange the appropriate radiologist review or other authorized clinical review, then explain the outcome and next step.
Rowan: That gives me a clear process without pretending the review outcome has already been decided.

The reporting clinician will interpret the images, and the referring service will explain the result through its actual process. No reporting deadline is supplied. Ren checks how Jules can contact the service rather than promising a result time.
Professional roles: Jules / Ren
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Jules: I saw a mark on the screen. Does that mean cancer, or can you tell me it is harmless?
Ren: I can hear that it worries you. My role here is to acquire images, not give their diagnostic interpretation.
Jules: But you see these pictures every day. Surely you have an idea of what that mark means.
Ren: An unverified impression would not give you a reliable answer. I do not want to mislead you in either direction.
Jules: Then who can answer the question properly, and how will the information get back to me?
Ren: The reporting clinician interprets the examination, and we should confirm how your referring service communicates the result.
Jules: Is a report the same thing as someone discussing what it means for my situation?
Ren: No. Result communication includes explaining it in context, not merely creating a document in the system.
Jules: I sometimes see reports on my phone before anyone has spoken to me about them.
Ren: A patient portal can provide access, but seeing the words is not the same as discussing their meaning with the appropriate clinician.
Jules: If it says correlate clinically, does that mean the picture did not work?
Ren: Clinical correlation means considering the finding with other clinical information. It is not automatically a statement that acquisition failed.
Jules: Could an earlier examination help the person who is preparing the report?
Ren: Comparison images may provide useful context. The team follows its process for obtaining and reviewing them.
Jules: Can you promise that someone will call tomorrow? Waiting without a contact is the hardest part.
Ren: I need to verify the report turnaround arrangements rather than guess. No definite reporting time has been established in this conversation.
Jules: I understand the limit, but I would still appreciate knowing whom to contact with the question.
Ren: Let us identify the follow-up contact and the referring service's route for discussing your result.
Jules: That is more useful than a guess. I can ask about the report through that route.
Ren: Yes. Respecting scope of practice still allows a helpful handoff for your result inquiry.
Vocabulary in this exchange: acquisition: Process of obtaining the imaging data. / reporting clinician: Authorized professional responsible for the imaging interpretation. / comparison images: Earlier images used to assess relevant differences. / referring service: Clinical service that requested or coordinates the examination. / result communication: Process of explaining a result to the appropriate recipient. / report turnaround: Time between the relevant examination stage and report availability.

Professional roles: Kim / Noor
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Kim: I have noticed a possible mismatch between the image label and the verified examination details. Further release is paused.
Noor: Thank you for escalating it. Preserve the original record and state exactly what you have verified so far.
Kim: The label discrepancy is the concern. I have not established how it arose or whether another service received it.
Noor: Good. Keep the event description separate from any assumption about who or what caused it.
Kim: I have applied the authorized release hold through our process, rather than making an undocumented change to the image.
Noor: We need the approved correction route and an account of what happened in order.
Kim: I will give an event chronology using verified times and actions, marking anything I have not confirmed.
Noor: Include the point at which you noticed the issue and the people contacted afterward.
Kim: The preserved original remains available. I have not deleted it or substituted an earlier image.
Noor: That protects the record while the appropriate people determine the correction and notification requirements.
Kim: Should I call this a near miss now, or wait until the impact is known?
Noor: Use the appropriate classification after review. Unconfirmed impact is not the same as proof that no one received incorrect information.
Kim: We need to identify every relevant downstream recipient through the authorized investigation rather than assume the local screen is the only record.
Noor: Correct. Fixing one display does not prove that all related records are aligned.
Kim: I will request correction authorization and keep the actions visible in the approved system.
Noor: We also need the audit trail, including what was changed, by whom, and through which process.
Kim: Once the immediate issue is contained, the investigation can examine contributing factors without guessing at blame.
Noor: Yes. Establishing a root cause requires investigation; it is not settled by the first report.
Kim: I will complete the required report and flag the unresolved system and recipient questions.
Noor: That supports record integrity and a useful learning review while the actual correction process continues.
Vocabulary in this exchange: label discrepancy: Conflict between an image label and verified examination information. / near miss: Event caught before the relevant potential harm occurs. / audit trail: Traceable history of actions and changes in a record. / record integrity: Accuracy and trustworthiness of a maintained record. / release hold: Temporary prevention of further distribution pending review. / downstream recipient: Person or system receiving information later in the workflow.
