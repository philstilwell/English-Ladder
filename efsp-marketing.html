03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. The scheduling buyer may not be the user

The lesson's scheduling-tool brief targets everyone in an office; the buyer and daily user have not been identified.

  1. Marketing strategist: The brief targets everyone who works in an office. Who actually buys this scheduling tool?
  2. Product manager: We haven't separated the buyer from the daily user, so that audience is too broad.
  3. Marketing strategist: Could we clarify whose problem the campaign needs to address before creating a persona?
  4. Product manager: Yes. A purchasing decision and a daily scheduling task may involve different people.
  5. Marketing strategist: Then an ideal customer profile, or ICP, shouldn't simply repeat everyone in an office.
  6. Product manager: Agreed. We need evidence about the relevant organization and the roles involved.
  7. Marketing strategist: What job to be done, or JTBD, should the research investigate first?
  8. Product manager: The actual scheduling difficulty. We haven't established which difficulty matters most, so let's ask rather than invent it.
  9. Marketing strategist: I'll separate segment, buyer, user, and unanswered problem questions in the discussion.
  10. Product manager: Good. We can define the campaign outcome once that audience understanding is clearer.
2. One signup list contains two different needs

Separate fictional research exercise: event signups include office managers seeking room booking and recruiters seeking interview coordination; purchasing authority is unknown.

  1. Demand generation lead: Can we treat this signup list as one persona because everyone requested scheduling information?
  2. Researcher: The stated needs differ: office managers mention room booking, while recruiters mention interview coordination.
  3. Demand generation lead: So the shared topic doesn't establish a shared job to be done.
  4. Researcher: Exactly. We should clarify the desired task and who participates in the purchase.
  5. Demand generation lead: Could we assume the office managers control budget because of their titles?
  6. Researcher: No. Purchasing authority hasn't been established for either group in this exercise.
  7. Demand generation lead: Then I'll ask which workflow each contact wants to improve before choosing the campaign message.
  8. Researcher: Also distinguish expressed interest from evidence that the segment matches our ideal customer profile.
  9. Demand generation lead: I'll keep the two stated needs separate and avoid inventing budget ownership.
  10. Researcher: Good. That gives us focused discovery questions instead of a persona built from job-title assumptions.
3. A productivity goal needs a named task

Separate fictional campaign intake: a team wants to promote a document tool as improving productivity, but no target task or desired behavior is specified.

  1. Campaign planner: When you say productivity, which task should the audience be able to complete more effectively?
  2. Product lead: We haven't chosen a task. The brief currently describes the entire document tool.
  3. Campaign planner: Then we need clarification before deciding on a segment or a useful outcome.
  4. Product lead: Could a broad message work until we have more detailed audience evidence?
  5. Campaign planner: It might be a hypothesis, but we shouldn't call it a validated value proposition.
  6. Product lead: What would you ask first in the research conversation with a potential user?
  7. Campaign planner: Which document task creates difficulty, and what would a successful result look like?
  8. Product lead: That would help distinguish a feature request from the job they actually need done.
  9. Campaign planner: Yes. We'll keep any persona provisional until the evidence supports a more specific description.
  10. Product lead: Let's clarify the task and outcome before asking creative to produce campaign concepts.

Scenario 2 · A webinar audience with unverified buying roles

A fictional webinar attracted trainers and procurement staff. The next campaign must invite product demonstrations, but no one has checked which group uses the product or influences purchasing.

Role A: You plan the campaign. Ask about role and purpose before treating attendance as buying intent.

Role B: You own webinar research. Explain the known attendee groups and missing decision-role information.

Possible opening: “We know who attended by job category, but not who uses the product or influences its purchase.”

Success checks
  • Distinguish attendance from purchase intent.
  • Name both supplied attendee groups.
  • Agree a focused question about user and buyer roles.
Add a complication

A sponsor wants to label every attendee a qualified buyer.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

  4. Product manager: Exactly. We can explain that users can reuse a template without inventing the resulting savings.
  5. Product marketer: How about create a template once and reuse it for later documents?
  6. Product manager: That describes the supplied capability, provided the final wording matches the feature's actual scope.
  7. Product marketer: I'll take that wording for review and remove the numerical promise.

  10. Product manager: Yes. Until then, capability and measured outcome should remain separate in our messaging.

03Campaign Briefs, GTM Planning, and Cross-Functional AlignmentAsk for an actionable next step · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Make a request with a clear action, purpose, and realistic timing.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A launch brief has a date and channel list but no audience, approved message, or measure of success.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

Creative brief
Document that guides creative work with audience, objective, insight, message, mandatories, and constraints.
GTM
Go-to-market plan for launching or growing a product, feature, offer, or market motion.
Buyer journey
Stages a buyer moves through from awareness to evaluation, decision, purchase, and adoption.
Sales enablement
Materials and training that help sales communicate value and move opportunities forward.

03 · Notice the language

Ask for an actionable next step

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  2. B. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
  3. C. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  2. B. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.
  3. C. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Paid conversions rose after a campaign change, but a discount began in the same week. A slide credits the campaign with the entire increase.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

Attribution
Assigning credit to marketing touchpoints for an outcome.
CPA
Cost per acquisition or action, depending on the defined conversion.
ROAS
Return on ad spend; revenue attributed to ads divided by ad spend.
Incrementality
Additional outcome caused by marketing that would not have happened otherwise.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence checks that responsibility has been accepted?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

  1. A. The check appears somewhere in the notes. A written record alone does not identify who will act.
  2. B. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check. It asks for an identifiable person or team responsible for the task.
  3. C. I mentioned the check in my earlier email. Mentioning a task does not show that anyone accepted it.
2. Which response best checks a critical handoff detail?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

  1. A. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct? This repeats the status and responsibility and invites correction.
  2. B. I assume all results are final, so we can finish. It changes pending information into a final result.
  3. C. I saw the message, so no clarification is needed. Receiving a message is not the same as confirming its meaning.

Your goal

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An email experiment records 120 clicks from 2,000 delivered messages in version A and 140 from 2,000 in version B. No significance analysis is complete.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

CTR
Click-through rate; clicks divided by impressions.
Lift test
Experiment estimating causal impact by comparing exposed and control groups.
Cohort
Group of users or customers sharing a time period, behavior, or trait.
Vanity metric
Metric that looks impressive but does not support a real decision.

03 · Notice the language

Give a useful status update

Lead with the current state, then the important change or open item. Use completed-action language only for completed work. Add a next update point when it is known; do not invent a date to make the message sound complete.

  • We have completed ...; ... remains open.
  • As of ..., the status is ... .
  • The next update will cover ... .

Improve this: Everything is progressing well.

See a clearer version

Three checks are complete; two remain open and are being reviewed.

The revision gives observable progress. A positive label alone does not tell the reader what is complete or still needed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence says a review is finished?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The team has completed the review.

  1. A. The team has completed the review. 'Has completed' states that the action is finished.
  2. B. The team is completing the review. 'Is completing' presents the work as in progress.
  3. C. The team plans to complete the review. 'Plans to complete' describes an intention, not completion.
2. Which update distinguishes a plan from a confirmed fact?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it.

  1. A. Delivery is confirmed for Friday because we planned it. A plan does not establish carrier confirmation.
  2. B. The delivery plan proves that arrival will be Friday. A plan cannot prove a future arrival time.
  3. C. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it. This explicitly labels the date as planned and identifies the missing confirmation.

1. One percentage point with significance still pending

The lesson's email test records A: 120 clicks from 2,000 deliveries; B: 140 from 2,000. Significance analysis is incomplete.

  1. Experiment analyst: Version A has a six-percent click rate; B has seven percent, from two thousand deliveries each.
  2. Marketing director: Then can we announce B as the proven winner of the experiment?
  3. Experiment analyst: Not yet. The observed difference is one percentage point, and significance analysis remains incomplete.
  4. Marketing director: What part of the report can we confidently share today?
  5. Experiment analyst: The supplied counts, delivered-message denominator, and observed rates, clearly separated from the pending inference.
  6. Marketing director: Could we call the change a one-percent relative increase in CTR?
  7. Experiment analyst: No. It's one percentage point; the relative increase from six to seven is about sixteen-point-seven percent.
  8. Marketing director: I'll use the correct description and avoid calling the result conclusive.
  9. Experiment analyst: I'll complete the planned checks and report the uncertainty before recommending a rollout.
  10. Marketing director: Good. A measured difference is progress, but it isn't the finished experiment conclusion.
2. A cohort report with a shorter observation window

Separate fictional funnel report: one signup cohort has thirty days of follow-up, another only ten; final conversion comparison is pending.

  1. Growth manager: The newer cohort has fewer purchases. Can we report that its conversion performance is worse?
  2. Analytics lead: It has only ten days of follow-up, while the older cohort has thirty.
  3. Growth manager: Then the data collection is at different stages, not a completed matched-window comparison.
  4. Analytics lead: Correct. We can report observed activity, but the unequal observation periods limit the conclusion.

  6. Analytics lead: That would align the window, but availability and other differences still need checking.

  1. Executive reporting lead: The dashboard shows three hundred submissions. May I call that three hundred new people in the funnel?
  2. Marketing analyst: Not yet. We haven't checked whether some people submitted more than once.
  3. Executive reporting lead: So submission events and unique people are different measures, even if the number looks useful.

  6. Marketing analyst: Not necessarily. It can serve a purpose if labeled correctly and linked to the question we're answering.

  8. Marketing analyst: Good. The duplicate review should use a defined method rather than remove records arbitrarily.
  9. Executive reporting lead: Then we'll update the funnel readout when that check is complete.
  10. Marketing analyst: Yes, and explain any revised count instead of silently changing what the original metric meant.

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.
  • I'm sorry for ... . The correct information is ... .
  • Let me check that the revised explanation addresses ... .

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

See a clearer version

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry the process took longer than expected. This acknowledges duration, but does not take responsibility for the unclear wording.
  2. B. I'm sorry we could not meet the original date. This acknowledges a missed date, not the communication problem in the question.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.
