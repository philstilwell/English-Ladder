Lesson 01: Taking a dietary history without judgment
Original fictional case: Rina works rotating warehouse shifts. On yesterday's late shift she ate toast before work, had no scheduled meal break, bought noodles after work, and drank two mugs of tea. She cannot recall the mug size. Maya, a nutrition professional, is collecting a dietary history, not diagnosing a deficiency or prescribing a diet. Rina says a typical week is different from yesterday.
Language workshop: Lunch depends on the break
Communication goal: Use neutral follow-up questions to distinguish eating patterns from assumptions about motivation.
Language guidance: One recalled day cannot establish the usual pattern. A missing meal does not identify its cause until the client explains the circumstances.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Could you walk me through what you ate yesterday? / Was that before your shift or after you got home? / What happens when there is no meal break? / Which cup or container did you use? / An approximate amount is useful; you do not need to guess an exact number. / How does that compare with a more usual day? / I am trying to understand the routine, not grade your choices. / The missed meal was linked to the break schedule. / Are you asking about the finding or the next step? / Please correct me if I have misunderstood that detail. / That is what the current record says; it has not been independently confirmed. / That is a possibility, not an agreed outcome. / That decision sits outside my authority. / Who is responsible for that specific next step? / Let me restate the distinction more clearly. / We agree on the action, the owner, and the point for review.
Vocabulary: dietary history: Account of usual food and drink intake and relevant circumstances. / 24-hour recall: Structured account of food and drink consumed during a specified day. / meal pattern: Timing and arrangement of eating occasions. / shift work: Work scheduled outside or across conventional daytime hours. / eating occasion: Any episode of consuming food or drink. / portion estimate: Approximate amount actually consumed. / household measure: Familiar container or utensil used to describe an amount. / beverage intake: Drinks consumed during the relevant period. / snacking: Eating between the person's main meals. / appetite: Desire to eat, distinct from the opportunity to eat. / satiety: Sense of fullness after eating. / meal omission: Absence of a meal from the eating pattern. / recall bias: Inaccuracy arising from what someone remembers or reports. / usual intake: Eating pattern over time rather than one isolated day. / weekend variation: Difference between weekday and weekend routines. / preparation method: Way a food is cooked or otherwise prepared. / food record: Account of foods and drinks recorded over a period. / neutral probe: Nonjudgmental follow-up question that elicits detail. / clarification request: Question that resolves an unclear meaning or instruction. / source attribution: Identification of where information came from. / working assumption: Provisional premise used while information is incomplete. / scope boundary: Limit of the work or authority being discussed. / action owner: Named person responsible for a specified next step. / check-back: Restatement used to verify that a message was understood.
Speaking task: Read the corrected exchange and stress yesterday, usual, and approximate. Change roles and repeat the questions that distinguish appetite from break access. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied choices. Check the explanations, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Maya: Nutrition professional / Rina: Client
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: A client recorded one cup of soup but meant a large mug, not a measured cup. The exact volume remains unknown; the container can be checked next time.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Maya: That helps. I am understanding the routine rather than deciding whether you made good or bad choices.
Rina: I appreciate that. A neutral probe feels easier to answer than asking why I skipped food again.
Maya: We can review a few different days next time using the record format we agree on.
Rina: I can bring a food record from both shift types, without pretending that each amount was measured.

Lesson 02: Explaining serving sizes and label comparisons
Original fictional case: An invented yogurt package contains two servings. Per serving, its label lists 12 grams of total sugars, including 5 grams of added sugars. Jordan eats the whole package. Nutrition educator Lee explains the printed figures without setting a personal sugar target. A second product uses a different serving weight, so a direct per-serving comparison may be misleading.
Language workshop: The package contains two servings
Communication goal: Explain label arithmetic and distinguish included nutrients from additional amounts.

On yesterday's late shift she ate toast before work, had no scheduled meal break, bought noodles after work, and drank two mugs of tea. She cannot recall the mug size. Maya, a nutrition professional, is collecting a dietary history, not diagnosing a deficiency or prescribing a diet. Rina says a typical week is different from yesterday.
Professional roles: Maya / Rina
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Maya: Could you walk me through yesterday, starting with what you had before your shift?
Rina: Toast and tea. I can manage this 24-hour recall, but yesterday was not a normal day for me.
Maya: That is useful to know. We will compare it with other days after we get the sequence clear.
Rina: My shift work rotates, so breakfast sometimes happens when other people are having dinner.
Maya: When you said you missed lunch, did you mean you were not hungry or could not take a break?
Rina: There was no meal break. My appetite was fine; I simply could not leave the floor.
Maya: Thanks for separating those. What was the next thing you ate, including anything between meals?
Rina: Noodles after work. My portion estimate would be one takeout container, although I left some broth.
Maya: Do you remember how full it was, or would the restaurant's container help us describe it?
Rina: A container would help. A household measure is easier than trying to guess the weight.
Maya: And drinks across the whole shift, not only the drink that came with your food?
Rina: Two mugs of tea. That is my remembered beverage intake, but I cannot tell you the mug size.
Maya: I will leave the volume uncertain. Was there anything small that we have not mentioned?
Rina: No, not yesterday. My meal pattern is different when I work mornings.
Maya: Then we should not turn yesterday into a description of every week. What changes on mornings?
Rina: I get a proper break. My usual intake includes a meal at work, not just food after the shift.
Maya: That helps. I am understanding the routine rather than deciding whether you made good or bad choices.
Rina: I appreciate that. A neutral probe feels easier to answer than asking why I skipped food again.
Maya: We can review a few different days next time using the record format we agree on.
Rina: I can bring a food record from both shift types, without pretending that each amount was measured.

Professional roles: Jordan / Lee
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Jordan: I thought the yogurt had twelve grams of sugar, but my colleague says it had thirty-four.
Lee: Let us check the Nutrition Facts panel together before deciding which calculation fits what you ate.
Jordan: I ate the entire package. The front of it looked like something sold for one person.
Lee: The servings per container entry says two, so the printed per-serving amounts need to be doubled here.
Jordan: That would make the twelve grams twenty-four. Where does the extra five fit into the calculation?
Lee: Those are added sugars. The word including means they are already part of the twelve, not an extra five.
Jordan: Then the complete package contains ten grams added, inside the twenty-four grams total. Is that right?
Lee: Exactly. You have kept the per-container value separate from the amount listed for one serving.
Jordan: Does that mean the label is telling me I was supposed to eat only half the package?
Lee: No. The serving size explains the figures; it does not determine your individual portion or nutritional needs.
Jordan: The other yogurt looks lower in sugar per serving. Could I compare those two printed numbers directly?
Lee: First check the reference amount. Its stated serving is a different weight, so the numbers are not yet comparable.
Jordan: We would need equivalent amounts, not just two columns that happen to use the word serving.
Lee: Yes, a like-for-like comparison keeps the quantity consistent before interpreting the difference between products.
Jordan: What about the percentage beside the number? I assumed that meant a percentage of everything I ate today.
Lee: Percent Daily Value uses a general daily reference. It is not a measurement of your actual diet today.
Jordan: I also noticed an ingredient list. Would that answer a different question from these nutrient amounts?
Lee: Yes. The ingredient list tells you what the food contains, while this panel gives the specified nutrient figures.
Jordan: I understand now: check how much I ate, identify the reference, and avoid adding the included sugars twice.
Lee: Correct. Keep the per-serving value attached to its serving whenever you explain the calculation to someone else.

Quinn: Then the health claim can be assessed properly before anyone gives you a personalized recommendation about the product.

Dialogue 08: A referral is not a delivered meal
Original fictional setting: Dietitian Amira and community coordinator Ben discuss client Pat, who has consented to a relevant information exchange. Pat reports difficulty shopping while recovering at home. A community meal-service referral has been sent, but eligibility, start date, and delivery have not been confirmed. No diet order or swallowing assessment is supplied. Amira and Ben agree who will check the referral status and contact Pat.
Professional roles: Amira / Ben
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Amira: Pat has agreed to this relevant information exchange and reports difficulty shopping while recovering at home.
Ben: Thank you. Has the community referral been sent, or has the meal service already confirmed an arrangement?
Amira: It has been sent. I do not have confirmation of eligibility, a start date, or a first delivery.
Ben: I will check the eligibility criteria with the actual service instead of assuming that submission means approval.
Amira: Please also ask what support they can currently offer. We should not promise something outside their capacity.
Ben: Agreed. Service capacity may affect timing even when someone meets the access conditions.
Amira: Pat needs a clear account of what is pending, not a message that meals have started when they have not.
Ben: I will distinguish referral acceptance from the later delivery arrangement when I give the update.
Amira: There is no verified diet order in this handoff. Please do not infer one from the name of my service.
Ben: Understood. A diet order would need the actual authorized instruction, not a guess based on the referral source.
Amira: The same applies to food texture. This case does not contain a swallowing assessment or related instruction.
Ben: I will not add texture modification to the request unless the appropriate verified information is provided through the real process.
Amira: We should keep the information exchange limited to what the service needs for this purpose.
Ben: Yes. Consent to share does not mean unrestricted sharing of every record with every organization.
Amira: When you contact Pat, please distinguish leaving a message from actually speaking with the client.
Ben: I will record a contact attempt separately if there is no answer, and follow the appropriate process for the unresolved need.
Amira: Who will confirm the start date and check what the actual arrangement is while the referral is pending?
Ben: I will own that check and report the delivery schedule only when the service confirms it. Any immediate need follows the real support pathway.
Amira: Then the next update should tell us the status, what Pat has been told, and what remains outstanding.
Ben: That will make this a closed-loop referral rather than an administrative task that ends as soon as a form is submitted.
Vocabulary in this exchange: community referral: Request for support from a community service. / eligibility criteria: Conditions a person must meet to access a service. / service capacity: Amount of support a service can currently provide. / referral acceptance: Confirmation that the receiving service has accepted a request. / delivery schedule: Agreed timing of deliveries. / consent to share: Permission for a defined information exchange under applicable requirements.
