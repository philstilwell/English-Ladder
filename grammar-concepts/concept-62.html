Actually or definitely? Facts and certainty

Choose actually to emphasize a fact or contrast and definitely to express certainty, without treating them as interchangeable.

Level ranges are study guidance. Start with the examples and choose your own pace.

ACTUALLY: A FACT AGAINST AN EXPECTATION

“I thought it was Monday.” “Actually, it’s Tuesday.”

The reply replaces the expected day with the real day. Actually introduces that contrast; it means in fact here.

DEFINITELY: CERTAINTY

“Are you sure it’s Tuesday?” “Yes, it’s definitely Tuesday.”

The day stays the same. Definitely emphasizes that the speaker is sure of the answer.

ACTUALLY CAN INTRODUCE A SURPRISE

I actually enjoyed the film more than I expected.

The result differs from the speaker’s expectation. No other person needs to have made an incorrect statement.

Understand

The useful distinction

Actually emphasizes what is true or real, often in contrast with an expectation. If someone says “I thought it was Monday,” you can reply “Actually, it’s Tuesday.” The word introduces the fact that corrects the expectation. In this use it means in fact. It does not simply mean definitely, and it is not a general way to say how certain you are.

Definitely expresses certainty: you present the statement without doubt. “Are you sure it’s Tuesday?” “Yes, it’s definitely Tuesday.” The speaker emphasizes confidence in the answer. Definitely can also stand alone as an emphatic answer: “Will you come?” “Definitely!” The word communicates certainty; it does not provide independent evidence that a claim is true.

These are different jobs, not a rule that actually always corrects another person. It can introduce surprising information: “I actually enjoyed the film more than I expected.” It can also help soften a correction or refusal, although tone and context still matter. Both words can appear in grammatical sentences about the same situation: “The shop is actually open” highlights reality, often against an expectation; “The shop is definitely open” emphasizes certainty. Choose the meaning you intend, not a supposed percentage scale.
