01Platform Technology and Scientific ThesisMatch certainty to evidence · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A platform produces a promising signal in one experimental system. An investor slide says it will work across several diseases.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

platform
A foundational technology or system that supports several applications or products.
modality
A type of therapeutic or technological approach, such as a small molecule or cell therapy.
proof of concept
Early evidence that an idea can work under specified conditions.
translatability
Likelihood that a finding in one model, setting, or population will predict results in the intended real-world or clinical setting.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. One experimental system, not several diseases

Fictional platform review: a signal was observed in one experimental system, while an investor slide claims broad disease applicability.

  1. Scientific founder: The slide says our platform will work across several diseases. Does the experiment establish that?
  2. Research lead: No. We observed a promising signal in one system, not broad disease translatability.
  3. Scientific founder: Could we call it proof of concept while keeping the broader opportunity visible?
  4. Research lead: Yes, if we define exactly what concept and experimental setting the evidence supports.
  5. Scientific founder: The modality is the same across the proposed programs. Doesn't that reduce the uncertainty?
  6. Research lead: It may inform our hypothesis, but a shared modality doesn't demonstrate the same outcome everywhere.
  7. Scientific founder: How should I describe the broader thesis without presenting the future work as completed?
  8. Research lead: Call it a development hypothesis and identify the additional systems we still need to study.
  9. Scientific founder: I'll narrow the claim and keep the observed result separate from the proposed applications.
  10. Research lead: I'll review the experimental description so the platform story remains ambitious without overstating the evidence.
2. A delivery result is not a therapeutic outcome

Separate fictional research discussion: a platform delivered a reporter into a laboratory cell model; no therapeutic payload or patient outcome was tested.

  1. Business lead: The reporter entered the cells. Can we say the therapeutic modality has demonstrated benefit?
  2. Platform scientist: No. This experiment evaluated delivery of a reporter, not a therapeutic payload or patient outcome.
  3. Business lead: Then what part of the platform thesis does the result actually support?
  4. Platform scientist: A specific delivery capability in this cell model under the tested conditions.
  5. Business lead: Could we describe that as a limited proof of concept rather than a failed therapeutic study?
  6. Platform scientist: Exactly. The study didn't ask the therapeutic question, so neither success nor failure on that question is established.
  7. Business lead: What should we say about translatability when partners ask whether it will work clinically?
  8. Platform scientist: That remains to be investigated. Explain the evidence boundary and the proposed next questions.
  9. Business lead: I'll use a delivery-specific headline and remove the implied patient-benefit claim.
  10. Platform scientist: Good. I'll keep the reporter, model, and experimental scope explicit in the supporting explanation.
3. An observed effect with an uncertain mechanism

Separate fictional platform meeting: an experimental effect is repeatable within the supplied study, but the proposed mechanism has not been isolated.

  1. Program director: The effect repeated. Can we now tell the board that our proposed mechanism is confirmed?
  2. Discovery scientist: The repeated observation supports the effect in this study, but the mechanism hasn't been isolated.
  3. Program director: Isn't reproducing an effect the same as explaining why it occurs in the system?
  4. Discovery scientist: No. An observation and a causal explanation require different evidence, even when the observation is consistent.
  5. Program director: How do we avoid making the platform sound directionless while preserving that distinction?
  6. Discovery scientist: Present the mechanism as our working thesis and explain the tests intended to distinguish alternative explanations.
  7. Program director: Should we change the modality description because the mechanism remains uncertain?
  8. Discovery scientist: Only if the description itself is inaccurate. Don't revise established terminology simply to mask an evidence gap.
  9. Program director: I'll separate the repeatable effect from the unconfirmed explanation on the slide.
  10. Discovery scientist: I'll review those statements and keep any claim about broader translatability explicitly provisional.

A fictional platform delivered payload A in one test system. Payloads B and C have not been tested. Discuss a partner-facing explanation of what is demonstrated and what remains a platform hypothesis.

  • Separate the platform thesis from evidence of broad applicability.

02Assay Development and ReproducibilityExplain a fair comparison · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An assay gives consistent results within one run but different results across three days. A colleague calls it fully reproducible.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

assay
Laboratory procedure used to measure the presence, amount, activity, or quality of a biological target.
sensitivity
Ability of a test, model, or process to correctly identify true positives or detect a change in an input.
specificity
Ability of a test, model, or process to correctly identify true negatives and avoid false positives.
reproducibility
Obtaining consistent results using the same data and methods; conventions vary by research field.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  2. B. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
  3. C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  2. B. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.
  3. C. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An animal study shows an effect at one tested exposure. A summary describes the result as proof of human benefit.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

in vitro
Performed outside a living organism, typically in a laboratory vessel, cell system, or controlled assay.
in vivo
Performed in a living organism, such as an animal or human study participant.
toxicity
The capacity of a substance or exposure to cause harmful biological effects.
safety margin
The difference between a limit and an observed or expected value under a stated definition.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence says a review is finished?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The team has completed the review.

  1. A. The team is completing the review. 'Is completing' presents the work as in progress.
  2. B. The team plans to complete the review. 'Plans to complete' describes an intention, not completion.
  3. C. The team has completed the review. 'Has completed' states that the action is finished.
2. Which update distinguishes a plan from a confirmed fact?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it.

  1. A. The delivery plan proves that arrival will be Friday. A plan cannot prove a future arrival time.
  2. B. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it. This explicitly labels the date as planned and identifies the missing confirmation.
  3. C. Delivery is confirmed for Friday because we planned it. A plan does not establish carrier confirmation.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

IP
Intellectual property: legally recognized rights in certain creations, inventions, and identifying marks.
freedom to operate
Assessment of whether a product or activity can proceed without infringing another party's enforceable intellectual-property rights.
material transfer agreement
An agreement setting conditions for transferring and using research materials.
publication rights
The agreed rights and conditions governing disclosure or publication of work.

03 · Notice the language

Ask for an actionable next step

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  2. B. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
  3. C. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  2. B. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.
  3. C. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  2. B. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.

Answer: C. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

1. Completed experiments versus the funding milestone

Fictional board preparation: three assay runs at site A met the within-site criterion. The next funding-review milestone requires reviewed evidence from both sites A and B; site B has not run the assay, and funding is not guaranteed.

  1. Chief executive: Three runs met the criterion at site A. Have we reached the funding milestone?
  2. Program lead: Not yet. The milestone requires reviewed evidence from both sites, and site B hasn't run the assay.
  3. Chief executive: So completed within-site work is progress, but it doesn't establish between-site performance?
  4. Program lead: Exactly. Site A's result addresses one question; the second site's evidence remains missing.
  5. Chief executive: Can I describe this as all technical risk removed before the board discusses funding?
  6. Program lead: No. That would hide the untested site and overstate what those three runs establish.
  7. Chief executive: Then today's status is site A criterion met, site B work pending, milestone not yet achieved.
  8. Program lead: Correct. Completing the eventual milestone would support funding review, not guarantee financing or extend runway automatically.
  9. Chief executive: I'll separate the scientific achievement, remaining evidence, and financial assumptions in the update.
  10. Program lead: I'll bring the site B planning questions so the board can discuss the next decision with that gap visible.
2. A milestone reached with a remaining uncertainty

Separate fictional board exercise: a predefined assay milestone is achieved, but between-site performance remains untested and was outside that milestone's scope.

  1. Board presenter: We achieved the assay milestone. Can I say the program's technical risk has been removed?
  2. Research lead: No. That milestone covered a defined scope; between-site performance remains untested.
  3. Board presenter: So achieving the milestone and completing all risk mitigation are not the same statement?
  4. Research lead: Exactly. A milestone can be achieved while important follow-up questions remain.
  5. Board presenter: How do I describe the achievement without letting the limitation erase its value?
  6. Research lead: State the agreed criterion was met, then explain the next unresolved question and its significance.
  7. Board presenter: Could we call it an inflection point if the board hasn't discussed the decision impact?

  9. Board presenter: I'll show the achieved milestone beside the remaining between-site assessment.
  10. Research lead: I'll prepare that distinction and keep any next funding decision separate from the completed scientific criterion.

Separate fictional finance rehearsal: current cash supports nine months at one planned spending rate and six months at a higher proposed rate; neither plan is selected.

Negotiate scope or timing while making conditions explicit.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A potential partner requests exclusive rights before the scope of the collaboration is defined.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

term sheet
A summary of proposed transaction terms, whose binding effect depends on its wording and context.
option
A possible course of action; in an agreement, a specified right that may be exercised.
exclusivity
An arrangement limiting specified rights or activities to one party for an agreed scope.
due diligence
Structured review of facts, risks, obligations, and evidence before a transaction, onboarding, investment, or decision.

03 · Notice the language

Offer a workable tradeoff

Acknowledge the goal and explain the available choices. Connect an offer to its actual conditions. A useful response makes room for discussion without promising approval or resources you do not control.

  • We can ..., provided that ... .
  • Would you prefer ... or ...?
  • I can take that proposal for review; I cannot yet confirm ... .

Improve this: If you will approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

See a clearer version

If you approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

For an ordinary future condition, the “if” clause uses present tense. The other clause can express the future consequence or possibility.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence presents a normal future condition clearly?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule.

  1. A. If the scope will change, we will review the schedule. For this ordinary future condition, use present simple in the “if” clause.
  2. B. If the scope changes, we reviewed the schedule. “Reviewed” places the response in the past instead of expressing the future response.
  3. C. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule. The “if” clause uses present tense; the main clause states the future response.
2. Which response negotiates a new request without silently promising extra work?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

  1. A. We guarantee the original date without assessing the addition. It promises an outcome without examining the consequences.
  2. B. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope. This offers a useful next step while keeping the approval and scope question open.
  3. C. We have included it, although its scope has not been reviewed. It treats unreviewed work as already included.

Original fictional case: A platform produces a promising signal in one experimental system. An investor slide says it will work across several diseases.
Language workshop: Match certainty to evidence
Communication goal: Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.
Language guidance: Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The available evidence shows ... . / This may indicate ..., but ... . / We have not yet established whether ... .
