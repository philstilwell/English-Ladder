01Product Discovery and Problem FramingAsk a precise question · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Clarify missing information before responding.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A stakeholder requests a new button without explaining the user problem. Support notes mention difficulty finding an existing action.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

user need
Something a user needs to accomplish in a particular context.
persona
A research-informed representation of a user group used to guide design or communication.
use case
A specific situation describing how someone uses a product or service to achieve a goal.
opportunity cost
The value of the best alternative given up when choosing an option.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

Could you explain what this means?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  3. C. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  2. B. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  3. C. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Find the action before adding another button

Fictional discovery discussion using the lesson case: a stakeholder requests a button, while support notes describe difficulty finding an existing action.

  1. Sales stakeholder: Could you add a new export button to the home screen in the next release?
  2. Product manager: Which user need would it address? Support notes mention difficulty finding the existing export action.
  3. Sales stakeholder: Customers ask our team where to download their records. I assumed another button would solve that.
  4. Product manager: That suggests a discoverability problem, but we haven't established the best solution or affected persona yet.
  5. Sales stakeholder: Could we look at the support examples before deciding where any new control belongs?
  6. Product manager: Yes. Let's clarify who was trying to export, from which page, and for what use case.
  7. Sales stakeholder: I don't have those details here. I'll bring the original notes rather than invent a typical customer.
  8. Product manager: Good. We should also consider opportunity cost before committing development capacity to a proposed solution.
  9. Sales stakeholder: I'll describe the request as an export-discovery question, not an approved home-screen feature.
  10. Product manager: Then we can investigate the problem and compare possible responses with the evidence in view.
2. Who needs the weekly digest?

Fictional discovery interview: a department lead requests a weekly email digest but has not identified recipients or decisions it should support.

  1. Department lead: Our product needs a weekly email digest. Can that go straight into the backlog?
  2. Product researcher: What decision would recipients make from it, and which people would actually receive the message?
  3. Department lead: I had managers in mind, but I haven't asked whether they need weekly summaries.
  4. Product researcher: Then the recipient persona and frequency are assumptions. Could we explore the underlying reporting task first?
  5. Department lead: Managers sometimes ask for project updates. I don't know whether email is their preferred channel.
  6. Product researcher: That's useful context, not confirmation of a digest use case. Can we speak with those managers?
  7. Department lead: Yes. I'll introduce you and avoid promising a feature before we understand their workflow.
  8. Product researcher: I'll ask what they currently review, what is missing, and what happens when information arrives late.
  9. Department lead: Can the discovery conversation also reveal whether an existing view already meets part of the need?
  10. Product researcher: Absolutely. That helps us compare options and avoid spending capacity before the problem is clear.
3. A competitor feature is not a user problem

Fictional roadmap intake: a stakeholder asks for color-coded folders because a competitor offers them; no customer use case has been established.

  1. Marketing lead: A competitor has color-coded folders. Should we match that feature before our next announcement?
  2. Product manager: What user problem would colors solve in our product, beyond making the feature lists look similar?
  3. Marketing lead: Possibly faster organization, but that's my interpretation rather than something customers have told us.
  4. Product manager: Then let's keep it as a hypothesis and identify a concrete organization task to investigate.
  5. Marketing lead: Could we ask people who manage many folders how they currently find the right one?
  6. Product manager: Yes. We should learn their use case and constraints without leading them toward colors as the answer.
  7. Marketing lead: I can help recruit relevant users, although we haven't defined that persona precisely yet.
  8. Product manager: Let's define the recruitment criteria together and consider the opportunity cost of any proposed build.
  9. Marketing lead: I'll hold the launch claim until we have a problem statement and a prioritization decision.
  10. Product manager: Good. Competitive information can prompt a question without dictating the solution we eventually choose.

Scenario 2 · Why do users ask for printing?

A fictional support queue contains four requests for a print button. The records do not explain whether users need paper copies, portable files, or approval evidence. Clarify the use case before proposing a feature.

Role A: Product manager: ask what task sits behind the request.

Role B: Support lead: explain the limited evidence and offer to follow up with the requesters.

Possible opening: “We have four requests for printing, but we haven't established what users need the output for.”

Success checks
  • Separate requested solution from underlying need.
  • Identify missing audience and task information.
  • Agree a focused follow-up without promising implementation.
Add a complication

A stakeholder says four identical feature requests prove one identical need.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

02Roadmaps and PrioritizationOffer a workable tradeoff · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Negotiate scope or timing while making conditions explicit.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A roadmap has three large features and capacity for two. All three sponsors describe their request as urgent.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

roadmap
A plan showing intended product or organizational direction over time, with varying levels of commitment.
prioritization
Deliberate ordering of work based on value, urgency, risk, capacity, and dependencies.
dependency
Work, approval, input, vendor, system, or decision that another activity relies on.
capacity
Available people, assets, time, space, or system throughput for a given workload.

03 · Notice the language

Offer a workable tradeoff

Acknowledge the goal and explain the available choices. Connect an offer to its actual conditions. A useful response makes room for discussion without promising approval or resources you do not control.

  • We can ..., provided that ... .
  • Would you prefer ... or ...?
  • I can take that proposal for review; I cannot yet confirm ... .

Improve this: If you will approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

See a clearer version

If you approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

For an ordinary future condition, the “if” clause uses present tense. The other clause can express the future consequence or possibility.

1. Which sentence presents a normal future condition clearly?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule.

  1. A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule. The “if” clause uses present tense; the main clause states the future response.
  2. B. If the scope will change, we will review the schedule. For this ordinary future condition, use present simple in the “if” clause.
  3. C. If the scope changes, we reviewed the schedule. “Reviewed” places the response in the past instead of expressing the future response.
2. Which response negotiates a new request without silently promising extra work?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

  1. A. We have included it, although its scope has not been reviewed. It treats unreviewed work as already included.
  2. B. We guarantee the original date without assessing the addition. It promises an outcome without examining the consequences.
  3. C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope. This offers a useful next step while keeping the approval and scope question open.

Your goal

Make a request with a clear action, purpose, and realistic timing.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A user story says the page should load "quickly." The team needs a testable completion condition.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

user story
A concise description of a user's goal and the value a product capability should provide.
acceptance criteria
Specific conditions a deliverable must meet to be accepted.
edge case
An unusual or boundary condition a product or process may need to handle.
definition of done
An agreed description of the work required before an item is considered complete.

03 · Notice the language

Ask for an actionable next step

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
  2. B. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  3. C. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.
  2. B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  3. C. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.

05Experimentation and A/B TestingMatch certainty to evidence · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

An A/B test shows a higher conversion rate in one version after one day. The planned test duration has not elapsed.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

A/B test
Controlled comparison of two variants to estimate the effect of a change on a defined outcome.
hypothesis
A proposed explanation or prediction that can be examined using evidence.
guardrail metric
A measure monitored to detect an unwanted consequence of an experiment or change.
statistical significance
A result meeting a specified statistical test threshold under its assumptions; it does not establish practical importance or causation.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

release
Authorization to make a product available or proceed to a next stage, depending on the field.
rollback
Returning a system or release to a previous state under an agreed process.
enablement
Resources and preparation that help a team perform a role effectively.
migration
Moving data, users, or operations from one system or arrangement to another.

03 · Notice the language

Transfer information and responsibility

State the current situation, relevant background, open task, and receiving role. Ask for acknowledgment or repeat-back of the critical item. Sending a message and transferring responsibility are not always the same thing.

  • The current status is ...; the outstanding item is ... .
  • The record shows ... .
  • Please confirm who has accepted ... .

Improve this: Someone should follow up on this.

See a clearer version

Please confirm which team has accepted the follow-up task.

The revised sentence asks for an identifiable receiving team instead of leaving responsibility with an unspecified 'someone'.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence checks that responsibility has been accepted?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

  1. A. I mentioned the check in my earlier email. Mentioning a task does not show that anyone accepted it.
  2. B. The check appears somewhere in the notes. A written record alone does not identify who will act.
  3. C. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check. It asks for an identifiable person or team responsible for the task.
2. Which response best checks a critical handoff detail?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

  1. A. I saw the message, so no clarification is needed. Receiving a message is not the same as confirming its meaning.
  2. B. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct? This repeats the status and responsibility and invites correction.
  3. C. I assume all results are final, so we can finish. It changes pending information into a final result.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

technical debt
Future work arising from design or implementation choices that make later changes harder.
API
Application programming interface; a defined way for software systems to exchange data or trigger actions.
platform
A foundational technology or system that supports several applications or products.
reliability
The ability to perform an intended function consistently under stated conditions over time.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
  2. B. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
  3. C. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.
  2. B. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.
  3. C. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

stakeholder
Person or group with an interest, risk, authority, or dependency in the work.
strategic fit
How well an option supports an organization's objectives and capabilities.
tradeoff
A choice that improves one objective while giving up something on another.
decision memo
A concise document presenting a decision question, options, evidence, and recommendation.

03 · Notice the language

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Improve this: Nobody objected, so everyone agrees.

See a clearer version

Before I record agreement, does anyone have a concern or need more time?

The revised question gives people a way to raise a concern. Silence can have several meanings, especially in unfamiliar or unequal-power situations.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
  2. B. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
  3. C. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.
  2. B. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.
  3. C. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Agree a criterion before choosing a feature

Fictional executive discussion using the lesson case: two executives favor different product options without a shared decision criterion.

  1. Product manager: We have competing preferences. Could we agree the decision criterion before selecting either product option?
  2. Executive sponsor: One executive values expansion, while the other wants stronger retention. Neither has defined the priority.
  3. Product manager: Let's invite both to explain strategic fit and the outcome each option is expected to support.
  4. Executive sponsor: Would collecting their opinions again simply repeat the disagreement without moving us forward?
  5. Product manager: We should ask for evidence and tradeoffs, then clarify who has the authority to decide.
  6. Executive sponsor: I'll confirm the decision owner. We also need the support team's view of the operational impact.
  7. Product manager: Agreed. Their perspective belongs in the discussion before any recommendation becomes a decision memo.

Fictional product meeting: an executive strongly supports a redesign; the accessibility lead has not spoken and review findings remain unknown.

  2. Product manager: We haven't heard from the accessibility lead, and their review findings aren't available yet.
