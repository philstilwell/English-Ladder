Lesson 01: Protecting Day 1 business continuity
Original fictional case: An overseas buyer completed the acquisition of a fictional US business on Friday. On Monday, employees must be able to enter the site, receive support, and follow the existing approved payroll process. The shared HR platform is not ready, and no immediate payroll migration is authorized. Integration consultant Aya and US operations lead Marcus prioritize continuity and identify the later migration work separately.
Language workshop: The deal closed; the payroll process did not move overnight
Communication goal: Separate legal closing, immediate operating needs, and later integration milestones.
Language guidance: Legal close and completed integration are different. The supplied Monday plan preserves the existing payroll process; it does not authorize immediate migration.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Closing is complete; platform integration is not. / Which services must work when employees arrive on Monday? / Payroll remains on the existing approved process. / Migration depends on readiness checks and the required authorization. / Who owns continuity if the new platform is delayed? / This is the temporary operating arrangement, not the final design. / Employees need one clear support route for the transition. / We are ready for the stated Day 1 scope, not every future integration change. / Are you asking about the finding or the next step? / Please correct me if I have misunderstood that detail. / That is what the current record says; it has not been independently confirmed. / That is a possibility, not an agreed outcome. / That decision sits outside my authority. / Who is responsible for that specific next step? / Let me restate the distinction more clearly. / We agree on the action, the owner, and the point for review.
Vocabulary: legal close: Completion of the transaction under its actual closing requirements. / Day 1 readiness: Preparedness for required operations immediately after closing. / business continuity: Ability to sustain required operations through change or disruption. / integration management office: Team coordinating integration governance and workstreams. / workstream: Defined area of coordinated integration work. / critical service: Service whose interruption materially affects required operations. / minimum operating requirement: Condition needed for the specified operation to function. / transition service agreement: Contract for specified temporary support during a transition. / standalone capability: Ability to perform a function without the temporary support being replaced. / interim operating model: Temporary arrangement used during transition. / target operating model: Intended future arrangement of roles, processes, and systems. / readiness checkpoint: Defined review of whether prerequisites are met. / dependency: Requirement or preceding activity on which another task relies. / continuity owner: Person accountable for keeping a specified operation functioning. / hypercare: Time-limited heightened support after a transition. / service desk: Contact function handling user support requests. / critical-path item: Task whose timing can constrain the overall sequence. / Day 100 milestone: Stated later integration target, not a universal legal deadline. / clarification request: Question that resolves an unclear meaning or instruction. / source attribution: Identification of where information came from. / working assumption: Provisional premise used while information is incomplete. / scope boundary: Limit of the work or authority being discussed. / action owner: Named person responsible for a specified next step. / check-back: Restatement used to verify that a message was understood.
Speaking task: Read the corrected exchange and distinguish closed, ready for Day 1, interim, and fully integrated. Switch roles and deliver the Monday message without announcing an unauthorized payroll change. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied choices. Check the explanations, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Aya: Integration consultant / Marcus: US operations lead
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: The employee portal has new branding, but the approved support process remains unchanged. The shared support platform has not launched.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Aya: Before the later migration, we can review the actual evidence that the required tests and authorizations are complete.
Marcus: That is the purpose of a readiness checkpoint. A target date should not substitute for verified readiness.
Aya: I will revise the message: the transaction has closed, immediate operations continue under the stated plan, and migration work remains scheduled for review.
Marcus: Good. The target operating model can remain our destination without being announced as a completed fact on the first working day.

Lesson 02: Clarifying integration decision rights
Original fictional case: A global sponsor supports standardizing customer credit terms. The fictional integration decision matrix requires finance ownership, local legal consultation, and steering-committee approval before the US process changes. Consultant Leo coordinates the proposal but cannot approve it. The sales workstream asks to implement immediately because the sponsor liked the concept. None of the required decision steps has been completed.
Language workshop: Sponsor support is not process-change authorization

Oscar: The procurement change saves fifty thousand dollars a month, so I reported six hundred thousand as realized integration savings.
Nina: That is the annualized run-rate saving, not the completed-period result. We have three months of actual savings in the supplied record.
Oscar: Three times fifty thousand gives one hundred fifty thousand of gross savings for those completed months.
Nina: Correct. That is the in-period realization before the related cost included in this reporting basis.
Oscar: The one-time implementation expense was ninety thousand in the same period, and the report uses the stated cash basis.
Nina: Then include the one-time integration cost rather than letting the headline imply the change was free to deliver.
Oscar: The net realized benefit is therefore sixty thousand, even though the monthly saving could continue beyond this quarter.
Nina: Exactly. The net benefit and the annual run rate are useful but different measures.
Oscar: I also noticed that IT listed the same vendor reduction in its workstream total because it helped remove duplicate licenses.
Nina: We need to prevent double counting. Acknowledging both teams does not create two separate economic benefits.
Oscar: We can assign one shared record to the saving and show IT and procurement as contributors.
Nina: Put that in the benefit register, with a single accountable owner for assembling and validating the evidence.
Oscar: We should also identify the reference cost we compared against, so the saving is not based on an unexplained estimate.
Nina: Yes. The agreed baseline and any adjustments need to be traceable rather than reconstructed after the result is announced.
Oscar: If a planned future expense never occurs, should we record that in the same way as this reduction in an existing cost?
Nina: Not automatically. Cost avoidance needs its own definition and evidence under the reporting methodology.
Oscar: I will show the monthly rate, actual months, gross amount, included cost, and net result in the steering pack.
Nina: That creates a clear benefit bridge, with the six-hundred-thousand annualization labeled separately.
Oscar: The revised headline will acknowledge the ongoing opportunity without saying that a full year's saving has already been achieved.
Nina: Good. Complete the required finance validation and retain the evidence, so the report is credible rather than merely larger.

Nina: That is the annualized run-rate saving, not the completed-period result. We have three months of actual savings in the supplied record.
Oscar: Three times fifty thousand gives one hundred fifty thousand of gross savings for those completed months.
Nina: Correct. That is the in-period realization before the related cost included in this reporting basis.

Nina: Then include the one-time integration cost rather than letting the headline imply the change was free to deliver.
Oscar: The net realized benefit is therefore sixty thousand, even though the monthly saving could continue beyond this quarter.
Nina: Exactly. The net benefit and the annual run rate are useful but different measures.

Nina: We need to prevent double counting. Acknowledging both teams does not create two separate economic benefits.
Oscar: We can assign one shared record to the saving and show IT and procurement as contributors.
Nina: Put that in the benefit register, with a single accountable owner for assembling and validating the evidence.
Oscar: We should also identify the reference cost we compared against, so the saving is not based on an unexplained estimate.
Nina: Yes. The agreed baseline and any adjustments need to be traceable rather than reconstructed after the result is announced.
Oscar: If a planned future expense never occurs, should we record that in the same way as this reduction in an existing cost?
Nina: Not automatically. Cost avoidance needs its own definition and evidence under the reporting methodology.
Oscar: I will show the monthly rate, actual months, gross amount, included cost, and net result in the steering pack.
Nina: That creates a clear benefit bridge, with the six-hundred-thousand annualization labeled separately.
Oscar: The revised headline will acknowledge the ongoing opportunity without saying that a full year's saving has already been achieved.
Nina: Good. Complete the required finance validation and retain the evidence, so the report is credible rather than merely larger.

Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Morgan: The process has worked for two days, and finance owns the remaining reporting issue. Can we close the workstream today?
Arjun: The acceptance criteria specify five consecutive stable days. We have completed two, not the required five.
Morgan: I do not want the integration team to remain indefinitely just because one minor report still needs attention.
Arjun: We can plan the handover, but service acceptance and closure need their actual evidence and authority.
Morgan: The finance lead has accepted responsibility for the report. Does that at least mean we can mark the defect closed?
Arjun: No. The defect backlog should show it as open with an owner, next action, and review route.
Morgan: The receiving team also needs to know how the new process works and whom to contact when something fails.
Arjun: That is knowledge transfer, not just a document email. We need to check that the operational team can use the material.
Morgan: We have a draft procedure, but the escalation contact still points to a consultant who leaves next week.
Arjun: Then the runbook needs a workable ongoing contact before we describe the support arrangement as complete.
Morgan: We should confirm the hours and scope of support instead of assuming the receiving team provides the same transition coverage.
Arjun: Yes. Support coverage and the operating resources need explicit agreement.
Morgan: Could we say the handover is prepared while leaving acceptance pending until the stated conditions are met?
Arjun: That is accurate. Ownership transfer still needs the receiving role's actual acceptance and the required record.
Morgan: If leadership wants an exception to the five-day condition, it should be a visible decision through the specified authority.
Arjun: Correct. Do not hide an open risk by changing the status label. Any exception needs its actual approval and conditions.
Morgan: I will tell the sponsor group that handover preparation is progressing, but the operating evidence does not yet meet the criterion.
Arjun: Include that distinction in the closure report, along with the reporting defect and the named operational responsibilities.
Morgan: After acceptance, we should review whether the integration achieved its intended service and value outcomes, not just whether the project ended.
Arjun: Schedule the post-integration review. That keeps attention on the ongoing business result without declaring unfinished work complete today.
