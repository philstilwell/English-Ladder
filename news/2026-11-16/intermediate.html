📅 November 16, 2026 - Four Planets Line Up Before Sunrise in November

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter will form a line in the sky before sunrise throughout November. All four planets will be visible to the naked eye, so viewers will not need a telescope to see them. Mercury will be the hardest of the four planets to spot. It will look dim and remain near the horizon, the line where the sky appears to meet the ground. Venus will appear above Mercury and to its right. Venus, which reflects a high proportion of sunlight, is the brightest object in the night sky after the Moon. On 15 and 16 November, Mars and Jupiter will appear very near each other in the sky. According to astronomer Gideon Bendavid-Walker, the gap between them will be roughly the width of a finger held at arm’s length. The pair will appear near the star Regulus, and the three objects may look like a group of three stars. Viewers should choose a place away from light pollution, meaning artificial light that makes the night sky harder to see. They should find an open view of the eastern horizon and allow around twenty minutes for their eyes to adjust to the dark. The best viewing time is approximately 45 to 60 minutes before sunrise.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. throughout (preposition): During the whole period of time mentioned.
2. visible to the naked eye (adjective phrase): Possible to see without using a telescope or similar equipment.
3. reflects (verb, present simple): Sends light back from a surface.
4. proportion (noun): A part of a total amount, considered in relation to the whole.
5. gap (noun): The space between two things.
6. roughly (adverb): Close to a particular amount or size, but not exactly.
7. light pollution (noun phrase): Artificial light that makes objects in the night sky harder to see.
8. adjust to (verb phrase): Become used to a different condition or situation.

Grammar Focus: Relative clauses that add extra information
Use “which” to add extra information about a thing already identified, such as a planet. Put commas around this relative clause. The main sentence still makes sense if you remove the extra information.
Example from the text: "Venus, which reflects a high proportion of sunlight, is the brightest object in the night sky after the Moon."
