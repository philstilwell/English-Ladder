📅 October 10, 2026 - Alison Hammond's On-Air Health Scare: A Panic Attack Experience
Television personality Alison Hammond recounted her alarming experience of a panic attack on live television, initially mistaking it for a cardiac event, which she described as one of the most frightening incidents she has ever faced.

Read the story

Television presenter Alison Hammond recounted her alarming experience of feeling as though she was having a cardiac event while live on ITV's This Morning last week. She left the programme midway through the broadcast, visibly clutching her chest before being hospitalised for a medical assessment. She later discovered she had suffered a panic attack, an experience she described as one of the 'scariest things I've ever been through'. Returning to her weekly slot, Hammond explained she couldn't catch her breath, genuinely thought she was having a heart attack, and expressed immense gratitude for being present. She added that the physical sensation felt "very real" and exactly "like a heart attack". Hammond described the instantaneous onset of the panic attack as a sudden wave of adrenaline that overwhelmed her body. Overwhelmed by the sensation, she immediately ran off the set, something she stated she would never normally do on live television. This frightening episode has instigated a profound shift in her outlook, prompting her to re-evaluate life and prioritise her well-being. She likened the surreal feeling of the attack to entering the 'Upside Down world' from the television series Stranger Things. Despite initial apprehension, she deemed it crucial to return to work quickly, thereby avoiding associating the panic with the programme itself. She engaged in discussions with Williams regarding the demanding pressures of presenting live television. Dr Williams clarified that while panic attacks and heart attacks share some symptoms, they are distinct medical conditions. She explained that heart attack pain typically radiates to areas like the arm or jaw, and does not subside with grounding techniques. According to the MIND mental health charity, panic attacks manifest as an extreme anxiety response with rapidly developing physical and mental symptoms.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. recounted (verb): Narrated or told a story, especially a personal experience.
2. cardiac event (noun phrase): A medical incident involving the heart, such as a heart attack.
3. midway (adverb): In the middle of a process or period.
4. assessment (noun): The evaluation or estimation of the nature, quality, or ability of someone or something, often for medical purposes.
5. immense (adjective): Extremely large or great; often used to emphasize the scale of a feeling or quality.
6. gratitude (noun): The quality of being thankful; readiness to show appreciation for and to return kindness.
7. instantaneous (adjective): Occurring or done in an instant or immediately.
8. profound (adjective): (Of a state, quality, or emotion) very great or intense; (of a person or statement) having or showing great knowledge or insight.
9. outlook (noun): A person's point of view or general attitude to life.
10. likened (verb): Compared to something else, suggesting a similarity.
11. surreal (adjective): Having the bizarre or dreamlike quality of surrealism; strange, unreal, or fantastic.
12. crucial (adjective): Of great importance; critical.
13. associating (verb): Connecting or linking something with something else in one's mind.
14. demanding (adjective): Requiring much skill and effort; difficult.
15. distinct (adjective): Recognizably different in nature from something else of a similar type.

Advanced Grammar: Participle Clauses of Result
Participle clauses starting with "thereby" + present participle (-ing form) are used to show the result or consequence of the action described in the main clause. They create a concise and formal way to link cause and effect, often indicating a direct and intended outcome.
Example from the text: "Despite initial apprehension, she deemed it crucial to return to work quickly, thereby avoiding associating the panic with the programme itself."

📅 October 09, 2026 - €5m Wine Heist: Thieves Target Prestigious Italian Winery
Thieves executed an elaborate plan, stealing 30,000 bottles of high-value wine from a renowned Italian winery, raising questions about insider knowledge and security vulnerabilities.

Read the story

Thieves executed an elaborate heist, stealing 30,000 wine bottles worth €5m in Italy, according to media reports. The incident at the Florence-based winery, Marchesi Antinori, is believed to be one of the largest heists of its kind in Italy. Italian media further reported that 30,000 bottles of wine were collectively valued at approximately €5m (£4.2m) from the prestigious facility. Marchesi Antinori, recognized as one of Italy's biggest family-owned wineries, stated the theft occurred at its storage facility in Tuscany on a Saturday. This happened as the company was preparing to dispatch a substantial consignment to international customers abroad. At least seven masked individuals reportedly deactivated the security alarms and disabled the cameras during the operation. The perpetrators then utilized company forklifts to load two large trucks with the winery's prized Tignanello, Solaia, and Guado al Tasso Tuscan reds. Renzo Cotarella, the chief executive, affirmed to Corriere della Sera that a theft of this magnitude and value was unprecedented in Italy. He also expressed optimism that authorities would ultimately be able to trace the stolen merchandise. Cotarella underscored this optimism by stating that 'Two trucks full of merchandise can't just disappear into thin air.' He also suggested the specific selection of bottles, typically selling for between €150 and €500 each, implied the perpetrators were expert wine connoisseurs with potential insider knowledge. The elaborate operation was only discovered on Monday morning when staff arrived on site, and no arrests have been made. Established in 1385, Marchesi Antinori exports its products to 170 countries and recorded a revenue of €263 million (£223m) last year. Italy has seen a surge in wine thefts this year, including recent incidents in the northern Piedmont region where hundreds of thousands of euros worth of bottles were taken.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. heist (noun): A robbery, especially one involving a large sum of money or valuable items.
2. facility (noun): A place, amenity, or piece of equipment provided for a particular purpose.
3. collectively (adverb): As a group; as a whole.
4. dispatch (verb): To send off to a destination or for a purpose.
5. substantial (adjective): Of considerable importance, size, or worth.
6. utilized (verb): To make practical and effective use of something.
7. prized (adjective): Regarded as very valuable or important.
8. affirmed (verb): Stated or asserted strongly and publicly.
9. unprecedented (adjective): Never done or known before; unparalleled.
10. optimism (noun): Hopefulness and confidence about the future or the success of something.
11. merchandise (noun): Goods to be bought and sold.
12. perpetrators (noun): People who carry out a harmful, illegal, or immoral act.

Advanced Grammar: Reporting Adverbs (e.g., "reportedly")
Reporting adverbs like "reportedly," "allegedly," or "supposedly" are used in news reporting to attribute information to others without directly quoting them. They allow writers to convey facts or claims while maintaining a degree of distance or indicating that the information comes from a secondary source and might not be fully confirmed by the writer. This practice is crucial for maintaining journalistic impartiality and signaling the certainty level of the information.
Example from the text: "At least seven masked individuals reportedly deactivated the security alarms and disabled the cameras during the operation."

A BBC News investigation has revealed that Bassam al-Hassan, a former high-ranking Syrian officer, is being bankrolled and safeguarded by US and Lebanese authorities. Hassan, who supervised Syria's chemical weapons program and advised former President Bashar al-Assad, is being hosted in Lebanon following the Assad regime's collapse in 2024. The investigation, conducted by the BBC and NPR, found Hassan was allowed to stay due to an agreement with the FBI and other US and Lebanese entities. His willingness to provide intelligence, particularly concerning the 2012 seizure of American journalist Austin Tice, underpins this arrangement. It was previously reported that Tice's capture was a planned operation, with Hassan involved in the leadership structure. Officials from Syria's new government seek Hassan's return for trial, but US authorities prefer he remains in Lebanon, leaving Damascus with limited recourse. The Tice family’s spokesperson said that, if the findings were true, using US taxes to support Hassan was offensive. He asserted that this constitutes assistance to a wanted war criminal’s escape and called for Hassan to be returned for trial. Sources confirm Hassan receives funds from US authorities via an intermediary, making the payments harder to track despite his US sanctions. However, individuals involved in the Tice investigation express concerns that Hassan's deal may continue for an unspecified duration with little discernible progress. He is considered valuable for his alleged knowledge of Tice's fate, Syria's chemical weapons program, and its military ties with Iran. Though Hassan told investigators Tice was executed and suggested a burial site, searches found nothing, casting doubt on his account's validity. Questions remain over the thoroughness of the search, with sources suggesting US authorities halted digging prematurely. The investigation also found that other Syrian officials connected to the Tice case had apparently found refuge abroad.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. bankrolled (verb): To provide financial support for someone or something, often in a significant way.
2. supervised (verb): To observe and direct the execution of a task, activity, or group, ensuring it is done correctly.
3. entities (noun): Organizations, institutions, or groups with a distinct and independent existence.
4. seizure (noun): The action of forcibly taking something or someone, often by legal authority or by force.
5. underpins (verb): To form the basis, support, or foundation for something, strengthening its existence or validity.
6. leadership structure (noun phrase): The hierarchical arrangement of individuals in positions of authority and decision-making within an organization.
7. limited recourse (noun phrase): Having few available options or inadequate means to obtain remedy, assistance, or legal redress.
8. asserted (verb): To state a fact or belief confidently and forcefully, often in the face of contradiction.
9. constitutes (verb): To be or be considered as; to form or make up a particular part of something.
10. intermediary (noun): A person who acts as a link between two parties to facilitate an agreement, communication, or transaction.

Advanced Grammar: Participial Phrases of Consequence
An “-ing” clause can express the result of the preceding situation. Here, “leaving Damascus with limited recourse” describes the effect of the US position on Syria’s options. Replacing it with “because” would make the limited options a reason instead of a result; an “-ing” form by itself does not always express a result.

3. dubbed (verb, past tense): Given a particular name or nickname to something or someone.

5. infiltrating (verb, present participle): Entering or gaining access to an organization or place surreptitiously and often with hostile intent.
6. necessitating (verb, present participle): Making something necessary as a result or consequence of a particular situation.
7. allegations (noun): Claims that someone has done something wrong; the word itself does not establish whether the claims are supported or proved.

9. unclear circumstances (noun phrase): Conditions or details surrounding an event that are not fully known, understood, or transparent.
10. impression (noun): An effect or feeling produced on someone's mind, often initially or subtly, by an experience or event.

Social media profoundly impacts the live concert experience by disseminating "big moments from headline acts" and revealing show details long before many fans attend, leading to divided opinions and artist responses.

Read the story

Social media is significantly altering the concert experience, with "big moments from headline acts" often shared online long before many fans attend the actual shows. Months before you have a chance to see your favourite artist, chances are you already know some of the big moments they have planned. You'll also probably know the set list, the stage design, and - if you've really been paying attention - where to stand to get the best view. For many major gigs, there's no escaping a stream of fan videos if you're online, whether you like it or not. For instance, surprises from Olivia Rodrigo's Unraveled tour, which scores of fans paid a lot for, have already been "dissected online". While some fans "lap up" the opportunity to see what's coming, others are taking "extreme measures" to preserve the element of surprise. Aurélia Collins, a dedicated fan, spends weeks trying to "trick her algorithm" into not serving her content about favourite artists. She finds this effort "worth it for the surprise," even if it means her social media feed is a bit bland. Collins was "gutted" when a spoiler about Niall Horan slipped through, just before her first live show after 16 years of fandom. Having missed live music when she was younger because of the cost, Collins now wants to take it in without advance spoilers. She wants the "full experience," emphasizing the element of surprise that people attending the opening night can get. Not all fans view spoilers negatively; Georgia McAdam claims she loves them. McAdam sees online spoilers more as "teasers" and believes no amount of videos can diminish the excitement of the real thing. She acknowledges that sometimes artists offer different "surprise songs" or unexpected actions at shows. Some artists react by "switching up" their sets in small ways each night to ensure fans coming to later dates still see something unexpected. For example, Taylor Swift featured "bespoke outfits" and surprise songs, while Sabrina Carpenter and Jay-Z also varied their shows. Laura Snapes, deputy music editor for The Guardian, notes that artists can ensure "they're staying in the press" by having something different happen nightly. Snapes advises fans seeking surprise to avoid absorbing online content about the shows. Phoebe Bridgers is taking things further by banning phones altogether at her shows. Attendees must lock their phones and recording devices in a special pouch when they enter the venue to see the US singer. Anyone found breaking the rules will be escorted out. McAdam would not support a ban on phones, arguing that recording at gigs is "part of the experience" for many fans. Conversely, Collins states she is "not totally against it" regarding phone bans. What Collins would prefer is to see more spoiler warnings on videos to give fans the option for surprise. Ultimately, Snapes suggests that even with set list knowledge, fans may still witness "moments of humanity and stuff that's unplanned." She cites Taylor Swift blowing her nose during the Edinburgh Eras show as an example of an unscripted moment.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. headline acts (noun phrase): The most important or famous performers at a concert or festival.
2. dissected (verb, past participle): Analyzed or examined in detail, often critically, by a large audience online.
3. lap up (phrasal verb): To eagerly accept or enjoy something, especially information or attention, with enthusiasm.
4. extreme measures (noun phrase): Difficult or severe actions taken to achieve a particular aim, often because normal methods are insufficient.
5. trick her algorithm (verb phrase): To manipulate the personalized content delivery system of a social media platform to avoid specific types of content.
6. gutted (adjective): Extremely disappointed or upset; feeling a deep sense of loss or frustration (informal).
7. take it in (phrasal verb): To fully comprehend, absorb, or appreciate something, often an experience or a view.
8. bespoke outfits (noun phrase): Clothing that is custom-made to a client's specifications or for a particular occasion.
9. switching up (phrasal verb): To change or vary something, often to introduce new elements or to keep it interesting.
10. escorted out (verb phrase, passive): To be formally led away from a place, typically by security personnel, often due to a violation of rules.

Advanced Grammar: Reduced Relative Clauses
Reduced relative clauses provide a more concise way to give extra information about a noun. They often omit the relative pronoun (e.g., 'who', 'which', 'that') and a form of 'be' (e.g., 'is', 'are', 'was', 'were'). For example, 'Anyone [who is] found breaking the rules will be escorted out' becomes 'Anyone found breaking the rules will be escorted out.' The participle ('found' or '-ing' form) directly modifies the noun, making the sentence more efficient.
Example from the text: "Anyone found breaking the rules will be escorted out."

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

2. prohibited (adjective): Forbidden by a rule or law.

4. implications (noun): The possible results or effects of an action or a decision.
5. co-ordinated (adjective): Organized so that all the parts work together effectively.
6. assessing (verb, present participle): Evaluating or estimating the nature, ability, or quality of something.
7. asserting (verb, present participle): Stating a fact or belief confidently and forcefully.
8. morally binding (adjective phrase): Creating an obligation based on ethical principles rather than legal requirements.
9. squarely (adverb): Directly and firmly; without doubt or equivocation.
10. humility (noun): The quality of having a modest or low view of one's own importance.

Advanced Grammar: Expressing Obligation and Recommendation with Modals
In English, modal verbs like "must" and "should" are used to express different degrees of obligation, necessity, or recommendation. "Must" conveys a strong obligation or something considered essential and unavoidable. "Should" suggests a strong recommendation, advice, or what is generally considered right, but implies less forceful compulsion than "must." The choice of modal can subtly shift the speaker's emphasis on certainty or the imperative nature of an action.

Definitions in English.

1. kicks off (verb phrase): To start or begin an event, period, or activity, often with significant energy or fanfare.
2. bonanza (noun): A situation or event that creates a sudden increase in wealth, good fortune, or profits, often referring to a spectacular or profitable event.
3. full-length (adjective): Reaching to the floor or extending for the entire usual length, typically describing clothing or mirrors.
4. sheer (adjective): Referring to a fabric or garment that is very thin, light, and transparent, allowing much to be seen through it.
5. halterneck (noun): A style of woman's top or dress in which a strap or straps pass around the back of the neck, leaving the shoulders and often the back bare.

7. credentials (noun): Qualifications, achievements, or experiences that establish someone's authority, expertise, or suitability in a particular field.
8. shimmering (adjective): Shining with a soft, slightly wavering or trembling light, often giving an impression of subtle movement or sparkle.
9. keeping a particularly close eye on (idiom): To monitor something or someone very carefully and attentively, often because it is important, interesting, or potentially problematic.
10. solidify (verb): To make something stronger, more definite, or more certain; to establish firmly or permanently.

Advanced Grammar: Non-restrictive Relative Clauses
Non-restrictive relative clauses provide additional, non-essential information about a noun or noun phrase in the main clause. They are typically set off by commas and use relative pronouns such as 'who,' 'which,' or 'whose.' The sentence's core meaning remains intact even if the non-restrictive clause is removed, distinguishing it from restrictive clauses that are crucial for defining the noun.

The most revealing aspect of the recent summit in Washington may be found in what the two leaders chose not to discuss, and what remains unknown. While thunderstorms are a common occurrence in Thailand during this period, the recent deluge has overwhelmed Bangkok's famously congested infrastructure. Fortunately, unlike the 2011 calamity that claimed hundreds of lives across Thailand, no fatalities have been reported thus far in this particular incident. All 50 of Bangkok's districts have been officially designated as emergency zones due to the severity of the situation. The Thai Meteorological Service forecasts continued heavy precipitation throughout the week, though the low-pressure area responsible for recent deluges is expected to migrate towards central Thailand. The chief administrator confirmed that the eastern sector of the city had borne the brunt of the rainfall, with ongoing efforts to pump out excess water.
