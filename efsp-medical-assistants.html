01Clarifying the visit at check-inAcknowledge the expectation before checking the booking · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Describe a visit mismatch without dismissing the patient's reason for attending.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

The schedule says annual review. Patient Alex expected a visit about a recent concern. The assistant cannot decide whether the appointment type is suitable. Nurse Dana can clarify the visit plan. Alex prefers Alex, while the verified record says Alexandra.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

chief concern
The main reason a patient gives for seeking care at the current visit.
intake form
A clinic document used to gather patient information before or during a visit.
patient identifier
A detail used with the clinic's verification process to distinguish one patient from another.
preferred name
The name a patient asks staff to use when addressing them.

Useful word combinations

  • confirm the visit purpose: check what the patient expects to discuss
  • verify patient identifiers: establish the correct record using the clinic's checks
  • clarify an intake response: ask what an unclear answer means
  • use the preferred name: address the patient as requested without confusing record identification

C Notice the language

Acknowledge the expectation before checking the booking

A booking label and the patient's chief concern serve different purposes. Ask what the patient expected, preserve their wording, and leave decisions about the visit's clinical suitability to the team.

  • The schedule says ___; you expected ___.
  • How would you describe your main concern today?
  • I can address you as ___ while verifying the record under ___.

Improve this: "You're booked for a review, so that's all we can discuss."

See a clearer version

The booking says review; I'll tell Dana about your additional concern so she can clarify the plan.

This checks the mismatch without making an unsupported scheduling or clinical decision.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Patient: Alex is what I'd like you to call me.
Assistant: I'll use your ____, while checking the record separately.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. preferred name

  1. A. chief concern Chief concern is the reason for seeking care.
  2. B. appointment type Appointment type describes the booked visit.
  3. C. preferred name Correct. Preferred name is the requested form of address.
  4. D. referral reason Referral reason concerns why another service was requested.
2. Patient: The booking says annual review, but I expected to discuss a new concern. What happens now?
Assistant: I'll ask Dana ____ the visit plan before we confirm any change.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. to clarify

  1. A. to clarify Correct. Ask Dana to clarify uses the request pattern ask someone to do something.
  2. B. clarified Clarified is a past form; this request needs to plus the base verb.
  3. C. clarification Clarification is a noun, but the gap needs a verb describing Dana's requested action.
  4. D. clarifying Clarifying is an -ing form; ask someone requires to plus a verb here.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Alex's visit expectation

Fictional check-in using the lesson case.

  1. Assistant: Alex, today's booking says annual review. What chief concern did you expect to discuss?
  2. Patient: Not exactly. I expected to discuss a recent concern, and I'm worried the booking is wrong.
  3. Assistant: I'll tell Dana about that difference so she can clarify the visit plan.
  4. Patient: Please don't assume I came for the wrong reason just because of the schedule label.
  5. Assistant: I won't. What would you like me to say is your main concern today?
  6. Patient: I'd like the clinician to hear about a change I noticed, rather than just review paperwork.
  7. Assistant: I'll preserve that request for Dana. I haven't confirmed whether the appointment type will change.
  8. Patient: Also, please call me Alex. Alexandra is on the record, but I don't use it socially.
  9. Assistant: I'll use Alex as your preferred name while keeping the verified record linked to Alexandra.
  10. Patient: Thank you. That sorts out the name question, and I'll discuss the visit plan with Dana.
2. The blank box

Fictional intake form has a blank contact field; the assistant checks its meaning privately.

  1. Assistant: The emergency-contact field on your intake form is blank. Did you want help clarifying it?
  2. Patient: I wasn't sure whether it asked for my number or someone else's emergency contact.
  3. Assistant: It asks for an emergency contact. Your own telephone number is in the section above.
  4. Patient: I understand now. I'd like to discuss whom to list before giving you a name.
  5. Assistant: That's fine. I'll mark the field as not provided yet rather than assume nobody is available.
  6. Patient: Does leaving it blank mean I can't have the conversation with the clinician today?
  7. Assistant: I haven't been told that. I can ask reception about any remaining registration requirements.
  8. Patient: Please ask them, but don't enter my sister automatically just because she brought me here.
  9. Assistant: I won't infer your choice. The emergency-contact field remains open for you to clarify.
  10. Patient: Good. I can decide whom to list after I understand what the clinic needs it for.
3. A quieter identity check

Fictional waiting room; patient requests privacy while identifiers are checked.

  1. Patient: Could we check my details somewhere quieter? I don't want everyone hearing my birth date.
  2. Assistant: Yes, the side desk is available. We'll continue your check-in there, away from the waiting chairs.
  3. Patient: Will you need the name from my record or the name I prefer people to use?
  4. Assistant: We need patient identifiers to verify the record, plus your preferred name for conversation.
  5. Patient: My record uses Samuel, but I prefer Sam. The surname is the same on both.
  6. Assistant: Thank you, Sam. We'll keep those purposes clear rather than create a second record for the name.
  7. Patient: I thought asking for a different name might mean starting all my registration again.
  8. Assistant: We can review your name preference within the existing verified record.
  9. Patient: That's helpful. I can answer the identity questions comfortably now that we're at this desk.
  10. Assistant: Let's continue here, and then I'll confirm your check-in status without announcing your private details outside.

02Rooming and factual patient reportsSeparate a report from an interpretation · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Attribute symptom language accurately without diagnosing or minimizing.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

During rooming, patient Pat reports tiredness since Monday. The assistant observed Pat answering questions while seated but has not assessed the cause or severity. Nurse Dana is receiving the report. No measurement values or clinical conclusions are supplied.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

rooming
Preparing a patient for the clinician's visit through the clinic's delegated intake activities.
vital signs
Basic physiological measurements, such as temperature and pulse, recorded for clinical review.
reported symptom
A bodily experience described by the patient, not necessarily observed by staff.
onset
The time or circumstances in which a reported symptom began.

Useful word combinations

  • record the patient's words: preserve the meaning and source of a symptom account
  • clarify symptom onset: ask when the patient says a change began
  • distinguish reported from observed: separate the patient's account from staff observations
  • relay a measurement query: pass uncertainty about a recorded measurement to the clinician

C Notice the language

Separate a report from an interpretation

Use 'reports' for the patient's experience and 'I observed' for what you directly noticed. Neither phrase establishes a diagnosis. Time words such as 'since Monday' and 'on Monday' convey different durations.

  • The patient reports ___ since ___.
  • I observed ___ during ___.
  • The cause and ___ have not been established in this account.

Improve this: "Pat is tired because of poor sleep."

See a clearer version

Pat reports tiredness since Monday; the cause has not been assessed in this exchange.

The new wording keeps the stated experience without adding a cause.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Nurse: Did you see the tiredness begin on Monday?
Assistant: No. Pat ____ that it began then.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. reported

  1. A. reported Correct. Reported attributes the onset to Pat's account.
  2. B. diagnosed Diagnosed means identified a condition, which Pat did not do here.
  3. C. observed Observed would attribute a direct observation rather than a symptom report.
  4. D. measured Measured requires an actual measurement, not this account.
2. Patient: It started Monday and is still happening.
Assistant: You have experienced it ____ Monday.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. since

  1. A. since Correct. Since links a starting point to continuing experience.
  2. B. by By gives a deadline or latest point, not this duration.
  3. C. on On identifies a day without expressing continuation to now.
  4. D. during During refers to a period rather than this starting point.

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. What Pat reported

Fictional staff handoff using the lesson case, without clinical assessment.

  1. Assistant: Pat reports tiredness since Monday and wants to discuss that change with the clinician.
  2. Nurse: Which part did Pat tell you, and what did you observe yourself during rooming?
  3. Assistant: The reported symptom and onset came from Pat. I observed Pat answering questions while seated.
  4. Nurse: Have you established a cause, or is the reason for that change still unassessed?
  5. Assistant: I haven't established a cause or severity. I don't want my observation to imply either conclusion.
  6. Nurse: Did Pat describe it as constant, or have you only confirmed when it began?
  7. Assistant: Only the reported start on Monday. The pattern wasn't clarified in our conversation.
  8. Nurse: I'll take that distinction into the assessment. Please keep the missing detail explicit in your handoff.
  9. Assistant: Then the handoff is reported tiredness since Monday, answers while seated observed, pattern not clarified.
  10. Nurse: That's clear. I'll ask Pat about the unresolved details rather than infer them from the note.
2. Yesterday or last evening

Fictional patient clarifies the time meant by yesterday; no diagnostic conclusion.

  1. Assistant: You said the change began yesterday. Do you mean morning, afternoon, or evening?
  2. Patient: I meant after dinner last evening. I wasn't trying to say it lasted all day.
  3. Assistant: Thank you. I'll pass on after dinner yesterday evening rather than yesterday morning.
  4. Patient: I can't remember the exact hour. Is it a problem if I don't give a precise time?
  5. Assistant: You can say you don't know the exact time. I shouldn't supply one for you.
  6. Patient: Could you also tell the clinician it happened again this morning, not continuously overnight?
  7. Assistant: Yes, I'll preserve that distinction: last evening and again this morning, with the exact times uncertain.
  8. Patient: I don't know why it happened. Please don't write that I blamed the food.
  9. Assistant: I haven't added a cause. Your account describes timing, and the clinician can explore its meaning.
  10. Patient: That's what I wanted. I was using dinner to describe the time, not an explanation.

Your goal

Identify conflicting medication information and request a clinician's review.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

Patient Ren says one medicine was stopped at another clinic. The local list still includes it. Ren does not have the other clinic's instruction. Nurse Dana can review the discrepancy with the clinician; the assistant cannot confirm discontinuation or change instructions.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

medication reconciliation
A clinician-led process of comparing medication information to identify and resolve differences.
medication list
A record of medicines reported or documented for a patient, with its source and review status.
nonprescription medicine
A medicine available without a prescription, also called an over-the-counter medicine.
allergy history
Recorded information about reported or established allergies, including details needing clinical review.

Useful word combinations

  • compare medication lists: identify differences between two stated sources
  • attribute a reported change: say who reported a medication change
  • flag an allergy-history discrepancy: draw attention to conflicting recorded allergy information
  • request clinician reconciliation: ask the responsible clinician to resolve medication-list differences

C Notice the language

Ask for reconciliation, not a guessed correction

A patient report is important evidence to relay, not an instruction the assistant independently verifies. Keep source, date, and uncertainty visible. Do not infer a medicine from color or recommend changes.

  • The patient reports ___; the local list still shows ___.
  • We do not yet have ___ from ___.
  • Could you review this difference with ___?

Improve this: "I'll remove it because you think the other clinic stopped it."

See a clearer version

I'll flag your report for the clinician; the current list still needs reconciliation.

This preserves the reported change without independently deciding the medication plan.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Nurse: Has the other clinic confirmed the change?
Assistant: No. It is a ____ change from the patient.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. reported

  1. A. prescribed Prescribed implies a current authorized instruction.
  2. B. verified Verified falsely says confirmation occurred.
  3. C. reported Correct. Reported identifies the source without verification.
  4. D. reconciled Reconciled would imply that differences were resolved.
2. Patient: I buy this medicine without a prescription. Should I mention it in the history?
Assistant: Yes, tell the clinician about the ____ medicine as well.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. nonprescription

  1. A. compounded Compounded describes preparation, not whether a prescription is needed.
  2. B. inpatient Inpatient describes a care setting, not purchase without a prescription.
  3. C. nonprescription Correct. Nonprescription names the category described.
  4. D. discontinued Discontinued means stopped, which is not stated.

Your goal

Distinguish collection, dispatch, and receipt in a laboratory enquiry.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

A requisition gives 09:20 as collection time; the courier sheet says 10:05 arrival. A colleague proposes replacing 09:20 with 10:05. Both times describe different events. No identity discrepancy or test result is stated.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

specimen
A sample of material from a patient submitted for examination or testing.
requisition
A document or electronic request specifying the laboratory testing requested by a clinician.
collection time
The recorded time when a specimen was obtained, distinct from receipt at the laboratory.
specimen identifier
A label or code linking a specimen to the appropriate patient and order.

Useful word combinations

  • match the requisition: compare the requested test details with the accompanying record
  • clarify the collection time: obtain the time of sampling rather than delivery
  • flag a label mismatch: report disagreement between identifiers for review
  • confirm laboratory receipt: establish that the receiving laboratory has logged the specimen

C Notice the language

Clarify which event a timestamp describes

An accurate time paired with the wrong event is misleading. Ask who recorded the time and what happened then. This lesson does not teach specimen collection, labeling, handling, acceptance, or testing procedures.

  • Does ___ refer to collection or receipt?
  • The requisition records ___; the courier sheet records ___.
  • Which source confirms ___?

Improve this: "The sample time is ten oh five."

See a clearer version

Ten oh five is courier arrival; the recorded collection time is nine twenty.

The revision attaches each time to the correct event.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Colleague: Which time tells us when the sample was obtained, not when it arrived?
Assistant: The ____ time.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. collection

  1. A. reporting Reporting concerns communication of test information.
  2. B. receipt Receipt concerns arrival at the receiving location.
  3. C. dispatch Dispatch concerns sending the specimen onward.
  4. D. collection Correct. Collection is when the sample was obtained.
2. Laboratory: We logged arrival, but suitability review is incomplete.
Assistant: Receipt is confirmed; acceptance is still ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. pending

  1. A. pending Correct. Pending marks the unfinished review.
  2. B. completed Completed contradicts the stated incomplete review.
  3. C. withdrawn Withdrawn would mean removed or cancelled.
  4. D. duplicated Duplicated means repeated, not awaiting completion.

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Two times, two events

Fictional paperwork discussion using the lesson case.

  2. Colleague: It's the courier arrival time. I thought the most recent timestamp should replace the older one.

  4. Colleague: So the difference doesn't automatically mean someone entered a wrong time on the requisition?

Your goal

Repair a scheduling message while keeping result interpretation with the clinician.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

The assistant's message incorrectly says a results discussion is booked Tuesday. The schedule confirms Thursday at 14:00 with Dr Chen. The patient rearranged work. The assistant can correct the message and confirm the booking; no results are discussed in this exercise.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

electronic health record
The clinic's digital record of patient information, encounters, and related care documentation.
amendment
A traceable correction or addition to an existing record made through the required process.
release of information
The provision of patient records or details to a recipient after applicable permission and identity checks.
follow-up task
An assigned action still needed after an encounter, message, or review.

Useful word combinations

  • request a record amendment: raise a specific correction for review
  • confirm the release recipient: establish who is to receive the requested information
  • trace a contact attempt: identify whether and how staff tried to reach a patient
  • accept follow-up ownership: explicitly take responsibility for the remaining action

C Notice the language

Correct the information and acknowledge its impact

State what was wrong and what the verified record says. An amendment should remain traceable. Updating a record, reaching the patient, and finishing a follow-up task are different actions.

  • My earlier message incorrectly said ___.
  • The confirmed information is ___.
  • The correction is recorded; ___ still needs to happen.

Improve this: "Sorry for the confusion; check the portal."

See a clearer version

I gave the wrong day. Your confirmed discussion is Thursday at two in the afternoon with Dr Chen.

A specific correction is more useful than a vague apology.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Patient: Which day is correct?
Assistant: My message was wrong. The ____ appointment is Thursday at two in the afternoon.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. confirmed

  1. A. cancelled Cancelled contradicts the existing booking.
  2. B. provisional Provisional would describe a tentative booking.
  3. C. requested Requested would mean asked for, not booked.
  4. D. confirmed Correct. Confirmed matches the verified schedule.
2. Colleague: The note is corrected, but the patient has not been reached.
Assistant: The contact task is still ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. outstanding

  1. A. outstanding Correct. Outstanding means the contact remains to be done.
  2. B. completed Completed falsely says contact occurred.
  3. C. duplicated Duplicated means repeated, which is not stated.
  4. D. withdrawn Withdrawn would mean the task was cancelled.
