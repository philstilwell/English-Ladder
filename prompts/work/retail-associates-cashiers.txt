Lesson 01: Finding the item the shopper actually means
Original fictional case: A shopper asks for the blue shirt in an advertisement. The ad shows two blue shirts: linen L214 and cotton C218. The shopper wants a medium cotton shirt; local availability has not been checked. Confirm item and size before discussing stock.
Language workshop: Ask one question that narrows the search
Communication goal: Confirm product variation before searching for stock.
Language guidance: Color alone may describe several items. Use material, code, and size to distinguish them without claiming availability prematurely.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): Do you mean the ___ or the ___? / The item code is ___; is that the one? / I'll check this store's stock in ___.
Vocabulary: stock-keeping unit: An identifier for a particular product variation, often abbreviated SKU, such as a specific shirt size and color. / barcode: A machine-readable pattern used to identify an item when scanned. / size range: The set of sizes in which a product is offered. / fitting room: A designated shop space where customers can try on clothing.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Shopper: want the medium cotton blue shirt, not the linen shirt; have not seen local stock information.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: The shopper points at the wrong picture while repeating the cotton code. Clarify before searching.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: The advertisement shows two blue shirts, so let me check which one you mean. Is it the cotton shirt, code C218, in medium? Once we've confirmed that variation, I can check this store's stock. The picture alone doesn't tell us whether your size is available here today.

Language workshop: Compare the same practical requirement
Communication goal: Use label evidence instead of broad claims about quality.
Language guidance: Price, lining, fit, and care are different features. Identify the deciding requirement and avoid extending the label's meaning beyond what it states.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): Both cost ___, but the labels say ___. / This matches your requirement for ___. / The label doesn't establish ___.

Optional second-round challenge: The shopper assumes identical prices mean identical care requirements. Return to the two labels.

Lesson 03: Resolving shelf prices and offer conditions
Original fictional case: A shopper has one juice bottle priced at $3. The shelf offer says two qualifying bottles for $5; single bottles remain $3. The shopper expects one bottle for $2.50. Explain the quantity condition without treating half the bundle price as the single-item offer.
Language workshop: Explain the condition that changes the price
Communication goal: Distinguish an average bundle price from a single-item price.
Language guidance: Use the offer's quantity condition and the same price basis. Don't infer a universal pricing rule from this fictional promotion.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The offer applies when you buy ___. / One item remains ___ under these terms. / That is the average, not ___.
Vocabulary: shelf-edge label: A label beside displayed goods stating the item description and price. / unit price: A price expressed per standard amount, such as per liter, for comparison. / multibuy offer: A promotion requiring the purchase of a stated number of qualifying items. / price check: A request to verify an item's applicable price when information is unclear.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Shopper: want just one bottle unless the two-bottle offer clearly fits your needs.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: The second bottle is a nonqualifying flavor. Explain that quantity and qualifying item both matter.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: The five-dollar offer applies when you buy two qualifying bottles. One bottle is still three dollars under these displayed terms. Two would average two dollars fifty each, but that average isn't the single-bottle price. Would you like one at three, or two qualifying bottles for five?

Shopper: Please do. I don't want an argument with people who have been waiting longer than me.

Associate: Then the service desk is the right place. The colleague there can check your exchange request directly.
Shopper: Thanks. I'll go there, and the queue can keep moving without me waiting in the wrong place.

Supervisor: Both tasks are assigned. Preserve the earlier entry through our normal correction process.
