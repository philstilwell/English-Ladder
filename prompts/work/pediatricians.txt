Lesson 01: Hearing the child and caregiver
Original fictional case: Ten-year-old Leo says stomach discomfort happens before school. His caregiver reports seeing it after dinner. Dr Park asks Leo for his account first, then clarifies each setting and timing. No cause, examination finding, or school-avoidance diagnosis is established. The two accounts may describe different episodes.
Language workshop: Two accounts of the same week
Communication goal: Reconcile different symptom accounts without deciding that one speaker is unreliable.
Language guidance: Before school and after dinner may describe different episodes. Neither phrase proves a cause or a motive.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Leo, tell me what happens in your own words. / Is that when it starts or when someone notices it? / Those may be two different episodes. / What becomes difficult when it happens? / The accounts differ; that does not tell us why. / Your caregiver notices it after dinner. / What does that word mean when you use it? / Tell me which part of my summary needs correcting. / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.
Vocabulary: presenting concern: Main issue bringing the child or family to the consultation. / caregiver account: Information supplied by a person caring for the child. / child account: Information described directly by the child. / symptom onset: Time when a symptom first began. / episode duration: Length of an individual occurrence. / symptom frequency: How often a symptom occurs. / associated symptom: Additional symptom occurring with the main concern. / functional impact: Effect on everyday activities and participation. / school attendance: Pattern of being present at school. / sleep pattern: Usual timing and quality of sleep. / appetite change: Difference from the person's usual desire to eat. / baseline behavior: Usual behavior used for comparison. / direct observation: Information personally witnessed by the speaker. / recalled event: Event described from memory rather than current observation. / contextual difference: Difference in circumstances surrounding two accounts. / developmental language: Wording adapted to the child's communication development. / rapport: Working relationship supporting comfortable and accurate communication. / summary check: Confirmation that a summary reflects the speaker's account. / qualified interpreter: Trained language professional supporting accurate communication between speakers. / teach-back: Checking an explanation by inviting the listener to restate its meaning. / patient preference: A person's stated wishes about care or communication. / clinical handoff: Transfer of relevant care information and responsibility between professionals. / documented plan: Agreed next steps recorded for the people involved. / source attribution: Identification of who reported, observed, or established a fact.
Speaking task: Read the corrected conversation. Give each source its own attribution. Swap roles. Contrast how often with how long without inventing a diagnosis. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied words. Check every explanation, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Dr Park: Pediatrician / Leo: Child patient
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: A teacher reports seeing discomfort at lunchtime. Leo has not yet discussed that episode with the clinician. The cause remains unknown.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Dr Park: Exactly. Direct observation covers what someone witnesses, not every sensation you experience.
Leo: So you will keep the two timings separate until we understand whether they describe the same pattern?
Dr Park: Yes. We will clarify episode duration, timing, and other details without assigning a cause from this conversation alone.
Leo: Then a summary check lets me correct what you heard before it becomes the record.

Lesson 02: Explaining a growth trajectory
Original fictional case: Caregiver Ana sees the 25th percentile on a growth chart and worries that her child has scored only 25 out of 100. Dr Bell explains that a percentile compares a particular measurement with a reference population; it is not a grade or a target. The full growth trajectory, measurement quality, and clinical context still require review. No growth disorder is diagnosed in the case.
Language workshop: A percentile is not a school grade
Communication goal: Explain a comparison measure and its limitations without labeling a child by a number.
Language guidance: The 25th percentile is not 25 percent healthy, 25 percent grown, or a target to improve toward 100.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): What did the number suggest to you? / This is not a score out of 100. / It compares this measurement with a reference group. / Let us look at the measurements over time. / We should verify how this measurement was taken. / A higher percentile is not automatically a better result. / This point alone does not establish a growth disorder. / The pattern and the child's circumstances belong together. / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.

Dialogue 02: A percentile is not a school grade
Original fictional setting: Caregiver Ana sees the 25th percentile on a growth chart and worries that her child has scored only 25 out of 100. Dr Bell explains that a percentile compares a particular measurement with a reference population; it is not a grade or a target. The full growth trajectory, measurement quality, and clinical context still require review. No growth disorder is diagnosed in the case.
Professional roles: Ana / Dr Bell
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Ana: The chart says 25th percentile. I thought that meant my child had scored only 25 out of 100.
Dr Bell: A percentile is not a school grade. It tells us where a particular measurement sits in a comparison group.
Ana: So it does not mean that my child is only a quarter as healthy as expected?
Dr Bell: Correct. The reference population provides the comparison; it does not turn a higher number into an automatic goal.
Ana: Then what do you look at when deciding whether the growth pattern needs attention?
Dr Bell: We review the growth trajectory, the quality of the measurements, and the child's wider clinical circumstances.
Ana: I have earlier measurements from another clinic, but I am not sure they used the same method.
Dr Bell: Those serial measurements may help, provided we check their timing, method, and comparability rather than assume everything matches.
Ana: Would a mistake in entering the height make the chart look different even if nothing changed?
Dr Bell: A plotting error can affect the display. That is one reason to verify the underlying data.
Ana: I also heard the phrase growth velocity. Is that another word for the percentile?
Dr Bell: No. Growth velocity describes the rate of growth over time, not the comparison position at one visit.
Ana: So two children at the same percentile today might still have different histories?
Dr Bell: Yes. A longitudinal assessment considers the sequence, not merely whether two points happen to share a label.
Ana: Does that mean I should stop paying attention to the measurements altogether?
Dr Bell: Not at all. We check measurement technique and context because the measurements matter; we simply avoid treating them as grades.
Ana: What other information would help explain the pattern when you review it?
Dr Bell: Relevant history can include a nutritional history and family information, alongside the actual examination and other clinical details.
Ana: I understand. The number alone does not tell us that a particular disorder is present.
Dr Bell: Exactly. Even centile crossing needs interpretation. We will explain the reviewed pattern without making a diagnosis from this single point.
Vocabulary in this exchange: growth trajectory: Pattern of growth measurements over time. / percentile: Position of a measurement relative to a reference distribution. / reference population: Group providing the comparison distribution for a measure. / serial measurements: Measurements collected at different times for comparison. / growth velocity: Rate of change in growth over time. / measurement technique: Method used to obtain a physical measurement.

Dr Shah: We can discuss appropriate early intervention and the local process while explaining what remains uncertain.
Ben: I would like to know whom to contact if we do not hear about the referral.
Dr Shah: We will explain how to check referral status, including the difference between sending a request and having a confirmed appointment.
Ben: That gives us a next step without pretending the questionnaire has answered everything.
Dr Shah: Exactly. Ongoing developmental surveillance continues alongside the appropriate assessment and communication with your family.

Dr Lin: No. Any catch-up schedule requires the verified history and applicable recommendation, not a guessed sequence.
Dana: That helps me ask a more specific question instead of trying to decide everything from one memory.
Dr Lin: Good. An informed choice depends on clear information and a chance to resolve the questions that matter to you.

Dialogue 05: The label, the units, and the tool
Original fictional setting: Caregiver Omar sees mg on a prescription and mL on a measuring device and assumes they are interchangeable. Dr Cole explains the difference between drug amount, liquid volume, and concentration. The actual bottle, prescription, and appropriate measuring tool must be checked with the prescribing or dispensing team. No numerical dose, concentration, interval, or instruction to administer medicine is provided here.
Professional roles: Omar / Dr Cole
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Omar: The prescription says mg, but the measuring device says mL. I thought they meant the same amount.
Dr Cole: A milligram measures an amount of drug by mass. A milliliter measures liquid volume.
Omar: Then I cannot just copy the same number from one unit onto the other?
Dr Cole: Correct. The actual concentration links drug amount and liquid volume. We must not guess it.
Omar: I have an older bottle at home. Could I use its instructions if the name looks similar?
Dr Cole: We need this product's current prescription label and verified instructions, not a remembered label from a different bottle.
Omar: I also need to know which measuring tool to use. A kitchen spoon seems easier.
Dr Cole: A kitchen spoon is not a reliable measure. The appropriate oral syringe or other specified device should match the verified instructions.
Omar: Some of the small lines on the device are difficult for me to distinguish.
Dr Cole: We should check the calibration mark spacing and whether the device is suitable, rather than asking you to estimate between unclear lines.
Omar: If the label and another instruction disagree, which one should I guess is current?
Dr Cole: Do not guess. A label discrepancy needs clarification with the responsible prescribing or dispensing team.
Omar: Would the pharmacy be able to check the product it actually supplied?
Dr Cole: Yes, the dispensing pharmacy is an appropriate contact for verifying the dispensed product and its instructions.
Omar: I use another over-the-counter product sometimes. Should that be part of the review too?
Dr Cole: Yes. Reviewing the active ingredient helps identify possible duplication; similar or different brand names do not settle that question.
Omar: Once everything is verified, can I show you how I understand the instruction?
Dr Cole: A caregiver demonstration is useful. It checks our explanation and the tool, not your intelligence.
Omar: I can now distinguish the amount of drug from the liquid that contains it.
Dr Cole: Exactly. The dose volume must match the verified instructions. Let us check the bottle, prescription, and measuring device together before confirming what to give.
Vocabulary in this exchange: milligram: Unit of mass used to express an amount of drug. / milliliter: Unit of liquid volume, abbreviated mL. / concentration: Amount of drug contained in a stated volume or quantity. / dose volume: Volume of a liquid corresponding to a verified dose. / oral syringe: Measuring device designed for administering liquid medicine by mouth. / calibration mark: Mark indicating a measured quantity on a device.
