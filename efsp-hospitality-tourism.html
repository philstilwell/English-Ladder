Your goal

Acknowledge a communication problem and give a corrected message.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A guest arrives before the room is ready after receiving an unclear confirmation message. The desk has a luggage-storage option but no verified room-ready time.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.
  • I'm sorry for ... . The correct information is ... .
  • Let me check that the revised explanation addresses ... .

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

See a clearer version

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry the process took longer than expected. This acknowledges duration, but does not take responsibility for the unclear wording.
  2. B. I'm sorry we could not meet the original date. This acknowledges a missed date, not the communication problem in the question.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Arrival confirmed, room not ready

Fictional hotel training: a corporate travel coordinator arrives with a guest group before rooms are ready; luggage storage is available, but readiness is unverified.

  1. Travel coordinator: Your confirmation said everything was ready. Our delegates expected to check in immediately.
  2. Front desk supervisor: I'm sorry our message confused confirmed reservations with rooms ready for occupancy.
  3. Travel coordinator: We have a meeting shortly. Can you give us an exact waiting time?
  4. Front desk supervisor: Not yet. Housekeeping hasn't confirmed readiness, and I shouldn't invent a time.
  5. Travel coordinator: Could you at least take the cases while we attend the meeting?
  6. Front desk supervisor: Yes, luggage storage is available. I'll also request an update on room inventory.
  7. Travel coordinator: Does that mean an upgrade is possible if our rooms remain unavailable?
  8. Front desk supervisor: I can ask, but no upgrade has been approved or confirmed available.
  9. Travel coordinator: Please store the luggage and contact me when you have verified information.
  10. Front desk supervisor: Certainly. I'll keep the room status separate from any service recovery request.
2. The breakfast inclusion correction

Separate fictional case: a reservation lists room only, but an agent's welcome message incorrectly says breakfast included; a corporate booking manager calls.

  1. Booking manager: Your welcome message includes breakfast, but our travelers were asked to pay today.
  2. Reservations agent: I've checked the reservation. It says room only; my welcome message was incorrect.
  3. Booking manager: That mistake affects the meal budget I gave everyone before their arrival.
  4. Reservations agent: I'm sorry. I should have checked the booked package before sending that wording.
  5. Booking manager: Can you simply add breakfast to all six rooms for the remaining nights?
  6. Reservations agent: That requires approval. I can take the discrepancy to our front desk supervisor.
  7. Booking manager: Please don't describe this as guests misunderstanding the package they were offered.
  8. Reservations agent: Agreed. I'll explain that our message created an expectation the reservation doesn't match.
  9. Booking manager: Please give me the corrected booking details and tell me who is reviewing compensation.
  10. Reservations agent: I'll confirm the room-only status and identify the supervisor handling the service recovery request.
3. A quiet-room request is not a guarantee

Separate fictional case: a tour leader's arrival sheet incorrectly calls a quiet-room request guaranteed; no alternative room has been verified.

  1. Tour leader: My arrival sheet guarantees courtyard rooms, but your colleague says they're only requested.
  2. Duty manager: You're right to question that. Our sheet overstated the status of the request.
  3. Tour leader: Several travelers chose this hotel because they wanted rooms away from street noise.
  4. Duty manager: I'm sorry we created that expectation. The courtyard preference is recorded, not guaranteed.
  5. Tour leader: What can you actually do before I distribute the room keys today?
  6. Duty manager: I'll check current room inventory and ask reception to explain verified options individually.
  7. Tour leader: Can I announce that everyone will receive an upgrade instead of a courtyard room?
  8. Duty manager: No upgrade is confirmed. Please tell them we're checking assignments, without promising an alternative.
  9. Tour leader: I'll correct the announcement, but I need one contact for any further concerns.
  10. Duty manager: I'll be that contact and review the service recovery options after checking availability.

Two scenarios to practice

Scenario 2 · An arrival entrance changes

In a fictional hotel, a conference group's confirmation names the main entrance, but its booked reception desk is at the garden entrance. Both entrances are open; luggage assistance has not been confirmed. Correct the message by phone before arrival.

Role A: Front desk supervisor: acknowledge the incorrect entrance instruction and explain the verified reception location.

Role B: Conference coordinator: explain that delegates have the old instruction and ask about luggage assistance.

Possible opening: “Our confirmation named the wrong reception entrance, and I want to correct that before your group arrives.”

Success checks
  • Take responsibility for the incorrect message.
  • Distinguish the confirmed entrance from unconfirmed assistance.
  • Agree how the coordinator will pass on the correction.
Add a complication

The coordinator asks you to guarantee that a porter will meet the coach, but availability is unknown.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
I'm sorry the message was unclear. Your room is not ready yet. We can store your luggage while I check the status, and I'll give you an update as soon as I have a confirmed time.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Notice: Acknowledge a communication problem and give a corrected message. Identify the wording that does this, then name one detail from the case that the response preserves.

Apply it to this case

In “Guest Arrival and Front-Desk Escalation”, choose one confirmed detail from the situation and one item that still needs clarification. Draft a two-sentence response using “My earlier message did not make ... clear.”. Keep the known detail accurate and ask about the missing one.

Check your reasoning

Compare with the case above and the model response. Can you point to the words that support your factual statement? Is your question about something the case leaves open? If you introduce a possible outcome, clearly label it as a possibility.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

Vocabulary for this lesson

A room's recorded availability or service condition, such as occupied or out of order.
out of order

Transfer information and responsibility

Two short language checks

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

itinerary
A planned sequence of travel activities, locations, and times.
force majeure
A contractual provision addressing specified events beyond a party's control; effects depend on the wording and law.
waiver
A documented relinquishment or exception concerning a right or requirement, subject to applicable terms and law.

A provider responsible for delivering travel or tour services in a destination.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  3. C. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  2. B. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  3. C. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.

Three conversations at work

1. Ask before interpreting a declined service

Fictional staff coaching: a guest declines luggage assistance; a porter attributes the choice to nationality, although the guest gave no reason.

  1. Porter: The guest declined luggage assistance. People from that country probably prefer carrying everything themselves.
  2. Guest services supervisor: We don't know that. What did the guest actually say when you offered the service?
  3. Porter: Only 'No, thank you.' They didn't explain why they preferred not to use it.
  4. Guest services supervisor: Then respect the choice without adding a cultural explanation to the guest profile.
  5. Porter: Should I ask them why they refused, so we can improve our service standard?
  6. Guest services supervisor: There's no need to press for a reason. Ask about future assistance only if it's useful.
  7. Porter: I could say, 'Would you like us to leave you to settle in, or help later?'
  8. Guest services supervisor: Yes, provided it doesn't become repeated pressure after they've clearly declined assistance.
  9. Porter: I'll record only a relevant stated preference, not a guess about nationality or personality.
  10. Guest services supervisor: Good. Our service can respond to the individual without treating an assumption as a cultural expectation.
2. A recovery gesture needs a preference check

Separate fictional case: a hotel may offer flowers or a fruit basket as an approved recovery gesture; neither has been selected for a corporate traveler.

  1. Guest relations coordinator: We can offer flowers or a fruit basket after the room mix-up. Which should we send?
  2. Duty manager: Have we asked the traveler whether they would welcome either recovery gesture in their room?
  3. Guest relations coordinator: No. I assumed flowers would be more appropriate based on the guest's profile photograph.
  4. Duty manager: A photograph doesn't tell us that preference. We should offer the options without making that assumption.
  5. Guest relations coordinator: Could asking spoil the gesture and make the apology feel less personal to them?
  6. Duty manager: It can be considerate. Explain the options briefly and make clear that declining is perfectly acceptable.
  7. Guest relations coordinator: I'll ask, 'Would either option be welcome, or would you prefer no delivery?'
  8. Duty manager: That works. Don't infer dietary preferences from the guest's name when discussing the fruit basket.
  9. Guest relations coordinator: I'll confirm the choice directly and record only what is needed for this delivery.
  10. Duty manager: Then the gesture supports the apology rather than imposing our idea of good service.
3. Formal or first-name introductions

  3. Event host: We have names on the rooming list. Could we choose a style based on their countries?
  4. Front office trainer: That would substitute a national assumption for an individual preference we can ask about respectfully.
  5. Event host: How can the team ask without turning each check-in into an awkward interview?

  9. Event host: I'll brief the team to ask naturally and use the answer consistently during the stay.
  10. Front office trainer: Good. Clarifying a preference is more reliable than trying to predict it from a nationality.
