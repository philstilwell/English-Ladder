# Quoting language in lessons

Use quotation marks when a word, phrase, or example sentence is being discussed
inside an explanation, question, or answer feedback. This distinguishes the
language being taught from the instructions around it.

- What does “about” mean in “about twenty people”?
- Which pair introduces the topic after “talk” and after “discuss”?
- Use “on” with a named day, as in “on Monday”.
- Choose the correction: “We met in Monday.”

Enclose a complete example phrase once. Avoid marking each word separately
inside it. Ordinary prose, standalone answer choices, dialogue lines, vocabulary
headwords, and visually separate example cards do not need extra quotation marks.
For example, the instruction “Use ‘for’ to introduce the matter someone is blamed
for” names the first occurrence of *for*; the second occurrence is ordinary prose.

Curly double quotation marks are the default for new web text. Existing correctly
paired straight or single quotation marks remain valid. Printable guides may use
straight quotes with their embedded fonts. Quotation marks around language
examples do not attribute those examples to a news source. Direct quotations
attributed to a source must remain verbatim; never turn a paraphrase into a quote.

Grammar and workplace lessons store the distinctions in their canonical teaching
text, shared by web pages, feedback, AI prompts, and PDF guides. Daily lessons
use the same instruction in both writing and review. Archived teaching text has
reviewed exact-string display corrections in `content/teaching-typography.json`.
These corrections apply only to explanations, questions, and feedback; they do
not rewrite readings, source evidence, answer choices, or translation records.
Do not add a global word-replacement rule: words such as “in”, “that”, and “it”
also occur as ordinary prose.

Keep the original 44 grammar graphics. Explain qualifications and exceptions in
the text alongside them; do not edit the artwork to make these typography changes.
