Your goal

Name the mismatch between the assignment and the client's expectation without blaming either person.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

You arrive at 9:00 for a two-hour visit. Your assignment lists breakfast and light household tasks. The client expects transport to a 10:00 appointment, but transport is not listed and you have no authorization. The coordinator is available by phone.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

care plan
The current agreed record of a client's needs, support, and authorized arrangements.
assigned visit
A scheduled period of support with duties assigned to a particular worker.
scope of duties
The limits of the tasks a worker is authorized to carry out.
client preference
A person's stated choice about how they want support provided.

Useful word combinations

  • review the care plan: check the current agreed support before starting
  • confirm the visit window: establish the expected arrival period
  • respect a preference: accommodate a choice within the agreed support
  • clarify the assignment: ask exactly which duties are authorized

03 · Notice the language

Check expectations before starting

A care plan records agreed support; a client's immediate request may differ. Acknowledge the need, identify what your current assignment actually says, and ask the person who can clarify it. An omission is not proof that nobody arranged support.

  • My assignment lists ___; were you expecting something else?
  • Could we check whether ___ was arranged separately?
  • I can confirm ___, but I still need to check ___.

Improve this: Nobody booked your ride.

See a clearer version

Transport is not listed in my assignment. I will check whether it was arranged separately.

The revised statement describes the information you have without claiming to know every arrangement.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Coordinator: You are scheduled to support this client from nine to eleven.
Caregiver: Understood. That is my ____ for this morning.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. assigned visit

  1. A. assigned visit Correct. An assigned visit is the client support allocated to this caregiver.
  2. B. referral request A referral request asks for a service; this visit is already assigned.
  3. C. shift handoff A shift handoff transfers information between workers, not the visit itself.
  4. D. on-call availability On-call availability means being available if needed, not this scheduled client visit.
2. Coordinator: Has anyone arranged a driver?
Caregiver: I ____; I have only checked my own assignment.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. have not confirmed that

  1. A. have not confirmed that Correct. This accurately marks what remains unknown.
  2. B. have already cancelled that Cancellation is an action the scenario never establishes.
  3. C. have ruled that out Ruling it out claims you checked all possible arrangements.
  4. D. can guarantee that A guarantee is not supported by your limited information.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. The appointment is not on my assignment

A caregiver arrives for the fictional 9:00 visit and checks an unexpected transport request.

  1. Client: You're here. We need to leave for my clinic appointment in forty minutes.
  2. Caregiver: Your appointment is at ten, correct? I see breakfast and light household tasks on my assignment.
  3. Client: I mentioned the ride when I spoke with someone last week.
  4. Caregiver: You may have discussed a separate arrangement. I don't see transport listed here.
  5. Client: Does that mean nobody is coming to pick me up today?
  6. Caregiver: I haven't confirmed that. May I call the coordinator before we make that assumption?
  7. Client: Yes, please. I don't want to miss another appointment this month.
  8. Caregiver: I'll explain the appointment time and ask what transport, if any, was arranged.
  9. Client: While you check, can I have my breakfast at the table?
  10. Caregiver: Certainly. We'll continue the agreed visit tasks while the coordinator checks the transport details.
2. A changed start time

A caregiver and coordinator reconcile two different visit times before a planned home visit.

  1. Caregiver: My assigned visit starts at nine tomorrow, but the client's reminder says half past nine.
  2. Coordinator: I changed the visit window yesterday. Your printed schedule may be an older version.
  3. Caregiver: Which time should I confirm with the client before I leave today?
  4. Coordinator: Half past nine. The visit is still two hours, ending at eleven thirty.
  5. Caregiver: The client usually expects breakfast earlier. Has that change been discussed with her?
  6. Coordinator: Not yet. I need to check whether the later time works for her.
  7. Caregiver: Then I shouldn't present nine thirty as an agreed change at this point.
  8. Coordinator: Correct. Please tell her I'm checking the revised arrangement and will call shortly.
  9. Caregiver: I'll distinguish the proposed time from the confirmed plan and wait for your update.
  10. Coordinator: Thank you. I'll confirm the final window with both of you before today's visit ends.
3. Whose preference is it?

A client explains how they want ordinary household support organized during the visit.

  1. Caregiver: Your plan includes light housekeeping today: the dishes, laundry, and clearing the table. Which would you prefer first?
  2. Client: My daughter always says to start with the laundry, but I'd rather clear the table.
  3. Caregiver: You'd like the table ready before we deal with the laundry. Have I understood?
  4. Client: Exactly. I enjoy sitting there, and the folded towels aren't urgent today.
  5. Caregiver: That order fits today's assignment. Shall we keep your newspapers in their usual basket?
  6. Client: Please leave the top one out. I'm halfway through an article in it.
  7. Caregiver: I'll leave that newspaper on the side table and put the others in the basket.
  8. Client: Thank you for asking. Sometimes things disappear before I've finished using them.
  9. Caregiver: I'll check before moving personal items. Let me know if you'd prefer another arrangement.
  10. Client: That works. Once the table is clear, we can decide which laundry needs attention.

06 · Say it

Two scenarios to practice

Scenario 1 · Confirming the visit and the care plan

You arrive at 9:00 for a two-hour visit. Your assignment lists breakfast and light household tasks. The client expects transport to a 10:00 appointment, but transport is not listed and you have no authorization. The coordinator is available by phone.

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You are the client. The appointment is at 10:00, and you remember discussing transport last week, but you do not know whether it was booked.

Try a harder second round

The coordinator says a separate transport service may have been contacted but needs five minutes to confirm. Explain the uncertainty and agree what to do while waiting.

Scenario 2 · A request from a visiting relative

During a two-hour visit, a relative asks you to clean the garage. Your assignment covers breakfast and light tasks in the living area. The client wants the remaining time used for laundry. No garage work is authorized.

Role A: Caregiver: acknowledge the relative, explain the assigned duties, and check the client's preference.

Role B: Relative: you thought any household cleaning was included. You can contact the coordinator about future services.

Possible opening: “Could you clean out the garage before you leave today?”

Success checks
  • Identify the actual assignment without judging the relative.
  • Keep the client's stated preference in the discussion.
  • Refer any new service request to the coordinator without accepting a private arrangement.
Add a complication

The relative offers extra cash. Your employer requires service changes to go through the coordinator, not private arrangements.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
Your appointment is at ten, and I understand why you expected a ride. My assignment today lists breakfast and light household tasks, but it does not include transport. May I contact the coordinator now to check what was arranged? I cannot confirm a ride until we have that information.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Notice: Name the mismatch between the assignment and the client's expectation without blaming either person. Identify the wording that does this, then name one detail from the case that the response preserves.

Apply it to this case

In “Confirming the visit and the care plan”, choose one confirmed detail from the situation and one item that still needs clarification. Say a two-sentence response using “My assignment lists ___; were you expecting something else?”. Keep the known detail accurate and ask about the missing one.

Check your reasoning

Compare with the case above and the model response. Can you point to the words that support your factual statement? Is your question about something the case leaves open? If you introduce a possible outcome, clearly label it as a possibility.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

The client declines help arranging clothes before a visitor arrives. The visit has thirty minutes left. Choosing clothes is part of the agreed support, but the client prefers ten quiet minutes first. There is no immediate safety concern.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

consent
Agreement to an action, which must be checked rather than assumed.
personal space
The physical and conversational distance a person wants around them.
reassurance
Language that offers support without promising an outcome you cannot guarantee.
refusal
A person's decision not to accept a proposed action or service.

Useful word combinations

  • ask permission: check that the person agrees before acting
  • offer a choice: present manageable options without pressure
  • respect personal space: avoid moving closer without agreement
  • accept a refusal: acknowledge a no and clarify what happens next

03 · Notice the language

Offer choices without applying pressure

A polite question is not meaningful choice if a refusal is immediately ignored. State available options neutrally, allow an answer, and separate the person's preference from what the schedule allows.

  • Would you prefer ___ or ___?
  • You can say no; I need to explain ___ so you can choose.
  • I'll give you ___, then check whether you would like ___.

Improve this: You need to let me help now or we'll run out of time.

See a clearer version

We have thirty minutes left. Would you prefer a short break before we choose clothes, or shall we leave that task today?

The time limit is clear without making assistance sound compulsory.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: I'd rather choose my clothes after a break.
Caregiver: Would you ____ ten quiet minutes first?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. prefer

  1. A. require me 'Require me' needs a following action such as 'to wait'.
  2. B. insist 'Insist' normally needs 'on' before this noun phrase.
  3. C. prefer Correct. 'Prefer ten quiet minutes' directly asks about the client's choice.
  4. D. permit 'Permit' asks permission for an action, but this phrasing does not identify that action naturally.
2. Client: Not today, thank you.
Caregiver: I understand. I'll ____ and check what other support you would like.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. respect your decision

  1. A. confirm the task is complete Declining a task does not mean the task was completed.
  2. B. take that as agreement A refusal is not agreement.
  3. C. repeat the instruction Repeating an instruction does not acknowledge the stated choice.
  4. D. respect your decision Correct. This recognizes refusal while keeping other support available.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Ten minutes before we start

A client requests quiet time during a scheduled visit with thirty minutes remaining.

  1. Client: I'd rather not sort my clothes right now. I've had people coming in all morning.
  2. Caregiver: You'd like a break from activity. Would ten quiet minutes help before we discuss it again?
  3. Client: Yes, but I don't want you standing beside the chair while I rest.
  4. Caregiver: Of course. I can wait in the kitchen if that suits you and the visit arrangement.
  5. Client: That's fine. Please don't start opening drawers while I'm having my break.
  6. Caregiver: I won't. Your personal space matters. We have thirty minutes left, so we can choose a smaller task afterward.
  7. Client: And if I decide not to do the clothes at all today?
  8. Caregiver: I'll respect that choice. We can confirm what other agreed support you want instead.
  9. Client: Good. Please come back in ten minutes and ask me then.
  10. Caregiver: Ten minutes in the kitchen, then I'll check with you before we do anything else.
2. Let me finish the sentence

A caregiver repairs an overly hurried offer of help with a household task.

  1. Caregiver: Shall I put those books away for you before your visitor arrives this afternoon?
  2. Client: I was going to say that I want the green one left out.
  3. Caregiver: I'm sorry; I started moving them before you finished. I'll stop and listen.
  4. Client: The green book stays here, and the other two belong on the lower shelf.
  5. Caregiver: Green book here, two books on the lower shelf. Is that the arrangement you want?
  6. Client: Yes. I know it seems small, but I like to find things myself.
  7. Caregiver: That makes sense. Would you like me to move those two now, or leave them?
  8. Client: You can move them now. Just keep them where I can see their covers.
  9. Caregiver: I'll place them with their covers visible and ask you to check the position.
  10. Client: Thank you. That's the help I wanted, without everything being reorganized around me.
3. The family member answers first

A caregiver makes room for the client's own preference during a relative's visit.

  1. Relative: She wants you to rearrange the whole cupboard today. We discussed it yesterday evening.
  2. Caregiver: Thank you. I'd like to check her preference directly before we agree on that task.
  3. Relative: She's tired, so I'm trying to save her from having to explain everything.
  4. Caregiver: I appreciate that. We can keep the question short and give her time to answer.
  5. Relative: What if she changes her mind after we have already started moving things?
  6. Caregiver: Then we'll pause and ask what she wants to keep or change before continuing.
  7. Relative: All right. She may only want the cups moved onto the lower shelf.
  8. Caregiver: That would be a more specific request. I'll ask her which items she wants within reach.
  9. Relative: I'll let her answer, and I can explain yesterday's discussion if she asks me.
  10. Caregiver: Thank you. We can support the conversation without replacing her own choice with our assumptions.

03Reporting a change without diagnosingSeparate what you observed from what you inferred · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Report a change using a source, time, and clear limit on your knowledge.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

During a fictional routine visit, the client says, 'I feel more tired than yesterday.' You notice they stop twice while describing breakfast, which is unusual for your visits. You are not qualified to diagnose. No emergency signs are specified; use the local procedure for reporting changes and urgent concerns.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

baseline
A person's usual pattern used as a reference when describing a change.
observation
Something a worker directly sees, hears, or otherwise notices.
reported symptom
A person's own account of how they feel, distinguished from a worker's diagnosis.
escalation
Passing a concern to the appropriate authorized person or urgent response process.

Useful word combinations

  • notice a change: identify something different from the usual pattern
  • report an observation: describe what you directly saw or heard
  • quote the client's words: distinguish their report from your conclusion
  • escalate a concern: pass it to the authorized person or emergency process

03 · Notice the language

Separate what you observed from what you inferred

An observation is something you directly noticed; a reported symptom is the person's account. A baseline is their usual pattern, not a diagnosis. Do not convert one unfamiliar behavior into a clinical conclusion or delay urgent action to finish a language exercise.

  • At ___, I noticed ___; the client said ___.
  • Compared with my usual visits, ___.
  • I cannot determine the cause; I am reporting ___.

Improve this: She's getting ill because she sounds tired.

See a clearer version

She said she feels more tired than yesterday. I noticed two pauses while she described breakfast, and I am reporting the change.

The revision separates the client's statement from the caregiver's observation and removes an unsupported diagnosis.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Supervisor: Did the client say she was tired, or is that your interpretation?
Caregiver: That was her ____: 'I feel more tired than yesterday.'

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. reported symptom

  1. A. reported symptom Correct. The phrase identifies the statement as the client's own account.
  2. B. diagnosis A diagnosis identifies a condition; the client described how she felt.
  3. C. instruction She did not give an instruction.
  4. D. prediction She described a current feeling, not a future event.
2. Supervisor: Do you know what caused the change?
Caregiver: I ____; I can describe what I noticed.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. cannot determine the cause

  1. A. cannot determine the cause Correct. This states a knowledge limit while preserving useful observations.
  2. B. have confirmed the cause The scenario supplies no confirmed cause.
  3. C. can rule out any concern An observation alone cannot rule out every concern.
  4. D. know it will pass This predicts an outcome without evidence.

1. What did you actually notice?

  1. Supervisor: Your message says the client seems different today. What exactly have you noticed during the visit?

Your goal

Respond helpfully to a request that exceeds the caregiver's authorized role.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Your fictional assignment permits reminders only, not administering medicines or recommending doses. The client asks whether to double a missed dose. You do not know the medicine details. The care plan identifies a nurse contact for medication questions.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

medication reminder
An authorized prompt about medicine, distinct from administering it or choosing a dose.
authorized task
A duty the worker is permitted to perform under the applicable assignment and procedures.
clinical advice
Guidance about a health condition or treatment from an appropriately qualified professional.
referral
Directing a question or concern to a person qualified or authorized to handle it.

Useful word combinations

  • stay within your role: perform only duties you are authorized to carry out
  • refer a clinical question: connect the person with an appropriate professional
  • check the current instructions: verify approved guidance rather than rely on memory
  • decline a request respectfully: state a limit and offer a suitable next step

03 · Notice the language

Decline the decision, not the person

A boundary is clearest when it names the specific decision you cannot make and the next person who can help. Do not suggest a dose, interpret a prescription, or let a relative's confidence substitute for authorized guidance.

  • I can help with ___, but I am not authorized to ___.
  • That question needs ___; may we contact them?
  • I don't want to guess about ___. Let's check the approved route.

Improve this: I don't do medicines. Ask somebody else.

See a clearer version

I can help you contact the nurse, but I am not authorized to advise on a missed dose.

The second response keeps the boundary specific and provides an appropriate route to help.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

dietary restriction
A documented limit affecting food choices, which must not be guessed or reinterpreted.
substitution
An item proposed or used in place of another item.
meal preference
A person's choice about what or how they would like to eat.
intake record
An approved record of food or drink consumed, with estimates identified as estimates.

Useful word combinations

  • check a restriction: verify a documented limit before discussing alternatives
  • offer an approved alternative: suggest an option consistent with current instructions
  • record what was consumed: describe the actual amount without guessing
  • distinguish preference from restriction: separate a personal choice from a required limit

03 · Notice the language

Clarify food choices without giving nutrition advice

A preference describes what someone wants; a restriction limits what is appropriate under their plan. The caregiver should not invent a clinical reason for a restriction or promise that a substitute is suitable before it has been checked.

  • The plan lists ___; the requested substitute still needs ___.
  • Is that a preference, or is it part of the documented restriction?
  • I can offer ___ now, or help check ___.

Improve this: You can't have that because it's bad for you.

See a clearer version

That food is not listed among the approved options I have. I need to check before describing it as an approved substitute.

The revision reports the actual information gap without judging the client's choice or inventing a medical explanation.

Vocabulary for this lesson

confidentiality
The obligation to protect private information from inappropriate disclosure.
authorized contact
A person verified as permitted to receive specified information or take part in specified communication.
identity verification
The approved process for establishing who a person contacting the service actually is.

03 · Notice the language

Protect privacy without sounding dismissive

Knowing a client's name or address is not proof of authorization. Family relationships do not tell you exactly what may be shared. Use the organization's verification procedure, avoid confirming private facts unnecessarily, and do not promise access you cannot authorize.

  • I can pass on your request without sharing ___.

Your goal

Separate completed work, unresolved work, and responsibility for the next step.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

At 11:00 you finish a fictional visit. Breakfast support and laundry are complete. A transport query for tomorrow's 10:00 appointment is still pending with the coordinator. The incoming caregiver starts at 15:00. The coordinator, not the incoming caregiver, is arranging transport.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

handoff
The transfer of relevant information and responsibility between workers or teams.
pending action
A task or decision that remains unresolved and needs a next step.
read-back
Repeating key information to confirm that it has been heard and understood correctly.
follow-up owner
The person explicitly responsible for taking an unresolved matter to its next step.

Useful word combinations

  • give a concise handoff: transfer the relevant facts and unfinished work
  • name the follow-up owner: identify who will take the next action
  • read back the key detail: repeat it to check shared understanding
  • close the communication loop: obtain confirmation that the message was received and understood

03 · Notice the language

Transfer a pending action without losing ownership

A handoff is not just a list of events. The receiving person needs to know what matters for their visit, which facts remain uncertain, and who is acting. A read-back catches misunderstandings before 'requested' becomes 'confirmed'.

  • Completed: ___. Still pending: ___.
  • ___ is handling the follow-up; please check ___ by ___.
  • Could you read back the status and the person responsible?

Improve this: Everything's done except the ride. Someone will sort it out.

See a clearer version

Breakfast and laundry are complete. The coordinator is checking transport for tomorrow's ten o'clock appointment; the ride is not yet confirmed.

The revised handoff names the pending action, the owner, and the limit on certainty.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Incoming caregiver: So the ride is booked for tomorrow?
Outgoing caregiver: Not yet. It is ____ with the coordinator.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. pending

  1. A. duplicated No second booking has been established.
  2. B. completed Completed would mean the arrangement was finished.
  3. C. cancelled No cancellation has been reported.
  4. D. pending Correct. Pending means the arrangement is awaiting an outcome.
2. Outgoing caregiver: Who will arrange the transport?
Incoming caregiver: The coordinator is the ____; I will check the update.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. follow-up owner

  1. A. follow-up owner Correct. This identifies responsibility for the unresolved action.
  2. B. appointment provider The appointment provider delivers the appointment service, not necessarily transport coordination.
  3. C. incoming caregiver The incoming caregiver checks the update; the coordinator arranges transport in this case.
  4. D. emergency contact An emergency contact is not automatically the person handling this routine transport query.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Your fictional 9:00 visit will be delayed. At 8:50, dispatch estimates arrival between 9:30 and 9:45 but cannot guarantee it. The client had planned breakfast support at 9:00. Dispatch is checking an approved backup arrangement and will update the client by 9:10.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

visit window
The agreed period during which a scheduled visit is expected to begin.
service disruption
An interruption or change that affects the planned delivery of support.
acknowledgment
Confirmation that a message or concern has been received, not necessarily resolved.
contingency plan
An approved alternative arrangement used when the original plan cannot proceed as expected.

Useful word combinations

  • acknowledge the impact: recognize how a delay affects the person
  • give a realistic update: state what is known without inventing an arrival promise
  • follow the missed-visit procedure: use the organization's established response
  • confirm the next contact: say who will update the person and when

03 · Notice the language

Repair trust with a bounded promise

People need practical information during delays. Separate the estimated arrival from the promised next contact, and explain the escalation route if the update is missed. Apologizing should not lead to an invented guarantee or an unauthorized change to support.

  • I'm sorry this affects ___. What we know now is ___.
  • The current estimate is ___; it is not yet guaranteed.
  • ___ will update you by ___ about ___.

Improve this: Don't worry, someone will definitely be there in ten minutes.

See a clearer version

Dispatch is checking the backup arrangement and will update you by nine ten. The current arrival estimate is nine thirty to nine forty-five.

The revised message distinguishes a confirmed communication commitment from an uncertain arrival estimate.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: Are you guaranteeing that somebody arrives at nine thirty?
Caregiver: No, that is the current ____; dispatch will update you by nine ten.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. estimate

  1. A. confirmation Confirmation would imply the arrival arrangement has been verified.
  2. B. estimate Correct. An estimate describes a current expectation, not a guarantee.
  3. C. completed visit The visit has not yet taken place.
  4. D. standing instruction A standing instruction is an ongoing direction, not this travel prediction.
2. Client: Is the backup confirmed? What will dispatch do by nine ten?
Caregiver: They will ____; the arrangement is still being checked.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. give you a status update

  1. A. confirm that the backup has arrived Arrival has not been confirmed, and the backup is still being checked.
  2. B. give you a status update Correct. A status update can report progress without promising that arrangements are complete.
  3. C. treat the expected time as a firm booking An expected time is not equivalent to a confirmed booking.
  4. D. guarantee an exact arrival time The case promises an update, not an exact arrival guarantee.
