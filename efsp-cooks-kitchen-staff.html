01Clarifying a prep listAsk what the number counts · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Clarify quantities before interpreting a kitchen shorthand.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

The prep list says 'soup: 24' without a unit. Yesterday's sheet used portions; the storeroom label uses containers. The lead intended 24 portions from the current recipe, not 24 containers. No batch has started.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

mise en place
Ingredients and equipment organized for a kitchen's planned work.
prep list
A list of ingredients or components to prepare before or during service.
yield
The usable amount a recipe or ingredient is expected to produce.
batch
A quantity of food prepared as one planned production unit.

Useful word combinations

  • check the required yield: confirm the usable amount needed
  • work from the prep list: use the current task list
  • prepare one batch: make the specified production quantity
  • clarify the unit: distinguish portions, containers, and weights

03 · Notice the language

Ask what the number counts

Numbers need units. A recipe's yield and a container count describe different things; familiarity with yesterday's sheet isn't confirmation for today.

  • Does ___ mean portions or ___?
  • Which recipe version applies to ___?
  • So the required yield is ___, not ___.

Improve this: I'll make twenty-four.

See a clearer version

Do you mean twenty-four portions from the current recipe?

The question attaches a unit and recipe to the number.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Lead: Twenty-four means individual servings, not a weight or production grouping.
Cook: The required yield is twenty-four ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. portions

  1. A. containers Containers hold food but don't necessarily equal individual servings.
  2. B. kilograms Kilograms measure weight, not a number of individual servings.
  3. C. portions Correct. Portions count the individual servings the lead specified.
  4. D. batches Batches are production groups that may contain many servings.
2. Cook: Could you confirm ____ applies today?
Lead: The version dated Monday.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. which recipe

  1. A. which recipe does 'Does' is unnecessary before the verb 'applies'.
  2. B. which does recipe This reverses the subject phrase.
  3. C. which recipe Correct. The noun phrase is the subject of 'applies'.
  4. D. recipe which The question word must precede the noun here.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Twenty-four what?

The ambiguous soup prep line in the case.

  1. Prep cook: The soup line says twenty-four. Does that mean individual portions or filled storage containers?
  2. Lead cook: It means twenty-four portions today. We haven't specified a number of containers on this list.
  3. Prep cook: Thanks. Which recipe version should I use to check the required yield for that quantity?
  4. Lead cook: Use Monday's current recipe. The older sheet in the folder has a different yield.
  5. Prep cook: I'll work from Monday's version, then. Has anyone already started part of this batch?
  6. Lead cook: No, you're taking the whole soup assignment. The other cook is preparing salad components instead.
  7. Prep cook: So twenty-four portions is the total target, not twenty-four more than someone else's preparation?
  8. Lead cook: Correct. That's the total for your assignment, and nothing has been counted toward it yet.
  9. Prep cook: I'll mark the unit on my copy and repeat the target at the prep check.
  10. Lead cook: Good. Bring me any recipe discrepancy before assuming the older figures still apply today.
2. A total, not an addition

A kitchen assistant clarifies a revised list during preparation.

  1. Assistant: The updated list says twelve trays. Are those twelve additional trays or the overall total?
  2. Cook: Twelve is the total needed. Four trays have already been counted as complete for this assignment.
  3. Assistant: Then eight remain, assuming those four belong to the same order on this updated sheet.
  4. Cook: They do. The completed trays carry the same order reference as the revised list.
  5. Assistant: I'll work toward eight more, rather than adding twelve to the four already finished.
  6. Cook: Exactly. Please don't count the separate display tray toward this order; it belongs elsewhere.
  7. Assistant: Thanks for pointing that out. Is the display tray included in any of these figures?
  8. Cook: No, it's listed on the counter-service sheet and isn't part of today's twelve-tray order.
  9. Assistant: I'll keep the two counts separate when I report the remaining work to the lead.
  10. Cook: That will help. Report eight outstanding on this order and leave the display count unchanged.
3. Prep ready for whom?

A cook checks what a colleague means by 'ready'.

  1. Cook: You said my mise en place was ready. Is the garnish at my station yet?
  2. Colleague: It's prepared on the prep bench, but I haven't transferred it to your station yet.
  3. Cook: Thanks. I was looking in the station supplies and thought we had run out.
  4. Colleague: We haven't run out. The completed garnish is assigned to your station on this list.
  5. Cook: Could you confirm the quantity? I need to report our remaining prep before service starts.
  6. Colleague: There are eighteen portions for your station, and another six belong to the lunch counter.
  7. Cook: I'll report eighteen prepared for us, with the transfer still outstanding at this moment.
  8. Colleague: Yes, and please keep the lunch-counter allocation separate so it doesn't get counted twice.
  9. Cook: Who's bringing it over? I can collect it once we've finished this handover.
  10. Colleague: Please take that task. I'll tell the lead it's assigned to you, not already complete.

Scenario 2 · A handwritten abbreviation

A prep note reads '6 c.' The recipe uses cups, but the delivery list uses cases. The lead intended six cups for a practice recipe discussion; no preparation has begun.

Role A: Cook: ask what the abbreviation means.

Role B: Lead: expand it to cups and identify the recipe, not a delivery order.

Possible opening: “Could you spell out the unit beside the six?”

Success checks

  • Obtain the full unit.
  • Repeat the quantity without using the ambiguous abbreviation.
Add a complication

A colleague says the same abbreviation meant cases yesterday.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

The delivery note lists six cases of tomatoes; four are physically present. The driver says two may be on another trolley but has not checked. The receiving lead must resolve the record. Report the observed count without accusing the supplier or recording six as received.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

delivery note
A supplier's document listing items and quantities sent with a delivery.
short delivery
A delivery containing less than the quantity expected.
traceability
The ability to connect a food item to its source and relevant identifying records.
lot code
A label identifier linking a product to a production batch or lot.

Useful word combinations

  • count the delivered cases: check the actual number received
  • flag a missing label: report absent identifying information
  • match the delivery note: compare physical items with the supplier's list
  • report a short delivery: state the difference between expected and received quantities

03 · Notice the language

Report what was actually counted

Use 'lists' for the document and 'counted' for the observation. A possible explanation is not a verified delivery.

05Communicating a shortage and an alternativeOffer a named alternative with a condition · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Explain a shortage and obtain the missing choice.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Three portions of potatoes remain unallocated, but a new ticket requests five. Rice is available as a proposed alternative side; the server must check whether two diners accept it. The cook cannot silently substitute. Agree who obtains the diners' decision.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

eighty-six
Kitchen slang indicating an item is unavailable; usage should be confirmed locally.
par level
A workplace's planned stock quantity used as a replenishment reference.
portion count
The number of servings currently counted for a particular item.
substitute item
An alternative dish or component offered in place of an unavailable one.

Useful word combinations

  • run low on a component: have little of a needed item remaining
  • update the available count: report the current usable allocation
  • offer an alternative side: propose a different accompaniment for approval
  • confirm guest acceptance: check that the diner agreed to the offered change

03 · Notice the language

Offer a named alternative with a condition

An available substitute is not an accepted substitute. Use 'if' to connect the offer to the diner's decision.

  • We have ___ left for ___ requested.
  • We can offer ___ if ___.
  • Please confirm which seats accept ___.

Improve this: I'll change two sides.

See a clearer version

Rice is available for two sides if those diners agree.

The choice stays with the diner instead of being silently imposed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Server: Have the guests accepted rice yet?
Cook: No; the rice is ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. an available alternative

  1. A. the original requested side The original side request was potatoes, not rice.
  2. B. an accepted substitute Acceptance hasn't yet been obtained from those diners.
  3. C. an available alternative Correct. Rice can be offered but isn't yet chosen.
  4. D. a completed change The proposed change has not been completed or accepted.
2. Cook: If both guests ____ to rice, I can confirm the substitution.
Server: I'll ask them now.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. agree

  1. A. agrees A plural subject needs 'agree', not singular 'agrees'.
  2. B. agreeing This participle needs an auxiliary to form the clause.
  3. C. has agreed 'Has' doesn't agree with the plural subject 'both guests'.
  4. D. agree Correct. The plural subject takes 'agree' in this condition.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Three portions for five orders

The potato shortage in the case.

  1. Cook: The potato portion count is three, but this ticket asks for five sides.
  2. Server: Could you put rice on the other two plates and let me explain afterward?
  3. Cook: Please ask first. Rice is available, but we don't know which diners would accept it.
  4. Server: You're right. I'll offer rice to the table and get the actual seat choices.
  5. Cook: Thank you. Keep the three available potatoes separate from any portions already committed elsewhere.
  6. Server: Do you have enough rice for both proposed changes if two diners choose it?
  7. Cook: Yes, two rice sides are available for this proposal. Neither substitution is confirmed yet.
  8. Server: I'll return with the names of the two seats, or tell you if they decline.
  9. Cook: Then I'll read back the five sides against those seats before the ticket proceeds.
  10. Server: Agreed. I'll describe the shortage clearly rather than call rice the standard accompaniment.
2. A stock word needs a scope

A cook clarifies whether a shortage concerns one dish or an ingredient.

  1. Cook: You called the mushrooms eighty-six. Do you mean the mushroom starter or every dish containing mushrooms?
  2. Expediter: The mushroom starter only. The pasta station has its own allocated ingredient stock for service.
  3. Cook: Thanks. I'll tell the servers the starter is unavailable, not remove the pasta from their options.
  4. Expediter: Please use the full dish name. The shortened call caused confusion at the other station too.
  5. Cook: Is the starter unavailable for the rest of this service, or just while another batch is checked?
  6. Expediter: The lead has marked it unavailable for tonight. No further starter batch is planned.
  7. Cook: I'll repeat that as mushroom starter unavailable tonight, with mushroom pasta availability unchanged.
  8. Expediter: Exactly. The separate allocation is why the dish name matters more than the ingredient nickname.
  9. Cook: I'll also check earlier starter orders before discussing replacements.
  10. Expediter: The lead is checking earlier tickets separately; the starter is unavailable for new orders tonight.
3. A par level is not an order

A prep cook questions a replenishment request.

  1. Prep cook: The par level says forty portions, but we have twenty and only ten orders expected.
  2. Lead cook: Are you asking whether to prepare twenty more just to reach the reference level?
  3. Prep cook: Yes. I don't want to treat the printed par figure as a new production instruction.
  4. Lead cook: That's sensible. Today's approved prep target stays at twenty because the service plan is smaller.
  5. Prep cook: Then the existing twenty meet today's target, even though they're below the usual par level.
  6. Lead cook: Correct. The par is a planning reference, not an automatic instruction for every shift.
  7. Prep cook: Should I tell the incoming cook why the board shows a difference from that reference?
  8. Lead cook: Please do. Explain the reduced target so they don't add an unnecessary batch later.
  9. Prep cook: I'll say twenty available against today's target of twenty, with the usual par unchanged at forty.
  10. Lead cook: Exactly. Any further production needs a new decision based on the service update, not the gap alone.

The herb garnish for a soup is unavailable. The soup itself remains available, but any presentation change needs the lead's decision and a server update.

Your goal

Challenge an overbroad conclusion using the stated dietary concern.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Ticket 17 says 'no cheese.' The server then reports that the diner has a milk allergy. The sauce specification has not been checked. The cook must not treat omission of cheese as confirmation that the dish is suitable; the lead can review it with the server.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

recipe specification
The current documented ingredients and requirements for a particular dish.
allergen declaration
Information identifying allergens present in a product's ingredients.
cross-contact concern
A question about unintended allergen transfer during handling or preparation.
dietary modification
A requested change to a dish related to a diner's stated dietary need or choice.

Useful word combinations

  • check the specification: consult the current recipe information
  • refer the suitability question: ask the designated lead to assess the request
  • keep the order pending: leave the decision unresolved until the needed check occurs
  • distinguish omission from suitability: do not equate removing one component with resolving every dietary concern

03 · Notice the language

Name the missing check

A change to one ingredient doesn't establish the entire dish's suitability. Keep reported facts and the pending review explicit.

  • Omitting ___ does not establish ___.
  • The new information is ___.
  • We still need the lead to check ___.

Improve this: No cheese means the issue is settled.

See a clearer version

The cheese omission doesn't answer the milk-allergy question about the sauce.

The revised statement identifies the unresolved component.

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Server: Does removing cheese settle the sauce question?
Cook: No, the sauce is ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. still unverified

  1. A. confirmed free of milk The case gives no confirmed ingredient finding for the sauce.
  2. B. checked and cleared No completed suitability check has been reported here.
  3. C. still unverified Correct. The relevant sauce information remains unchecked.
  4. D. verified against today's recipe The current recipe hasn't yet been checked for this concern.
2. Cook: We are still waiting ____ the current recipe specification.
Lead: I'll get it for you.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. for

  1. A. since 'Since' introduces a starting point, not the awaited document.
  2. B. during 'During' introduces a period, not what someone awaits.
  3. C. by 'By' doesn't connect 'waiting' to its awaited object here.
  4. D. for Correct. 'Wait for' introduces the thing being awaited.

03 · Notice the language

Describe a fault without diagnosing it

An observation supports a report, not a technical diagnosis. Name the device, location, effect, and requested help.

  • The ___ at ___ is ___.
  • I observed ___; the cause is unknown.
  • Could you confirm an available ___?

Improve this: The electrics are broken.

See a clearer version

Scale B's display goes blank; the cause hasn't been established.

The observation is supportable and avoids guessing at a repair.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Lead: What did you observe, without knowing the cause?
Cook: The display ____; I couldn't read the quantity.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. went blank

  1. A. showed an error code No error code was observed or reported in this case.
  2. B. went blank Correct. This describes the visible change without diagnosing its cause.
  3. C. lost its external power supply Loss of the external supply is a possible cause, not an established observation.
  4. D. remained steadily lit A steadily lit display does not describe the reported disappearance.
2. Cook: Could you arrange ____ of scale B?
Lead: I'll contact the equipment lead.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. a review

  1. A. it reviewed This construction would need different wording, such as 'have it reviewed'.
  2. B. review it The bare verb doesn't fit this noun-phrase position.
  3. C. reviewing it This duplicates the object before 'of scale B'.
  4. D. a review Correct. The noun phrase fits before 'of scale B'.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

closing checklist
A list of tasks to confirm at the end of a kitchen shift.
waste log
A record of food discarded and the stated reason or quantity.
carryover
Food or work remaining from an earlier period, with any permitted use subject to workplace requirements.
sign-off
A recorded acknowledgment that a specified check or task has been completed or approved.

Useful word combinations

  • hand over an open task: transfer unfinished work with a named receiver
  • reconcile the portion count: check that records and counted amounts agree
  • record the stated reason: use the observed or confirmed explanation
  • leave a check unsigned: do not claim completion before verification

03 · Notice the language

Separate done, recorded, and approved

A written entry can await review. A task assigned to someone isn't complete until they perform it and report back.

  • ___ is complete; ___ awaits review.
  • Can you take ___ and confirm ___?
  • I'll retain responsibility for ___.

Improve this: It's all in the log.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Incoming cook: Has the lead reviewed the waste entry?
Outgoing cook: It is recorded, but review is ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. pending

  1. A. pending Correct. Pending means awaiting action.
  2. B. discarded The food was discarded; the review wasn't.
  3. C. finished The case says review hasn't happened.
  4. D. duplicated No duplicate review is reported.
2. Outgoing cook: Will you take the shelf check? It hasn't started yet.
Incoming cook: Yes, ____ when it's complete.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. I'll confirm

  1. A. I'll confirm Correct. Future language commits to reporting after the unfinished check.
  2. B. I confirmed Past tense would describe confirmation that already happened.
  3. C. I had confirmed Past perfect places confirmation before another past event.
  4. D. I've confirmed Present perfect claims a confirmation has already happened.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Recorded is not reviewed

The shift-change case with garnish, waste entry, and shelf check.

  1. Outgoing cook: Twelve garnish portions are counted. Two items on the closing checklist are still open.
  2. Incoming cook: Which items are open, and which one are you asking me to take over?
  3. Outgoing cook: The shelf check hasn't been done. Could you complete that check and report its status?
  4. Incoming cook: Yes, I'll take the shelf check. What is the second unfinished item on your list?
  5. Outgoing cook: The waste log lists three damaged tarts; the lead hasn't reviewed that entry yet.
  6. Incoming cook: Will you keep responsibility for that review, or do you want me to ask the lead?
  7. Outgoing cook: I'll keep the review follow-up. You only need to take the shelf check from this handover.
  8. Incoming cook: Understood. Twelve garnish portions counted; shelf check mine; waste review still yours and pending.
  9. Outgoing cook: Exactly. Please don't interpret the recorded waste entry as a completed sign-off by the lead.
  10. Incoming cook: I'll do the shelf check and let you know when it's finished.
2. A waste reason is unknown

A cook checks a proposed explanation for discarded food.

  1. Cook: The log says four portions discarded, but the reason column is blank on this entry.
  2. Lead cook: Do you know why they were discarded, or only how many were recorded here?
  3. Cook: Only the quantity. A colleague suggested it was an order cancellation, but hasn't confirmed that.
  4. Lead cook: Then don't add that explanation as fact. Ask the person who handled the discard.
  5. Cook: I'll keep the reason unresolved while requesting clarification, rather than choose the most likely category.
  6. Lead cook: Good. Is the quantity itself supported by a count, or does that also need checking?
  7. Cook: The colleague confirmed counting four portions. They couldn't explain why the decision was made.
  8. Lead cook: So we have a confirmed count and an unconfirmed reason. Keep those two statuses distinct.
  9. Cook: I'll tell the incoming cook exactly that and identify who is following up on the reason.
  10. Lead cook: Please take that follow-up yourself and bring me the answer for the record review.
3. Carryover is not permission

An incoming cook asks what an unfinished stock note establishes.

  1. Incoming cook: The handover mentions carryover sauce. Does that mean it's approved for use in today's service?
  2. Outgoing cook: No. The note identifies remaining stock, but the lead still needs to confirm its status.
  3. Incoming cook: Thank you. I won't read a remaining quantity as permission to use it.
  4. Outgoing cook: Exactly. The relevant records are with the lead, and I haven't made that decision.
  5. Incoming cook: Do you have a confirmed quantity so I can describe what's waiting for review?
  6. Outgoing cook: Two labeled containers are listed. I haven't converted that container count into a portion yield.
  7. Incoming cook: I'll report two containers with review pending, not a guessed number of available portions.
  8. Outgoing cook: That's accurate. The new prep plan depends on the lead's decision and the verified yield.
  9. Incoming cook: I'll ask for both details before treating the carryover as part of my available stock.
  10. Outgoing cook: Please do. I'll remain available to explain the earlier note if the lead needs context.
