Leaves or leave? Matching subjects and verbs

Choose the correct verb form in present-simple statements by matching it to the subject.

Level ranges are study guidance. Start with the examples and choose your own pace.

ONE TRAIN: IT LEAVES

The train leaves at six. The shop opens early.

Train and shop are singular here. Each can be replaced by it, so leaves and opens have the -s ending.

TWO TRAINS: THEY LEAVE

The trains leave at six. The shops open early.

Trains and shops are plural here. Each can be replaced by they. Use leave and open without the he/she/it ending.

CHECK THE SUBJECT AND THE SPELLING

I work. You work. She works. He watches. They study.

I and you take the base form even when referring to one person. Some he/she/it forms need -es or a spelling change: watches, studies.

Understand

The useful distinction

In present-simple statements, a singular subject that could be replaced by he, she, or it usually takes a verb ending in -s: “The train leaves at six”; “The shop opens early.” This matching is called subject–verb agreement. Use the present simple for habits, general facts, and regular schedules. These examples describe a timetable and a usual opening time.

With a plural subject, use the base form: “The trains leave at six”; “The shops open early.” The base form is the plain verb, such as leave or open. A plural noun may end in -s, but its present-simple verb does not take the he/she/it ending. I, you, we, and they also take the base form: “I leave early”; “You work here.” This is true even when I or you refers to just one person.

Check the spelling: work becomes works; watch becomes watches; study becomes studies. Have becomes has with he, she, or it. Be has its own forms: I am, you/we/they are, and he/she/it is. After a modal verb such as can, use the base form: “She can swim.” This lesson focuses on affirmative statements with ordinary verbs. For do/does questions and don't/doesn't negatives, use the related lessons.
