01Scheduling across calendars and time zonesResolve the time reference before booking · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Confirm whose clock a proposed meeting time refers to.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

A colleague asks for a 3:00 meeting with another office. For this fictional date, 3:00 in the colleague's office equals noon at the other office. The request does not identify whose time is intended. Both calendars are open at that equivalent time; confirm the intended time before sending invitations.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

calendar invitation
An electronic request placing a proposed meeting in participants' calendars.
tentative hold
A temporary reservation of time that has not yet been fully confirmed.
time zone
A region's system of local clock time used when comparing meeting times across locations.
buffer time
Time left between appointments for preparation, travel, or overruns.

Useful word combinations

  • place a tentative hold: reserve a proposed time without calling it confirmed
  • check for a conflict: identify appointments that overlap
  • allow a buffer: leave time between scheduled commitments
  • confirm the time zone: state which local clock time an invitation uses

C Notice the language

Resolve the time reference before booking

A calendar opening is not acceptance. State both local times for the supplied date and distinguish a hold from a confirmed meeting.

  • Do you mean ___ in ___ time?
  • On that date, it will be ___ for ___.
  • I've placed a hold; ___ is still unconfirmed.

Improve this: I'll book three.

See a clearer version

Do you mean three here, which is noon for the other office on that date?

The question identifies the missing time reference.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Colleague: I mean three in our office, which is noon there on this date.
Assistant: I'll show ____ on the invitation.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. both local times

  1. A. three in both offices That would describe different moments under the supplied time difference.
  2. B. noon in both offices That also changes the intended local time for this office.
  3. C. an unspecified afternoon time An unspecified time would leave the original ambiguity unresolved.
  4. D. both local times Correct. Both local times make the intended shared moment clear.
2. Colleague: You reserved the time, but Morgan hasn't replied. Is the meeting confirmed?
Assistant: No, the calendar entry is ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. a tentative hold

  1. A. a confirmed appointment A reserved opening does not by itself confirm the meeting.
  2. B. a declined invitation No reply does not establish that Morgan has declined.
  3. C. a tentative hold Correct. The time is reserved temporarily while acceptance remains outstanding.
  4. D. an accepted invitation Morgan has not replied, so acceptance cannot be recorded.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Whose three o'clock?

An assistant checks a meeting request before sending invitations.

  1. Assistant: You asked for three o'clock. Which office's time zone did you mean for this meeting?
  2. Colleague: Three here, please. I hadn't realized my message left that detail open for you.
  3. Assistant: For this date, that's noon in their office. Both calendars show an opening then.
  4. Colleague: Good. Could you reserve half an hour and include both local times in the invitation?
  5. Assistant: Yes. Is this a confirmed meeting, or should I place a tentative hold while you ask Dana?
  6. Colleague: A tentative hold. Dana still needs to agree, even though the calendar looks free.
  7. Assistant: I'll mark it tentative and wait for your confirmation before describing it as accepted.
  8. Colleague: Please allow fifteen minutes afterward as buffer time before my next call as well.
  9. Assistant: The calendar permits that. I've held thirty minutes plus the separate fifteen-minute buffer.
  10. Colleague: Thanks. I'll ask Dana today and tell you whether to confirm or release the hold.
2. Back-to-back meetings leave no travel time

An assistant flags a scheduling conflict.

  1. Assistant: Your client meeting ends at two, and the next appointment starts across town at two.
  2. Manager: I didn't notice the locations. That would leave no travel time between those appointments.
  3. Assistant: The second host offered two-thirty as an alternative. Would you like me to check that slot?
  4. Manager: Yes, but ask about the meeting length before moving anything already in the calendar.
  5. Assistant: It's a thirty-minute discussion, and their two-thirty opening is still available this afternoon.
  6. Manager: Then move the second appointment to two-thirty and keep the first one unchanged.
  7. Assistant: I'll send the revised invitation and identify the location so nobody expects a video call.
  8. Manager: Please ask the host to acknowledge the change rather than assume they saw the calendar update.
  9. Assistant: They've accepted two-thirty at their office. Your first meeting still ends here at two.
  10. Manager: Good. That leaves the agreed travel buffer, and I can prepare for the correct location.
3. One occurrence, not the whole series

An assistant clarifies a recurring-meeting change.

  1. Assistant: You asked to move next Tuesday's team call. Should the later weekly meetings stay unchanged?
  2. Colleague: Yes. Only next Tuesday needs moving because the visitor presentation overlaps with it.
  3. Assistant: The recurring series is at ten. Do you want that one occurrence at eleven instead?
  4. Colleague: Eleven works, and the usual thirty-minute length can stay the same for that date.
  5. Assistant: I'll change the individual occurrence, not every future invitation in the series.
  6. Colleague: Please make that clear in the update, because people may assume our weekly schedule has changed.
  7. Assistant: The message will name the specific Tuesday date and confirm later weeks remain at ten.
  8. Colleague: Can you also leave the original video link in place if the meeting system allows it?
  9. Assistant: The updated invitation retains that link. I've checked the revised date and time before sending.
  10. Colleague: I've received it. Only next Tuesday now shows eleven; the following week still shows ten.

Scenario 2 · An all-day hold hides an available hour

A calendar shows an all-day tentative planning hold. The owner can release 1:00-2:00 for a supplier call but wants the rest kept. The supplier has not accepted.

Role A: Assistant: clarify the partial release and keep supplier acceptance pending.

Role B: Calendar owner: authorize only the one-hour release.

Possible opening: “Can you clear that planning hold so I can speak to the supplier?”

Success checks
  • Name the released hour.
  • Keep the rest of the hold intact.
  • Distinguish available time from supplier acceptance.
Add a complication

A colleague assumes the entire day is now free.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
When you say three o'clock, do you mean three in your office's time zone? On this date, that would be noon for the other office. Both calendars are open then, but I haven't sent an invitation. Please confirm the intended local time so I can label the meeting accurately.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Check your reasoning

Review, then try again

  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Your goal

Use the appointment reference to identify the right receiving colleague.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

Visitor Taylor arrives for a 10:00 meeting with Jordan. The office has two Jordans: Jordan Chen in facilities and Jordan Patel in sales. Reference V12 names Jordan Chen. Check the reference, contact the host, and explain the reception wait; the visitor is not cleared to enter other areas alone.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

visitor log
A record of visitors' arrival and departure details kept under the organization's stated arrangements.
host
The employee responsible for receiving a particular visitor.
visitor badge
An identification pass issued for a visitor's permitted access.
appointment reference
A code or identifier linking a visitor to a particular scheduled visit.

Useful word combinations

  • confirm the host: identify the employee expecting the visitor
  • check the appointment reference: match the visit to its recorded booking
  • issue the visitor badge: provide the appropriate pass after the required check
  • wait in reception: remain in the visitor area until the receiving arrangements are confirmed

C Notice the language

Check identity of the host without unnecessary questions

A shared first name is not enough to route a visitor. Ask for the relevant reference rather than private details unrelated to the visit.

  • Could I check your appointment reference?
  • That lists ___ in ___.
  • Please wait here while I contact ___.

Improve this: Jordan is upstairs somewhere.

See a clearer version

V12 names Jordan Chen in facilities; I'll contact that host before directing you further.

The visitor gets a confirmed receiving person and a clear waiting location.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Visitor: I'm here for Jordan, reference V12.
Assistant: Your host is ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Jordan Chen in facilities

  1. A. either Jordan available first Availability does not change the named host for this visit.
  2. B. the colleague with the nearest desk Desk location does not identify the person responsible for V12.
  3. C. Jordan Chen in facilities Correct. V12 identifies Jordan Chen in facilities.
  4. D. Jordan Patel in sales That is the other Jordan, not the V12 host.
2. Visitor: This pass shows my name, today's date, and the word Visitor. Is it for me or my car?
Assistant: It's your ____ for this visit.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. visitor badge

  1. A. delivery authorization Delivery authorization concerns permission for goods, not this named visitor's identification.
  2. B. visitor badge Correct. The visitor badge identifies the guest under the visit arrangements.
  3. C. employee access card An employee access card is not the guest's identification pass.
  4. D. temporary parking permit A parking permit concerns a vehicle, not the visitor's identification.

Answer: B. subject to a ten-dollar deduction

  1. A. after every missed train The terms do not promise refunds after missed departures.
  2. B. subject to a ten-dollar deduction Correct. That condition matches the supplied fare terms.
  3. C. without any deduction The quote explicitly includes a ten-dollar deduction.
  4. D. only after departure The supplied refund condition applies before departure.
2. Traveler: Have you bought either ticket?
Assistant: No, I have compared the quotes but ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. haven't made a booking

  1. A. haven't made a booking Correct. Comparing quotes has not created a booking.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

deadline
The latest agreed time by which a task or response must be completed.
dependency
Something another task needs before it can proceed, such as a confirmed attendee list.
handover
An exchange passing current task status and outstanding work to another person.
coverage
An arrangement for another worker to handle specified duties during someone's absence.

Useful word combinations

  • agree the priority order: decide which task should come first
  • flag a dependency: identify the missing input holding up a task
  • hand over an open item: transfer an unfinished task with its current status
  • confirm coverage: check who will handle named duties during an absence

1. Manager: At ten, do my twenty-minute check first, then the thirty-minute visitor list due at ten-thirty.
Assistant: That means ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. the visitor list will miss its current deadline

  1. A. the visitor list will miss its current deadline Correct. The check plus list need fifty minutes, beyond the ten-thirty deadline.
  2. B. the visitor list will still meet its current deadline Only ten minutes would remain for the thirty-minute list after the check.
  3. C. the room pack can start earlier than planned Adding work before the list delays rather than advances the room pack.
  4. D. the spreadsheet check will follow the visitor list The manager explicitly asked for the spreadsheet check first.
2. Colleague: The room pack needs the final attendee list first.
Assistant: That list is a ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. dependency

  1. A. completed handover A required input is not itself a completed transfer of responsibility.
  2. B. replacement deadline The list is information, not a new completion time.
  3. C. coverage agreement Coverage names who handles duties, not the input required here.
  4. D. dependency Correct. The pack depends on receiving that input.

Lesson 01: Scheduling across calendars and time zones
Original fictional case: A colleague asks for a 3:00 meeting with another office. For this fictional date, 3:00 in the colleague's office equals noon at the other office. The request does not identify whose time is intended. Both calendars are open at that equivalent time; confirm the intended time before sending invitations.
Language workshop: Resolve the time reference before booking
Communication goal: Confirm whose clock a proposed meeting time refers to.
Language guidance: A calendar opening is not acceptance. State both local times for the supplied date and distinguish a hold from a confirmed meeting.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): Do you mean ___ in ___ time? / On that date, it will be ___ for ___. / I've placed a hold; ___ is still unconfirmed.
Vocabulary: calendar invitation: An electronic request placing a proposed meeting in participants' calendars. / tentative hold: A temporary reservation of time that has not yet been fully confirmed. / time zone: A region's system of local clock time used when comparing meeting times across locations. / buffer time: Time left between appointments for preparation, travel, or overruns.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Colleague: mean three in your own office; can explain the supplied noon equivalent when asked.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: One participant calls the time lunchtime. Check their local time rather than treating the phrase as a new booking request.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: When you say three o'clock, do you mean three in your office's time zone? On this date, that would be noon for the other office. Both calendars are open then, but I haven't sent an invitation. Please confirm the intended local time so I can label the meeting accurately.

TASK: Interactive workplace role-play
1. Use the supplied case or dialogue as the starting situation. Give a two-sentence briefing and offer two relevant professional roles for me to choose from. For a supplied dialogue, use its actual roles. Ask which role I want, then stop and wait. Do not write my replies.
2. After I choose, identify who you will play and the immediate communication goal. Play the other professional; where a meeting requires a third person, label each of your speakers clearly. Start with one natural workplace turn and wait for my reply. Keep most turns to one to three sentences and ask no more than one question at a time.
3. Let the exchange develop across six to ten learner turns, or end earlier if I type "feedback". Respond to what I actually say. Include a plausible clarification, disagreement or tradeoff without silently changing the starting facts. Mark any added constraint as a fictional second-round variation. Do not resolve approvals, evidence or commitments that remain uncertain.
4. Stay in role during the exchange. If my meaning is unclear, ask for clarification naturally. Give a hint only if I ask or cannot proceed. Do not deliver a model conversation in advance.
5. At the debrief, quote two of my phrases: one successful choice and one worth improving. Check factual accuracy, language, register and whether the next step was clear. Give up to three focused suggestions. Ask me to retry the weakest turn; wait. Only then offer an alternative wording and a harder replay.
Use voice replies when available. If I prefer selections, offer four plausible spoken replies and explain my choice before continuing. Do not require written compositions.
