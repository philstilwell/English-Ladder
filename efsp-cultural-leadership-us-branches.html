01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

In a branch meeting, two colleagues say the launch plan is "fine." One later emails a concern about staffing. The manager had recorded agreement.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

assumption
Something treated as true for planning or reasoning, though it may need verification.
clarification
A question or explanation that makes meaning more precise.
expectation
A stated or assumed understanding of what should happen or be delivered.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Reopening the launch agreement

The branch manager follows up on the lesson's launch meeting after receiving a staffing concern.

  1. Branch manager: I recorded agreement when you said the plan was fine. Your email suggests a staffing concern.
  2. Operations lead: I meant the launch sequence looked reasonable, not that we had enough people.
  3. Branch manager: Thank you for the clarification. Which part of my recorded expectation needs correcting?
  4. Operations lead: Please separate support for the sequence from confirmation that staffing is sufficient.
  5. Branch manager: Was your staffing concern already resolved when we finished the meeting?
  6. Operations lead: No. My email raises it because we still need to discuss coverage.
  7. Branch manager: Then my assumption was too broad. I should not have recorded full agreement.
  8. Operations lead: Could we reopen that point before presenting the plan as settled?
  9. Branch manager: Yes. I'll correct the record and invite both colleagues to clarify their positions.
  10. Operations lead: That works. We can discuss coverage without implying that either colleague approved it.
2. Available does not mean assigned

A separate fictional branch case: a training coordinator has two free hours Thursday, but no assignment has been agreed.

  1. Branch manager: You said you were available Thursday. May I announce you as the workshop facilitator?
  2. Training coordinator: Could you clarify the assignment first? I have two hours free, not an unlimited opening.
  3. Branch manager: I was assuming availability meant a commitment to lead the whole session.
  4. Training coordinator: That wasn't my intention. I need the session length and preparation expectation.
  5. Branch manager: We haven't settled either detail. What can I accurately tell the planning group?
  6. Training coordinator: Say that I have a possible two-hour slot, subject to reviewing the work.
  7. Branch manager: So you haven't accepted facilitation or preparation duties at this stage?
  8. Training coordinator: Correct. Please ask the group to define those duties before requesting confirmation.
  9. Branch manager: I'll bring back a specific request instead of adding your name to the agenda.
  10. Training coordinator: Thanks. Once the scope is clear, I can give you a definite answer.
3. Whose end of day?

A separate fictional handoff connects offices using Eastern and Pacific time. A request says only 'end of day.'

  1. Branch coordinator: Your request says end of day. Which time zone should we use for this handoff?
  2. Headquarters analyst: I meant Eastern time, but I didn't include a specific hour.
  3. Branch coordinator: Our team uses Pacific time. That leaves room for a different assumption.
  4. Headquarters analyst: You're right. I also need to clarify whether I want preliminary or reviewed numbers.
  5. Branch coordinator: Which version does your next meeting actually require us to supply?
  6. Headquarters analyst: I need reviewed numbers, but I haven't checked when your review finishes.
  7. Branch coordinator: Then let's establish availability before turning that expectation into a deadline.
  8. Headquarters analyst: Could you check the review schedule while I confirm the meeting's cutoff?
  9. Branch coordinator: Yes. We'll agree an exact time and zone after those two checks.
  10. Headquarters analyst: I'll leave the delivery time unconfirmed until we have that clarification.

06 · Say it

Two scenarios to practice

Scenario 1 · Check meaning before assuming agreement

In a branch meeting, two colleagues say the launch plan is "fine." One later emails a concern about staffing. The manager had recorded agreement.

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You need to know exactly what information is missing. Give one answer only after your partner asks a focused question; then ask them to confirm their understanding.

Try a harder second round

The other person uses an ambiguous word such as 'ready', 'approved', or 'urgent'. Clarify it before continuing.

Scenario 2 · A calendar acceptance with a condition

In a fictional branch, an analyst accepted a meeting invitation but added a note that they could attend only the first fifteen minutes. The manager has scheduled that analyst's item last in a thirty-minute meeting.

Role A: You are the analyst. Clarify what your acceptance meant and request a workable speaking slot.

Role B: You are the manager. Check the time limit and discuss moving the item without assuming the agenda change is already agreed.

Possible opening: “I accepted the invitation for the first fifteen minutes, but my item is currently last.”

Success checks
  • Distinguish accepting an invitation from full-meeting availability.
  • State the fifteen-minute limit accurately.
  • Agree a feasible speaking slot or an explicit follow-up.
Add a complication

Another participant needs the first ten minutes for a separate decision; negotiate the remaining time.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
When you said the plan was fine, did you mean you supported the date or still had concerns? Let's check each person's view before I record a decision.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Notice: Clarify missing information before responding. Identify the wording that does this, then name one detail from the case that the response preserves.

Apply it to this case

In “Check meaning before assuming agreement”, choose one confirmed detail from the situation and one item that still needs clarification. Draft a two-sentence response using “When you say ..., do you mean ...?”. Keep the known detail accurate and ask about the missing one.

Check your reasoning

Compare with the case above and the model response. Can you point to the words that support your factual statement? Is your question about something the case leaves open? If you introduce a possible outcome, clearly label it as a possibility.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

Vocabulary for this lesson

objection
A stated concern or reason for not accepting a proposal.
behavioral example
A specific description of what a person did or said.
boundary
A limit on acceptable conduct, responsibility, access, or scope.

03 · Notice the language

Disagree with a useful reason

Acknowledge the goal without pretending agreement. Name the specific problem and its implication, then ask a focused question or propose a next step. Be direct when a safety or ethical boundary requires it.

  • I understand the goal. My concern is ... .
  • That statement goes beyond ... .
  • Could we ... before confirming ...?

Improve this: That idea is ridiculous.

See a clearer version

My concern is that the proposal assumes capacity we have not confirmed.

The revised sentence identifies a testable concern about the proposal. It leaves room for a constructive response.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which response identifies a specific assumption and evidence against it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open.

  1. A. The proposal appears optimistic, and I would prefer a slower approach. This expresses a preference, but does not identify the assumption or the record that contradicts it.
  2. B. I would like another discussion before we proceed with the proposal. This requests discussion without stating the specific assumption or evidence.
  3. C. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open. It connects the concern to a specific assumption and record.
2. Which follow-up makes disagreement actionable?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan?

  1. A. Could we ask for general feedback before sending the next update? General feedback does not directly address the missing approval.
  2. B. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan? It names the unresolved condition and a practical next step.
  3. C. Could we confirm the plan and discuss the missing approval later? This confirms the plan before resolving the stated approval condition.

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Turning a dismissal into an objection

A separate fictional branch proposal allocates one employee to two simultaneous reception desks. The facilitator addresses a department lead's broad dismissal of that proposal.

  1. Meeting facilitator: You said this proposal makes no sense. Which specific assumption are you objecting to?
  2. Department lead: It assigns one employee to two reception desks operating at the same time.
  3. Meeting facilitator: That's a concrete concern. Please explain the coverage problem without dismissing the people who drafted it.
  4. Department lead: One person cannot cover both desks simultaneously. Are you asking me to soften that objection?
  5. Meeting facilitator: No. Keep the objection direct; the boundary concerns personal criticism, not disagreement about staffing.
  6. Department lead: Then we need either another employee or different operating times for the desks.
  7. Meeting facilitator: Additional staffing isn't confirmed. Could we assess staggered hours before assuming that resource is available?
  8. Department lead: Yes, but we'd need to check whether staggered hours meet the reception requirements.
  9. Meeting facilitator: I'll ask the proposal owner to assess those options with the requirement still visible.
  10. Department lead: Please record simultaneous coverage as unresolved, not as an approved staffing arrangement.
2. Protecting a review step

A separate fictional branch plans a customer mailing. The proposed schedule omits a review step agreed by this project team.

  1. Campaign lead: Let's remove the review meeting so the mailing can go out sooner.
  2. Branch manager: I understand the timing goal. My objection is that our agreed review would disappear.
  3. Campaign lead: Do you think the team is incapable of producing a reliable mailing?
  4. Branch manager: I'm not questioning anyone's ability. I'm pointing to a specific missing step.
  5. Campaign lead: Could we review only the changed sections instead of meeting about every page?
  6. Branch manager: That may work, provided the reviewer agrees the narrower scope covers the changes.
  7. Campaign lead: So your boundary concerns releasing unreviewed changes, not keeping a meeting for its own sake?
  8. Branch manager: Yes. Let's ask the reviewer what they need before confirming a shorter process.
  9. Campaign lead: I'll bring the changed sections and request a focused review proposal.
  10. Branch manager: Then we can discuss the date with that condition visible, rather than assume approval.
3. Responding to an interruption complaint

A separate fictional branch plan schedules training Monday, but its required practice accounts will be available Wednesday. The project lead interrupted the scheduler twice while this dependency was being explained.

  1. Scheduler: You interrupted me twice while I explained the dependency. Could I finish the timing concern?
  2. Project lead: Yes. I thought the dates were settled, but please explain what the proposal overlooks.
  3. Scheduler: Training is Monday, while the required practice accounts won't be available until Wednesday.
  4. Project lead: Could the trainer demonstrate the process Monday and let participants practice afterward?
  5. Scheduler: That changes the session scope. The planned Monday session requires participants to use those accounts.
  6. Project lead: Then my proposal assumes access before it's available. We should assess moving the session.
  7. Scheduler: Yes. Please let me complete these explanations before answering; otherwise the dependency gets lost.
  8. Project lead: That's a fair boundary. Could you check a later session with the trainer?
  9. Scheduler: I'll ask, but I can't confirm their availability or a revised date yet.
  10. Project lead: Understood. We'll record the Monday conflict and keep the replacement date pending that check.

03Make authority and participation clearHelp a group reach a clear next step · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Include relevant voices and clarify the decision process.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Your team wants more direction, but the manager has been answering every question. A new hire has stopped offering ideas.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

decision rights
The allocation of authority to recommend, approve, or make particular decisions.
role clarity
A shared understanding of a person's responsibilities and authority.
facilitation
Guiding a discussion so people can contribute, understand differences, and reach useful next steps.

03 · Notice the language

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Improve this: Nobody objected, so everyone agrees.

See a clearer version

Before I record agreement, does anyone have a concern or need more time?

The revised question gives people a way to raise a concern. Silence can have several meanings, especially in unfamiliar or unequal-power situations.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
  2. B. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
  3. C. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.
  2. B. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.
  3. C. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.

Your goal

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

commitment
An explicit promise or accepted responsibility to do something.
tradeoff
A choice that improves one objective while giving up something on another.
deadline
The latest agreed or required time for completing an action.

03 · Notice the language

Offer a workable tradeoff

Acknowledge the goal and explain the available choices. Connect an offer to its actual conditions. A useful response makes room for discussion without promising approval or resources you do not control.

  • We can ..., provided that ... .
  • Would you prefer ... or ...?
  • I can take that proposal for review; I cannot yet confirm ... .

Improve this: If you will approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

See a clearer version

If you approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

For an ordinary future condition, the “if” clause uses present tense. The other clause can express the future consequence or possibility.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence presents a normal future condition clearly?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule.

  1. A. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule. The “if” clause uses present tense; the main clause states the future response.
  2. B. If the scope will change, we will review the schedule. For this ordinary future condition, use present simple in the “if” clause.
  3. C. If the scope changes, we reviewed the schedule. “Reviewed” places the response in the past instead of expressing the future response.
2. Which response negotiates a new request without silently promising extra work?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

  1. A. We have included it, although its scope has not been reviewed. It treats unreviewed work as already included.
  2. B. We guarantee the original date without assessing the addition. It promises an outcome without examining the consequences.
  3. C. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope. This offers a useful next step while keeping the approval and scope question open.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Three people speak at once during a hybrid meeting. A remote colleague's question is missed, and nobody knows who can approve the plan.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

facilitation
Guiding a discussion so people can contribute, understand differences, and reach useful next steps.
meeting minutes
A written record of key discussion points, decisions, and agreed actions.
action item
A specific task agreed during a discussion, usually assigned to a person.

03 · Notice the language

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Improve this: Nobody objected, so everyone agrees.

See a clearer version

Before I record agreement, does anyone have a concern or need more time?

The revised question gives people a way to raise a concern. Silence can have several meanings, especially in unfamiliar or unequal-power situations.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
  2. B. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
  3. C. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.
  2. B. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.
  3. C. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.

Your goal

Describe observed behavior, its effect, and a useful next step.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Two weekly reports arrived a day after the agreed deadline. The manager does not know the reason. Other teams depend on the figures.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

feedback
Information about performance intended to support understanding and improvement.
expectation
A stated or assumed understanding of what should happen or be delivered.
support plan
Agreed actions and resources intended to help a person or team meet an identified need.

03 · Notice the language

Give feedback people can use

Use a specific example instead of a personality label. Explain its effect, invite the person's perspective, and agree on a practical improvement. Focus on communication that can be changed, not accent or national identity.

  • In [specific situation], I noticed ... .
  • The effect was ... . What was happening from your perspective?
  • Could we agree to ... and review it ...?

Improve this: You are always careless.

See a clearer version

The last two reports omitted the agreed summary section.

The revision identifies observable work. 'Always' and a personality judgment make the feedback harder to verify or act on.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which feedback begins with observable behavior?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The last two updates omitted the agreed completion date.

  1. A. I think the team needs to be more committed to the updates. Commitment is an interpretation, not a description of what the updates contained.
  2. B. The last two updates omitted the agreed completion date. The recipient can check the specific examples and discuss how to improve them.
  3. C. The last two updates seemed less professional than usual. “Less professional” is an evaluation; it does not identify a specific observable omission.
2. Which follow-up invites useful information before agreeing on support?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. What is making this part of the task difficult?

  1. A. What is making this part of the task difficult? An open question may reveal a barrier the speaker has not considered.
  2. B. Can you promise this will never happen again? A promise does not reveal the obstacle or what support would help.
  3. C. Would another reminder solve this problem? This proposes a solution before finding out what is making the task difficult.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
  2. B. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
  3. C. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.
  2. B. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.
  3. C. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Detail versus decision information

Headquarters and the branch discuss the lesson's daily report, which takes the branch an hour to prepare.

  1. Headquarters coordinator: We ask for daily detail, but I hear the report takes your branch an hour.
  2. Branch manager: Yes. Could we distinguish information you need to act from information that's merely available?
  3. Headquarters coordinator: That's useful. We haven't explained which decisions the daily detail supports.
  4. Branch manager: Without that explanation, we can't judge which parts of the workload add value.
  5. Headquarters coordinator: Would an operating agreement simply mean another reporting template for your team?
  6. Branch manager: Here, it would mean agreeing what we share, why, how often, and who responds.
  7. Headquarters coordinator: So the template would follow the information need, rather than define it?
  8. Branch manager: Exactly. Stakeholder engagement starts with asking users of the report what they actually require.
  9. Headquarters coordinator: I'll bring those decision needs to a discussion before requesting further detail.
  10. Branch manager: Then we can assess the workload and negotiate a reporting rhythm on that basis.
2. An exception report is not a complete log

A separate fictional branch proposes reporting delayed deliveries only; headquarters currently receives a log of every delivery.

  1. Branch analyst: Could we discuss an exception report instead of sending the full delivery log each day?
  2. Regional planner: Does exception report mean you would stop recording deliveries that arrive on time?
  3. Branch analyst: No. We would retain the complete log but highlight delays in the shared update.
  4. Regional planner: Then the distinction is between what you record and what you routinely send us?
  5. Branch analyst: Correct. The operating agreement would need to define which exceptions require attention.
  6. Regional planner: I sometimes need the full log for planning. Would that remain accessible?
  7. Branch analyst: That access needs agreement. I'm not proposing to remove it without discussing your needs.
  8. Regional planner: Let's identify when I need full detail and when a delay summary is sufficient.
  9. Branch analyst: We can then estimate the workload instead of assuming the shorter report saves time.
  10. Regional planner: Good. Bring both information needs into the proposal, including how I request the full log.

  1. Project sponsor: The service desk receives every announcement, so stakeholder engagement is already covered.
  2. Branch operations lead: Receiving information isn't the same as being consulted about the work it creates.

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry the process took longer than expected. This acknowledges duration, but does not take responsibility for the unclear wording.
  2. B. I'm sorry we could not meet the original date. This acknowledges a missed date, not the communication problem in the question.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.

Vocabulary:
- assumption: Something treated as true for planning or reasoning, though it may need verification.
- clarification: A question or explanation that makes meaning more precise.
- expectation: A stated or assumed understanding of what should happen or be delivered.
- objection: A stated concern or reason for not accepting a proposal.
- behavioral example: A specific description of what a person did or said.
- boundary: A limit on acceptable conduct, responsibility, access, or scope.
- decision rights: The allocation of authority to recommend, approve, or make particular decisions.
- role clarity: A shared understanding of a person's responsibilities and authority.
- facilitation: Guiding a discussion so people can contribute, understand differences, and reach useful next steps.
- commitment: An explicit promise or accepted responsibility to do something.
- tradeoff: A choice that improves one objective while giving up something on another.
- deadline: The latest agreed or required time for completing an action.
- meeting minutes: A written record of key discussion points, decisions, and agreed actions.
- action item: A specific task agreed during a discussion, usually assigned to a person.
- feedback: Information about performance intended to support understanding and improvement.
- support plan: Agreed actions and resources intended to help a person or team meet an identified need.
- operating agreement: An agreed description of how people will coordinate work and responsibilities.
- workload: The amount and type of work assigned or required over a period.
- stakeholder engagement: The involvement and interaction of people affected by an activity or decision.
- acknowledgment: Recognition of another person's contribution or confirmation that a message was received.
- accountability: Responsibility for explaining decisions and following through on agreed work.
- trust: Confidence that someone will act reliably and respectfully.
