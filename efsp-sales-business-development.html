Your goal

Clarify missing information before responding.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A prospect asks for a demonstration but has not explained the current workflow, problem, or buying process.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

The process of learning needs or facts; in litigation, formal exchange of relevant information.

A specific difficulty experienced by a customer or user.

The steps an organization follows to evaluate, approve, and make a purchase.

03 · Notice the language

Ask a precise question

Name the exact word, fact, or requirement you need clarified. Ask one focused question at a time. Repeat your understanding and give the other person a chance to correct it.

  • When you say ..., do you mean ...?
  • Could you clarify which ...?
  • So, my understanding is ... . Is that right?

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  3. C. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  2. B. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  3. C. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. A demonstration needs a workflow

The lesson's prospect requests a demonstration without explaining the workflow, problem, or buying process.

  1. Account executive: Before arranging the demonstration, could you describe the workflow you want us to focus on?
  2. Prospect operations lead: We haven't shared that yet. I don't want a general tour that misses our actual problem.
  3. Account executive: Which step currently causes the most difficulty, and who experiences that pain point?
  4. Prospect operations lead: I need to consult the users before answering precisely. My initial request was exploratory.
  5. Account executive: That's useful to know. Discovery can clarify the need before we assume a particular use case fits.
  6. Prospect operations lead: Would you also need to know our buying process before showing us anything?
  7. Account executive: We can hold an exploratory discussion, but we shouldn't mistake interest in a demonstration for a qualified purchase.
  8. Prospect operations lead: Understood. I'll identify the workflow owner and ask what they want to examine.
  9. Account executive: Thank you. We can then agree a focused demonstration and clarify the decision process at an appropriate stage.
  10. Prospect operations lead: Let's start there, without treating the meeting request as a commitment to buy.
2. A replacement request with an unclear trigger

Separate fictional case: a prospect asks about replacing a scheduling system, but has not said whether the concern is cost, usability, or a contract event.

  1. Business development representative: You mentioned replacing your scheduling system. Could you clarify what prompted you to explore alternatives now?
  2. Prospect service manager: Different teams have concerns, but we haven't agreed which issue is the main reason for considering a change.
  3. Business development representative: Then I shouldn't assume the pain point is pricing simply because you asked for a quote.
  4. Prospect service manager: Correct. The quote helps us explore, but it won't define what a replacement needs to improve.
  5. Business development representative: Who could explain a typical scheduling problem and the effect it has on the service team?
  6. Prospect service manager: I'll involve the scheduling supervisor. They can describe actual examples rather than general dissatisfaction.
  7. Business development representative: Good. Could we also clarify whether there is a buying deadline, rather than infer one from this inquiry?
  8. Prospect service manager: There isn't a confirmed date in the information I've shared. I'll check the internal planning process.
  9. Business development representative: I'll record that as unknown and keep the next discussion focused on requirements and qualification.
  10. Prospect service manager: That works. We need clarity before we compare replacements or imply that procurement has begun.
3. A trial request from outside the user team

Separate fictional case: an innovation manager requests a trial for a warehouse workflow, but the warehouse owner has not participated in discovery.

  1. Account executive: Could you explain which warehouse task the proposed trial should test and who owns that workflow?
  2. Prospect innovation manager: I requested the trial, but the warehouse owner hasn't joined the discussion yet. The task is still broad.
  3. Account executive: Then we should involve that owner before treating the trial as evidence of an agreed operational need.
  4. Prospect innovation manager: Could we run it with my team first to demonstrate momentum, even without their input?
  5. Account executive: We could discuss a limited exploration, but it wouldn't validate warehouse fit unless the relevant workflow is represented.
  6. Prospect innovation manager: I understand. Interest from innovation isn't the same as qualification with the people who would use it.
  7. Account executive: Exactly. Could you arrange a conversation about the current process, pain point, and intended decision?
  8. Prospect innovation manager: I'll ask the warehouse owner to participate and confirm capacity to assess a trial.
  9. Account executive: Thank you. We can then discuss scope without assuming a purchase decision or implementation commitment.
  10. Prospect innovation manager: I'll present the next meeting as discovery, not as an approved trial launch.

Two scenarios to practice

Scenario 1 · Discovery and Qualification

A prospect asks for a demonstration but has not explained the current workflow, problem, or buying process.

Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.

Partner's role and follow-up

You need to know exactly what information is missing. Give one answer only after your partner asks a focused question; then ask them to confirm their understanding.

Try a harder second round

The other person uses an ambiguous word such as 'ready', 'approved', or 'urgent'. Clarify it before continuing.

Scenario 2 · A quote request from an unknown buying role

A fictional prospect's analyst requests pricing for fifty users but has not explained the workflow, user group, or purchasing role. Hold a short discovery conversation that clarifies purpose without treating the analyst as the budget owner.

Role A: You are the account executive. Ask what the pricing will support and which workflow is in scope.

Role B: You are the prospect's analyst. Explain that you are gathering information and must confirm the user group internally.

Possible opening: “Could you clarify what decision the fifty-user estimate will support and which team would use the service?”

Success checks
  • Distinguish an exploratory estimate from a qualified purchase.
  • Clarify workflow and the contact's role without assumptions.
  • Agree a focused next discovery step with unknowns recorded.
Add a complication

Your sales colleague wants to mark the opportunity qualified because a user count was supplied.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
What are you trying to improve in the current workflow? Could we understand the problem and who will evaluate the solution? That will help us make the demonstration relevant to your decision.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Notice: Clarify missing information before responding. Identify the wording that does this, then name one detail from the case that the response preserves.

Apply it to this case

In “Discovery and Qualification”, choose one confirmed detail from the situation and one item that still needs clarification. Draft a two-sentence response using “When you say ..., do you mean ...?”. Keep the known detail accurate and ask about the missing one.

Check your reasoning

Compare with the case above and the model response. Can you point to the words that support your factual statement? Is your question about something the case leaves open? If you introduce a possible outcome, clearly label it as a possibility.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

03Objection Handling and Competitive PressureExplain a fair comparison · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A prospect says a competitor is cheaper, but the quotes include different service levels and implementation support.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

objection
A stated concern or reason for not accepting a proposal.
competitor
An organization offering an alternative that serves similar customer needs.
differentiator
A characteristic that meaningfully distinguishes an offering from alternatives.
total cost
The combined costs included in a defined comparison, with assumptions stated.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  2. B. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
  3. C. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  2. B. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.
  3. C. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.

Answer: A. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

Vocabulary for this lesson

champion
A contact inside a customer organization who actively supports a proposed solution.
economic buyer
The person with authority over the budget or financial approval for a purchase.
buying committee
The people who influence or approve an organization's purchase decision.

A defined label indicating the expected likelihood or stage of a sales opportunity.

Ask a precise question

Could you explain what this means?

After an introductory phrase such as 'Could you explain', the embedded question uses statement word order: “what this means”.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  2. B. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
  3. C. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  2. B. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.
  3. C. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

redline
A version of a document showing proposed additions, deletions, or changes.
liability
Legal responsibility or an obligation; in accounting, an amount or duty owed.
indemnity
A contractual obligation to compensate for specified losses, subject to its terms and applicable law.
commercial term
A business condition of an agreement, such as price, quantity, or payment timing.

03 · Notice the language

Ask for an actionable next step

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
  2. B. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  3. C. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.
  2. B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  3. C. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

channel partner
An organization that helps market, sell, deliver, or support another organization's offering.
exclusivity
An arrangement limiting specified rights or activities to one party for an agreed scope.
pipeline
A sequence of stages through which data, work, or sales opportunities move.
enablement
Resources and preparation that help a team perform a role effectively.

03 · Notice the language

Offer a workable tradeoff

Acknowledge the goal and explain the available choices. Connect an offer to its actual conditions. A useful response makes room for discussion without promising approval or resources you do not control.

  • We can ..., provided that ... .
  • Would you prefer ... or ...?
  • I can take that proposal for review; I cannot yet confirm ... .

Improve this: If you will approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

See a clearer version

If you approve the scope, we can assess the schedule.

For an ordinary future condition, the “if” clause uses present tense. The other clause can express the future consequence or possibility.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence presents a normal future condition clearly?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule.

  1. A. If the scope changes, we reviewed the schedule. “Reviewed” places the response in the past instead of expressing the future response.
  2. B. If the scope changes, we will review the schedule. The “if” clause uses present tense; the main clause states the future response.
  3. C. If the scope will change, we will review the schedule. For this ordinary future condition, use present simple in the “if” clause.
2. Which response negotiates a new request without silently promising extra work?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope.

  1. A. We can assess the addition and explain its effect on the agreed scope. This offers a useful next step while keeping the approval and scope question open.
  2. B. We have included it, although its scope has not been reviewed. It treats unreviewed work as already included.
  3. C. We guarantee the original date without assessing the addition. It promises an outcome without examining the consequences.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A deal is forecast to close this month, but procurement has not started and the customer has given no confirmed date.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

CRM
Customer relationship management: processes and systems used to manage customer information and interactions.
pipeline
A sequence of stages through which data, work, or sales opportunities move.
close date
The expected date on which a sales transaction will be completed.
commit
A sales forecast category indicating an expected close under the organization's defined criteria.
slippage
Movement of an expected completion or sales close date to a later time.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. A close date without customer confirmation

The lesson's deal is forecast to close this month, but procurement has not begun and the customer has given no confirmed date.

  1. Sales manager: The CRM shows a close date this month. What customer evidence supports that date?
  2. Account executive: Procurement hasn't started, and the customer hasn't confirmed a date. The entry reflects our expectation, not their commitment.
  3. Sales manager: Then we should distinguish the internal target from a verified step in the buying process.
  4. Account executive: Agreed. I shouldn't describe the deal as commit solely because the calendar contains a close date.
  5. Sales manager: What should change in the pipeline review while those dependencies remain unresolved?
  6. Account executive: I'll state the missing procurement step and date confirmation, then review the forecast category under our actual definitions.
  7. Sales manager: Good. Don't invent a later customer date simply to move the uncertainty into another month.
  8. Account executive: I'll record the uncertainty and ask the customer about the remaining process and realistic timing.
  9. Sales manager: That gives us evidence to assess possible slippage without claiming the deal is lost or guaranteed.
  10. Account executive: I'll update the record with confirmed information and keep assumptions visibly separate from customer statements.
2. A verbal preference is not a signed order

Separate fictional case: a prospect says the supplier is its preferred option, but contract review and signature authority remain unconfirmed.

  1. Account executive: The prospect called us their preferred option. I was going to mark the opportunity as effectively closed.
  2. Pipeline reviewer: Preference is positive evidence, but contract review and signature authority remain unconfirmed in the record.
  3. Account executive: Then I should preserve the customer's words without translating them into a purchase commitment.

3. Two records may describe one opportunity

  1. Revenue operations analyst: These two CRM records appear to describe the same prospect discussion, each carrying the full proposed value.

  4. Sales team lead: Then we need to verify whether the records concern distinct scopes or an actual duplicate.
  5. Revenue operations analyst: Exactly. We shouldn't delete or merge them blindly, but the possible duplication must be visible in the forecast.

  7. Revenue operations analyst: Good. The pipeline total should not present an unresolved duplicate as confirmed additional value.
  8. Sales team lead: And we should preserve the relevant history when the records are reconciled under our CRM process.
  9. Revenue operations analyst: Agreed. I'll flag the counting issue and keep the correction traceable rather than silently changing the total.
  10. Sales team lead: I'll coordinate the ownership check and return with evidence of one opportunity or two genuinely distinct scopes.
