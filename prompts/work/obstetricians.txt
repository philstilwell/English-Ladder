Lesson 01: Taking a sensitive pregnancy history
Original fictional case: At an initial visit, Avery is unsure of the last menstrual period and mentions a previous pregnancy loss. Dr Stone asks permission to discuss the history, records uncertain dates as uncertain, and asks which support person Avery wants involved. No estimated due date, gestational age, or pregnancy outcome is calculated in the case.
Language workshop: Dates, previous experiences, and preferred words
Communication goal: Clarify dating information and previous pregnancy experiences without making assumptions.
Language guidance: An uncertain menstrual date must not become a precise due date by assumption. A history abbreviation does not replace clarification of what actually occurred.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): May we discuss your previous pregnancy experiences? / It is fine to say that you do not remember the exact date. / I will record the date as uncertain. / Is there a term you would prefer us to use? / Who would you like involved in this conversation? / I will explain why I am asking each sensitive question. / I will not infer the outcome from an abbreviation alone. / Let me check that I have understood your history correctly. / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.
Vocabulary: gravidity: Number of pregnancies, counted under the relevant clinical convention. / parity: Birth history summarized using the applicable clinical convention. / gestational age: Duration of pregnancy expressed using the relevant dating method. / estimated due date: Estimated date used for pregnancy planning and dating. / last menstrual period: Reported starting date of the most recent menstrual period. / dating ultrasound: Ultrasound used to inform pregnancy dating in context. / menstrual regularity: Pattern of how consistently menstrual cycles occur. / obstetric history: Account of previous pregnancies and their relevant outcomes. / prior pregnancy loss: Loss in an earlier pregnancy. / live birth: Birth showing signs of life under the relevant definition. / preterm birth: Birth before the relevant full-term threshold. / ectopic pregnancy: Pregnancy implanted outside the usual uterine cavity location. / prenatal record: Record of pregnancy-related care before birth. / support person: Person the patient chooses to provide support. / preferred terminology: Words the person wishes used for sensitive experiences. / trauma-informed approach: Approach attentive to safety, choice, and prior distressing experiences. / dating uncertainty: Unresolved information affecting pregnancy dating. / history clarification: Follow-up questions resolving ambiguous historical information. / qualified interpreter: Trained language professional supporting accurate communication between speakers. / teach-back: Checking an explanation by inviting the listener to restate its meaning. / patient preference: A person's stated wishes about care or communication. / clinical handoff: Transfer of relevant care information and responsibility between professionals. / documented plan: Agreed next steps recorded for the people involved. / source attribution: Identification of who reported, observed, or established a fact.
Speaking task: Read the corrected history discussion. Preserve uncertain dates and the preferred terminology. Swap roles. Explain the difference between history terms without calculating a due date. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied words. Check every explanation, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Avery: Pregnant patient / Dr Stone: Obstetrician
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: An outside record contains an unfamiliar pregnancy-history abbreviation. The patient has not confirmed its meaning. The clinician requests clarification before adopting it.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Dr Stone: No. An estimated due date is an estimate, and its basis should be explained.
Avery: I appreciate knowing what is uncertain instead of feeling that I must remember every detail perfectly.
Dr Stone: A trauma-informed approach includes explaining the purpose of questions and allowing appropriate choice and pauses.
Avery: Then we can complete the history clarification with uncertain dates marked honestly and the previous loss described in my words.
END REFERENCE

Lesson 02: Explaining prenatal screening choices
Original fictional case: Riley receives a higher-chance prenatal screening result and thinks it proves a fetal condition. Dr Noor explains that screening estimates chance rather than establishing a diagnosis. The actual result and available diagnostic options need individualized counseling. No probability value, procedure choice, or pregnancy decision is supplied. Riley wants time to understand the information and include a chosen support person.
Language workshop: A higher chance is not a diagnosis
Communication goal: Explain screening uncertainty and voluntary next steps without directing a reproductive decision.
Language guidance: A higher-chance screening result is not diagnostic confirmation. A lower-chance result does not guarantee absence of every condition.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): This is a screening result, not a diagnosis. / It changes the estimated chance but does not settle the question. / A lower-chance result cannot rule out every condition. / We can arrange a discussion of the actual result and options. / What would you like explained before considering the next step? / Testing decisions require an informed, voluntary discussion. / We can include the person you choose with your permission. / We can pause to make sure the information is clear. / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.
Vocabulary: prenatal screening: Testing that estimates the chance of specified conditions before birth. / diagnostic testing: Testing intended to establish or clarify a condition. / cell-free DNA Professional roles: Dr Stone / Avery
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Dr Stone: Before we discuss dates, may I ask about previous pregnancy experiences and how you would like us to refer to them?
Avery: Yes. I had a pregnancy loss, and I would prefer that wording rather than moving straight to a number.
Dr Stone: I will respect that preferred terminology. The history matters, but the experience is more than an abbreviation.
Avery: I am also unsure of the exact date of my last period. I do not want to give a false answer.

Avery: Will a scan necessarily give a guaranteed day on which the baby will be born?
Dr Stone: No. An estimated due date is an estimate, and its basis should be explained.
Avery: I appreciate knowing what is uncertain instead of feeling that I must remember every detail perfectly.
Dr Stone: A trauma-informed approach includes explaining the purpose of questions and allowing appropriate choice and pauses.
Avery: Then we can complete the history clarification with uncertain dates marked honestly and the previous loss described in my words.

Dialogue 02: A higher chance is not a diagnosis
Original fictional setting: Riley receives a higher-chance prenatal screening result and thinks it proves a fetal condition. Dr Noor explains that screening estimates chance rather than establishing a diagnosis. The actual result and available diagnostic options need individualized counseling. No probability value, procedure choice, or pregnancy decision is supplied. Riley wants time to understand the information and include a chosen support person.
Professional roles: Riley / Dr Noor
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Riley: The message says higher chance. I read that as proof that the baby has the condition.
Dr Noor: This is prenatal screening, not diagnostic confirmation. Let us explain what this particular result can and cannot establish.
Riley: Does that mean the result is meaningless, or that I should simply ignore it?
Dr Noor: No. The screening probability informs the next discussion, but an estimate is not certainty.
Riley: I would like to understand how a positive result can occur when the condition is not present.
Dr Noor: That is a false-positive result. The actual test and your circumstances determine how the result should be interpreted.
Riley: And a lower-chance result would not mean every possible condition has been excluded?
Dr Noor: Correct. Residual risk and the scope of the test still matter.
Riley: Someone mentioned a predictive value, but I did not understand what was being predicted.
Dr Noor: Positive predictive value concerns what positive results mean in context. We should use the actual figures, not invent a number.
Riley: What kind of discussion would help me understand the further testing options?
Dr Noor: Genetic counseling can help explain the actual result, options, limitations, and what information you want.
Riley: Are amniocentesis and the placental-sampling procedure simply more screening blood tests?
Dr Noor: No. Amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling are procedures used for relevant diagnostic testing, with their own discussions of benefits and risks.
Riley: I do not want a procedure selected before I understand what question it would answer.
Dr Noor: That is appropriate. Decision support should clarify the question and your preferences rather than make the decision for you.
Riley: Could I include my chosen support person when we discuss the information again?
Dr Noor: Yes, with your permission. Voluntary testing requires a discussion you understand, with support you choose.
Riley: I also want to know what happens if the further result still does not answer everything.
Dr Noor: We should explain the possibility of an inconclusive result and other limits before you choose the next step.
Vocabulary in this exchange: support person: Person the patient chooses to provide support. / prenatal screening: Testing that estimates the chance of specified conditions before birth. / diagnostic testing: Testing intended to establish or clarify a condition. / screening probability: Estimated chance reported by a screening process. / false-positive result: Positive screening finding not confirmed for the condition assessed. / positive predictive value: Proportion of positive results that correspond to the condition in context.

Professional roles: Morgan / Dr Vale
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Morgan: My glucose screening result needs follow-up. Does that mean I definitely have gestational diabetes and have done something wrong?
Dr Vale: A glucose screening result is not a moral judgment. We need to review the actual method and result.
Morgan: I thought every service used exactly the same test and that the word positive settled everything.
Dr Vale: The testing pathway matters. We should explain the process used here rather than assume every test works identically.
Morgan: Someone mentioned a tolerance test. I do not know how that differs from the first test.
Dr Vale: A glucose tolerance test assesses the response to a glucose load. We will explain the actual procedure and instructions if it is the next step.
Morgan: Should I decide on my own whether to fast before an appointment?
Dr Vale: No. If a fasting sample is required, the preparation must come from the actual instructions rather than a guess.
Morgan: I also heard a number from a friend. Can I use that to interpret my own result?
Dr Vale: A diagnostic threshold belongs to the relevant method and criteria. A number without that context can mislead.
Morgan: If a diagnosis is later established, does that mean every person receives the same monitoring plan?
Dr Vale: No. An individualized target and any monitoring instructions come from the actual clinical plan.
Morgan: What does postprandial mean when people discuss the timing of a reading?
Dr Vale: Postprandial means after a meal. The precise measurement timing still needs the verified plan.
Morgan: If I keep readings later, would the surrounding information matter as well as the number?
Dr Vale: A glucose log needs the relevant timing and context, not isolated numbers without explanation.
Morgan: I would appreciate support that does not treat food choices as evidence that I am a bad parent.
Dr Vale: An appropriate nutrition consultation can address practical needs without blame. The actual recommendation depends on the assessment.
Morgan: Then the immediate task is to understand the next test and who will discuss its result with me.
Dr Vale: Exactly. We will confirm follow-up ownership and the actual arrangements without inventing a diagnosis, target, or treatment today.
Vocabulary in this exchange: gestational diabetes: Diabetes first diagnosed in pregnancy under the relevant clinical criteria. / glucose screening: Initial testing used in a particular glucose-assessment pathway. / glucose tolerance test: Test assessing the body's response to a specified glucose load. / fasting sample: Sample collected under the required fasting conditions. / postprandial: Occurring after a meal. / diagnostic threshold: Value or criterion used in the relevant diagnostic process.

Quinn: Would those alternatives include what happens if I do not choose the proposed option?
Dr Lane: The appropriate reasonable alternative discussion includes the available choices and their implications in your circumstances.
Quinn: Sometimes I nod because I have heard a sentence, not because I have agreed to everything in it.

Quinn: Could you summarize the recommendation again in shorter sentences before asking what I understand?
Dr Lane: Yes. That is part of a useful consent discussion, not a delay caused by asking an unreasonable question.
Quinn: Then my original preference is acknowledged, the changed situation is explained, and I can understand the actual options.
Dr Lane: That supports shared decision-making while respecting the real urgency and keeping you involved in the care discussion.

Dialogue 08: Clear words and space to respond
Original fictional setting: After the appropriate clinical assessment has confirmed an early pregnancy loss, Dr Gray meets patient Eden. Eden asks whether the finding is certain and worries that an ordinary daily activity caused the loss. The cause has not been established. Dr Gray explains the confirmed finding, allows silence, asks about chosen support, and arranges discussion of the actual management options. No procedure, medicine, or follow-up interval is prescribed.
Professional roles: Eden / Dr Gray
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Eden: Please tell me clearly what the assessment has established. Is the finding definite, or is there still uncertainty?
Dr Gray: I am sorry. The pregnancy has ended. This is a confirmed finding. We can pause before discussing anything further.
Eden: I keep thinking about carrying groceries yesterday. Did something ordinary I did cause this?
Dr Gray: The assessment does not establish that cause. I hear the self-blame, but we should not turn an ordinary activity into a proven explanation.
Eden: I do not want someone to tell me it does not matter because it happened early.
Dr Gray: It matters. Acknowledgment of loss means recognizing your experience, not comparing it with someone else's.
Eden: Could we sit quietly for a moment before talking about procedures or what happens next?
Dr Gray: Yes. Empathetic silence can be part of this conversation. I will stay with you while we pause.
Eden: I would like my sister involved, but only after I have understood the main information myself.
Dr Gray: We will respect that support preference and discuss what you want shared when she joins us.
Eden: When we talk about care, will you explain the options rather than assume I already know the medical words?
Dr Gray: Yes. Where appropriate, expectant management is one term we may discuss, with the actual circumstances and safety arrangements explained.
Eden: I have also seen references to medicines and procedures, but I do not know what applies to me.
Dr Gray: Medical management and surgical management need their own explanations of suitability, benefits, risks, and alternatives.
Eden: I would like time to understand the choices within whatever the actual clinical situation allows.
Dr Gray: Your management preference belongs in that discussion. We will explain any real timing considerations rather than assume your decision.
Eden: I may need support after leaving, not just information during this appointment.
Dr Gray: We can discuss bereavement support and the appropriate clinical contact route, according to what would be helpful to you.
Eden: A clear summary would help me remember what was confirmed and what still needs to be decided.
Dr Gray: We will provide a care summary that separates the finding, the unresolved cause, and the next steps actually agreed with you.
Vocabulary in this exchange: confirmed finding: Conclusion established through the appropriate assessment. / expectant management: Management involving observation under an appropriate clinical plan. / medical management: Management using prescribed medicines under the actual care plan. / surgical management: Management involving an appropriate procedure. / management preference: Person's informed preference among relevant care options. / bereavement support: Support offered after a significant loss.
