📅 November 28, 2026 - Huw Edwards Interviews Draw Criticism Over Media Responsibility
Interviews with Huw Edwards have prompted debate over broadcasters' editorial freedom, their responsibility towards victims and whether critical examination can become reputational repair.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

The prime minister's spokesperson urged broadcasters to act responsibly while expressing no interest in hearing Edwards' account following criticism of new interviews. Edwards, who was convicted in 2024 of offences involving indecent images of children, has been interviewed for a Channel 4 documentary and a podcast by the Observer and Tortoise Media. A Downing Street spokesperson placed the interests of victims of child sexual abuse and exploitation at the forefront of the government's response. Pressed on whether Channel 4 had acted irresponsibly, the spokesperson affirmed the broadcaster's editorial autonomy in choosing programmes and contributors. Channel 4 defended the documentary, saying its director had subjected Edwards' account to scrutiny without treating any questions as off limits. Dame Caroline Dinenage, chair of the House of Commons culture and media select committee, warned that media outlets should not merely provide a platform for Edwards. She also called for examination of whether Edwards would return more than £200,000 paid by the BBC after his first arrest. Three people who had previously spoken to BBC News about allegations of inappropriate behaviour by Edwards also criticised the interviews. An anonymous person unconnected to the criminal case interpreted the appearances as an attempt at reputational repair. Duncan Craig, head of We Are Survivors, said the companies appeared to be overlooking victims' interests. He also objected to the timing, describing the media as being in a period of reckoning over abuse. Although Craig endorsed rehabilitation in principle, he questioned whether these programmes amounted to it. In a promotional clip, interviewer Ceri Thomas expressed curiosity about how a man of Edwards' prominence had reached such a disastrous position. Journalist Sangita Myska condemned the documentary as an exercise in restoring Edwards' reputation. Broadcaster Richard Bacon disputed that characterisation, arguing that making a documentary about someone did not, in itself, constitute rehabilitation.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. editorial autonomy (noun phrase): Freedom to decide what to publish or broadcast and how to present it, without those decisions being directed by an outside authority.
2. scrutiny (uncountable noun): Close, critical examination intended to identify weaknesses, inconsistencies or matters requiring explanation.
3. off limits (adjective phrase): Excluded from what people are permitted to discuss or investigate.
4. platform (countable noun): An opportunity to reach a public audience and present an account or viewpoint, without necessarily receiving the publisher's endorsement.
5. reputational repair (noun phrase): An effort to improve how someone is publicly regarded after their conduct has damaged their standing.
6. reckoning (singular countable noun): A period when past wrongdoing receives sustained attention and prompts demands for those responsible to answer for their actions.
7. in principle (adverbial phrase): As a general position or belief, without necessarily supporting a particular practical instance.
8. prominence (uncountable noun): The state of being widely recognised and occupying a position that attracts public attention.
9. characterisation (countable noun): A description that presents something in a particular way and may reflect the speaker's interpretation.
10. constitute (verb, base form): To qualify as something or have the essential features that make that description applicable.

Advanced Grammar: Reduced passive adverbial clauses
A reduced passive opening omits its subject and a form of “be”, leaving a past participle. Its understood subject must match the main-clause subject: “Pressed” presents the spokesperson as receiving pressure, while “Pressing” would make them exert it.
Example from the text: "Pressed on whether Channel 4 had acted irresponsibly, the spokesperson affirmed the broadcaster's editorial autonomy in choosing programmes and contributors."

📅 November 27, 2026 - Zelensky Urges Stronger US Pressure as Ukraine's Defences Strain
Examine Zelensky's criticism of US priorities while distinguishing diplomatic positioning, reported military constraints and uncertainty over further talks.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Volodymyr Zelensky has accused the US of applying insufficient pressure on Russia and said delays in delivering essential defensive missiles were costing Ukrainian lives. He also criticised Washington's emphasis on the Middle East, arguing that countries there were receiving greater assistance. Zelensky expressed unease over Donald Trump's portrayal of Vladimir Putin as a friend in a post showing the two men embracing. His criticism comes after a period of caution in managing US relations following a bruising Oval Office encounter with Trump in 2025. The report describes the two US envoys seeking a peace deal as appearing more in tune with Moscow's position than Kyiv's. The latest talks, mediated in Abu Dhabi, produced no obvious progress, according to the report. Russia appears to have regressed to its initial hardline positions across the negotiating agenda, judging by its public statements. Zelensky said it remained unclear whether European negotiators would be at the table, despite the US envoys having announced their participation. He urged the US to live up to its reputation for strength and use its influence to push Russia towards results. The US presents its neutrality as part of an effort to secure a peace agreement that can hold. Zelensky said Ukraine could now destroy about 70% of Russia's fastest drones and that the proportion was improving. He described a severe deficit of interceptors, missiles used to stop incoming ballistic missiles, and accused the US of delaying deliveries of Patriot systems. Zelensky also confirmed that the US satellite communications systems Starlink and Starshield remained restricted in occupied Ukrainian territory. Ukrainian drone units reported that some Russian drones were travelling farther into Ukraine unhindered because their interceptor drones could no longer catch them. Ukraine has increased its long-range strikes on Russian targets, including oil refineries and ships carrying weapons or fuel. While acknowledging that such strikes were highly dangerous, Zelensky said Ukrainian forces never conducted them without coordinating with partners. Zelensky acknowledged that Ukraine was bracing for a difficult and dangerous winter. Some residents had already left town for the winter and businesses had closed as power cuts and attacks on logistics networks pushed operating costs unmanageably high.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. in tune with (idiomatic phrase): Broadly aligned with someone's views or priorities, without necessarily having a formal agreement.
2. mediated (verb, past participle): Conducted with a third party helping opposing sides seek an agreement.
3. regressed (verb, past participle): Returned to an earlier, less constructive position after apparent movement away from it.
4. hardline (adjective): Unwilling to soften demands or make substantial compromises.
5. at the table (idiomatic phrase): Participating directly in discussions where decisions or agreements are being negotiated.
6. live up to (phrasal verb): Meet the expectations created by a reputation, promise or stated standard.
7. deficit (noun): The amount by which what is available falls short of what is required.
8. interceptors (plural noun): Defensive missiles intended to destroy incoming missiles before they reach their targets.
9. unhindered (adjective): Free from obstruction or interference that would prevent further progress.
10. bracing for (verb phrase, present participle): Preparing mentally or practically for something expected to be difficult or unpleasant.
11. unmanageably high (adjective phrase): So high that those affected cannot cope with the financial burden.

Advanced Grammar: Reduced concessive clauses with “while”
Use “while” followed by an “-ing” form to acknowledge a contrasting consideration without making it a cause. The understood subject must match the main-clause subject; here, Zelensky acknowledges danger while asserting coordination with partners.
Example from the text: "While acknowledging that such strikes were highly dangerous, Zelensky said Ukrainian forces never conducted them without coordinating with partners."

The UK government and the EU are expected to publish two treaty texts on Thursday, following agreements reached the previous week as part of a broader deal. UK legislation intended to enact most of the deal is due to appear alongside the treaty texts. According to the BBC, were either party to terminate the agreement within the first ten years, it would have to pay to re-establish border infrastructure that is to be substantially reduced under the deal. Opposition leader Kemi Badenoch and Reform UK's Nigel Farage have indicated that they would refuse to make such payments. The broader deal covers British food exports, emissions trading and a youth experience scheme. Britain's aviation and maritime industries would also be affected by alignment with EU emissions schemes. Food businesses exporting to the EU are expected to be major beneficiaries of fewer administrative requirements and the dismantling of border facilities. An internal analysis projects a 16% increase in agricultural exports, a forecast rather than an established gain. Industry and government hope that savings for food producers and sellers will reach consumers, although shoppers may see no discernible reduction at the checkout. Industry insiders privately told the BBC that the global economy has too many moving parts to make the outcome clear, particularly amid the energy price shock resulting from war in the Middle East. The treaty text is also expected to specify how the UK will observe and help shape relevant EU rules without having a vote on them. The accompanying legislation is expected to detail plans to align UK and EU law through secondary legislation, meaning rules made under powers granted by an existing Act.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. enact (verb): To give a policy or agreement legal effect by passing legislation.
2. terminate (verb): To formally bring an agreement to an end, with any consequences its terms require.
3. re-establish (verb): To restore something that previously existed after it has been removed or reduced.
4. emissions trading (noun phrase): A system in which participants buy and sell allowances permitting specified quantities of greenhouse gas emissions.
5. alignment (noun): The process or state of bringing rules or systems into agreement so that they operate consistently.
6. beneficiaries (plural noun): People or organisations that gain an advantage from a particular arrangement or change.
7. dismantling (noun): The process of taking facilities apart and removing them from use.
8. moving parts (plural noun phrase): Interacting factors that can change independently, making an overall outcome difficult to predict.
9. observe (verb): To comply with a rule or requirement, rather than merely watch what happens.
10. secondary legislation (noun phrase): Rules made using authority granted by an existing Act, rather than by passing a new Act.

Advanced Grammar: Conditional inversion with “were” and a to-infinitive
Use “were + subject + to-infinitive” instead of “if + subject + past simple” for a formal hypothetical future condition. The main clause uses “would + infinitive” for its consequence; the structure does not establish that withdrawal is likely.

📅 November 23, 2026 - COP31 Deal Collapses Over Fossil Fuels and Finance
COP31's failed main agreement exposed competing priorities over energy and financial support, prompting questions about how international climate decisions are reached.

Read the story

Countries failed to secure a major new climate agreement at COP31 in Turkey after divisions over fossil fuels and finance emerged. The protracted discussions continued from their scheduled Friday finish into Sunday, making this the longest UN climate conference in history. Exhausted delegates received a fresh draft in the early hours of Sunday in an attempt to break the deadlock. The proposed agreement was unacceptable to the EU, Pacific island nations and others because it omitted any direct mention of fossil fuels, the main cause of global warming. Its only indirect reference to coal, oil and gas recalled the COP28 agreement to move away from fossil fuels in energy systems. Saudi delegate Mohammad Ayoub said Arab countries had participated in good faith, while arguing that their share of historical greenhouse gas emissions was small. Russia warned that moving away from fossil fuels too quickly would leave people unable to afford adequate energy. Not only did this year's deal omit concrete figures, but it also failed to specify the baseline against which the pledge would be measured. This lack of precision raised fears that richer nations would have excessive wriggle room in fulfilling the pledge. Chris Bowen, the Australian minister serving as COP31 president of negotiations, described delegates' red lines as irreconcilable. According to the UN, current government policies are likely to limit warming to about 2.8°C by 2100. Long-term warming is nevertheless likely to overshoot the Paris agreement's 1.5°C goal within the next few years. Many countries say they are committed to eventually bringing warming back down to 1.5°C, but this would require a major expansion of carbon dioxide removal technologies that do not yet operate at scale. Although the main agreement fell through, delegates made progress on a framework to support workers and communities as economies shift towards clean energy. The outcome prompted questions about whether a climate process that relies on consensus can succeed amid geopolitical divisions. UN climate chief Simon Stiell said he would seek advice on procedural reform, with the next conference scheduled for November 2027 in Ethiopia.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. protracted (adjective): Continuing longer than expected or desired, often suggesting difficulty or frustration.
2. deadlock (noun): A situation in which opposing positions prevent an agreement or further progress.
3. in good faith (adverbial phrase): With an honest intention to cooperate, rather than merely appearing to participate.
4. baseline (noun): A reference value against which a result or the fulfilment of a pledge can be measured.
5. wriggle room (noun phrase): An informal, often critical expression for freedom to avoid a firm commitment or interpret it advantageously.
6. irreconcilable (adjective): So opposed that the positions cannot be brought into agreement.
7. overshoot (verb, base form): To exceed a target or limit, without necessarily remaining above it permanently.
8. at scale (adverbial phrase): At a level of deployment large enough to address the wider problem, rather than through limited applications.
9. fell through (phrasal verb, past simple): Failed to be completed or secured after being planned or pursued.
10. procedural reform (noun phrase): Changes to the rules or methods through which a process operates and reaches decisions.

Advanced Grammar: Inversion after fronted “not only”
When “not only” begins a clause for emphasis, put an auxiliary before the subject. In the past simple, use “did” plus the base form of the verb; the following “but it also” clause retains ordinary subject–verb order.
Example from the text: "Not only did this year's deal omit concrete figures, but it also failed to specify the baseline against which the pledge would be measured."

Read the story

Negotiations at the COP31 climate summit in Turkey remained in deadlock, with countries unable to agree even after discussions had overrun by more than a day. The EU and Pacific island nations opposed the latest text because it omitted any reference to reducing global demand for fossil fuels. A second sticking point concerned the African Group's request for stronger financial support for adaptation to climate impacts, which the EU had previously opposed. Delegations moving their return flights from Sunday to Monday provided a further indication that agreement remained elusive. Should countries fail to reach the required consensus, the negotiations could be suspended. The summit could then end without agreement and be reconvened next year, an outcome with only one precedent since these climate meetings began in 1995. Ministers sought to have a commitment to shift away from oil, gas and coal reinstated after fossil fuel producers led by Saudi Arabia pushed for its removal. References to carbon capture, use and storage, a technology that stores released emissions underground, were criticised as a licence to continue burning oil and gas. The proposed agreement could, for the first time at such a summit, acknowledge that the goal of keeping global temperature rise below 1.5C has been missed. Australia's climate change and energy minister, Chris Bowen, resisted calls to include language on fossil fuels. He argued that the summit should prioritise implementation, meaning putting measures into practice, and focus on the transition towards clean energy. Meanwhile, renewable energy was expanding at unprecedented rates, with solar panels making the largest contribution to meeting new global energy demand that year and outstripping oil and gas. One reported proposal would keep fossil fuels off the table in exchange for Saudi Arabia and other fossil fuel producers pledging to obtain 35% of their energy consumption from electricity by 2035. An earlier objective of increasing adaptation funding to $120 billion by 2035 had been removed from the text. Ghana's environment minister presented funding as a rightful claim on behalf of people bearing the brunt of developed countries' activities. The departure of negotiating teams could also leave too few nations present to pass a global agreement.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. deadlock (noun): A situation in which opposing positions prevent those involved from reaching agreement.
2. sticking point (noun phrase): A particular unresolved issue that obstructs a wider agreement.
3. reconvened (verb, past participle): Brought together again after a meeting has ended or been interrupted.
4. reinstated (verb, past participle): Restored to a document or position after previously being removed.
5. licence (noun): An apparent justification or permission to act, used critically here because the behaviour is considered harmful.
6. implementation (noun): The process of putting an agreed policy or measure into practice.
7. unprecedented (adjective): Not previously experienced or recorded in the relevant context, here describing the rate of growth.
8. outstripping (verb, present participle): Exceeding something else in a particular measure, here the contribution to meeting new energy demand.
9. off the table (idiomatic phrase): Excluded from the matters being considered or negotiated.
10. bearing the brunt (participial phrase): Experiencing the greatest share of something harmful or difficult.

Wang also highlighted the unusual opportunity for direct exposure to a frontier-model developer, allowing public investors to invest directly in a company building leading-edge AI models with the potential to influence future productivity. Anthropic refused a US Department of Defense demand that its tools be available for essentially unrestricted use. The company has advocated slower AI development, arguing that advanced systems could pose catastrophic risks to humanity without appropriate safeguards. Nevertheless, Anthropic is seeking greater access to public financing to support rapid expansion. Broader concerns surround an AI business model dependent on scarce, expensive chips, controversial data centres and financing arrangements involving other technology companies. Minutes from the Federal Reserve Bank’s October meeting recorded concern about rapidly expanding AI-related debt financed through complex and opaque structures that are difficult to understand. A few participants also noted that AI-related financial exposures were becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. on condition of anonymity (prepositional phrase): With the agreement that the person providing information will not be publicly identified.
2. oversubscribed (adjective): Attracting requests for more shares than are available, without necessarily indicating how those shares will perform later.
3. private placements (plural noun phrase): Sales of shares or other investments arranged privately with selected investors rather than offered generally to the public.
4. financial obligations (plural noun phrase): Commitments requiring a person or organisation to make payments.
5. direct exposure (noun phrase): An investment connection through which a company’s performance can directly affect an investor’s financial returns.
6. frontier-model developer (compound noun): A company or organisation developing AI models at the leading edge of current capabilities.
7. unrestricted (adjective): Not subject to limits on how something may be used.
8. catastrophic (adjective): Causing exceptionally severe and widespread harm; stronger in meaning than “serious” or “disruptive”.
9. market volatility (noun phrase): A situation in which financial market prices change substantially or unpredictably.
10. opaque (adjective): Difficult to understand or examine because the relevant arrangements are unclear.
11. concentrated (adjective): Gathered within a limited group rather than distributed across many participants.

In a conditional clause, place “should” before the subject and use the verb’s base form to replace a clause beginning with “if”. This formal pattern presents a possible future condition; “could” in the main clause keeps the outcome uncertain.

📅 November 18, 2026 - Waymo Pauses US Freeway Services After California Ban
Examine Waymo's nationwide freeway pause, the conditions attached to California's ban, and the distinctions between reported safety performance, immediate risks and corrective action.

Read the story

Waymo has announced a temporary withdrawal of its driverless taxis from freeways throughout the United States. The nationwide decision followed California's prohibition on freeway operations after several traffic incidents. In imposing the ban, California's Department of Motor Vehicles described an “imminent hazard to public safety” and pointed to several Waymo vehicles entering an active construction area on US Route 101. The regulator identified recurring incursions into active construction areas as a safety risk, noting the presence of workers, vehicles and equipment. Waymo said that no injuries or collisions had been reported following the Route 101 incident south of San Francisco. Separate footage circulating widely on social media appeared to show one of its cars attempting to overtake patrol vehicles while they intentionally slowed highway traffic in San Francisco. California's ban took effect on 11 November, with its removal contingent on Waymo demonstrating effective corrective action to address the deficiencies that precipitated it. Waymo said its broader freeway pause was in keeping with its safety principles and was intended to allow it to assess performance in its other operating markets. The company also said it was working diligently with state officials towards restarting freeway services. Data on Waymo's website suggest that its vehicles are involved in 95% fewer crashes causing serious injury than human drivers. Although Waymo operates driverless taxis in 15 US cities, freeway journeys had been available in only a few locations, including San Francisco, Los Angeles and Miami. Its taxis remain permitted to operate on ordinary city streets. In May, the company recalled thousands of vehicles over a software problem that could allow them to enter flooded roads.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. withdrawal (noun): The removal of a service or operation from a particular setting, without necessarily ending it permanently.
2. imminent hazard (noun phrase): A dangerous situation considered capable of causing harm very soon.
3. recurring incursions (plural noun phrase): Repeated entries into an area where those entering should not be, suggesting a pattern rather than an isolated event.
4. overtake (verb): To move past another vehicle travelling in the same direction; an attempt to do this does not establish that the manoeuvre was completed.
5. contingent on (adjective phrase): Dependent on a particular condition being fulfilled, rather than certain to happen.
6. corrective action (noun phrase): Measures taken to remedy an identified problem or failure.
7. deficiencies (plural noun): Weaknesses or shortcomings that prevent something from meeting the required standard.
8. precipitated (verb, past simple): Brought about or triggered an event or decision, often with a suggestion of suddenness.
9. in keeping with (prepositional phrase): Consistent with a stated principle, policy or expected standard of behaviour.
10. diligently (adverb): With sustained care and effort; in a corporate statement, it emphasises a claimed commitment to handling a matter responsibly.

Advanced Grammar: An “-ing” clause after “contingent on”
After “contingent on”, use a noun or an “-ing” clause to express a necessary condition. In “Waymo demonstrating”, “Waymo” identifies the subject of that clause. The construction presents effective corrective action as a condition for lifting the ban, without asserting that it has been demonstrated.
Example from the text: "California's ban took effect on 11 November, with its removal contingent on Waymo demonstrating effective corrective action to address the deficiencies that precipitated it."

📅 November 17, 2026 - Robert Pattinson Named Bond as Amazon Gains Creative Control
Robert Pattinson’s appointment as James Bond combines a commercially reassuring casting choice with a transfer of creative authority, while several details about the next film remain unresolved.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

Robert Pattinson, known for Twilight and The Batman, has been selected as the next James Bond. Amazon MGM Studios revealed the casting in a social media post accompanied by an image of Pattinson as 007. The 40-year-old will become the seventh actor to portray Bond, with the next film expected in 2028. Denis Villeneuve will direct and Steven Knight will write the film, although its title and exact release date remain unannounced. The casting follows years of speculation surrounding one of entertainment’s most highly scrutinised roles. Amazon MGM chose an established, bankable star who is widely regarded as a safe pair of hands. Pattinson’s fearlessness, self-deprecating wit and ability to reinvent himself were praised as qualities that suit him to Bond. Although reports suggested that his agreement committed him to three films, that detail has not been officially confirmed. Pattinson first rose to prominence as Cedric Diggory in the 2005 film Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire. His five Twilight films alongside Kristen Stewart collectively earned more than $3 billion worldwide. He has also earned respect for taking roles in smaller productions, including experimental and arthouse films. In 2025, Barbara Broccoli and Michael G Wilson agreed to sell creative control of the franchise to Amazon MGM. The Broccoli family had controlled the series since 1962, when Sean Connery first appeared as Bond. For decades, the family protected the character’s integrity while resisting spin-offs and licensing. Some industry experts expect these forms of expansion to follow Amazon’s takeover. If the next Bond film opens in 2028 as expected, about seven years will have passed since No Time To Die by the time it is released, making this the longest gap in the franchise’s history.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. highly scrutinised (adjective phrase): Examined closely and critically, with considerable attention paid to choices and performance.
2. bankable (adjective): Considered likely to attract audiences and generate revenue, especially through an established reputation.
3. safe pair of hands (idiomatic noun phrase): Someone trusted to handle an important responsibility competently and reliably.
4. self-deprecating wit (noun phrase): Clever humour that gently makes fun of the speaker’s own qualities or limitations.
5. reinvent himself (verb phrase): Develop a substantially different professional identity or approach instead of remaining associated with one established image.
6. arthouse (adjective): Describing films that emphasise artistic expression or unconventional approaches rather than broad commercial appeal.
7. creative control (noun phrase): Authority over artistic decisions, such as how characters and stories are developed.
8. franchise (noun): A connected series of films together with its recurring characters, identity and associated commercial rights.
9. integrity (noun): The preservation of a character’s essential qualities and consistency.
10. spin-offs (plural noun): New productions developed from an existing series, often focusing on a related character or setting.
11. licensing (uncountable noun): The practice of granting others permission to use protected characters, names or other material commercially.

Advanced Grammar: Future perfect with a future time clause
Use the future perfect, “will have” plus a past participle, to view a completed period from a future point. After “by the time”, use the present simple for that future point. Here, the approximate seven-year interval depends on the film opening in 2028 as expected; it is not a confirmed release date.
Example from the text: "If the next Bond film opens in 2028 as expected, about seven years will have passed since No Time To Die by the time it is released, making this the longest gap in the franchise’s history."

📅 November 16, 2026 - Four Planets Form November’s Pre-dawn Parade
November’s four-planet display combines striking visual arrangements with practical observational challenges, particularly Mercury’s faint appearance near the horizon.

Tap an underlined word in the story to see its meaning.

Read the story

November’s pre-dawn sky offers a diagonal configuration of four planets. Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter will be observable without optical equipment in the eastern to south-eastern sky. Of these four planets, Mercury will be the hardest to discern. As the smallest planet and the one nearest the Sun, Mercury is persistently caught in solar glare. Its visibility is confined to twilight, and it remains faint and low on the horizon. An unobstructed south-eastern view across a flat horizon offers the recommended setting for spotting Mercury. What makes Venus especially conspicuous is that only the Moon appears brighter in the night sky. Among the major planets, Venus has the greatest reflectivity, the capacity to send incoming sunlight back into space. Observers can distinguish Venus from nearby Spica by looking for a steady glow rather than the star’s twinkling. Mars and Jupiter will be in particularly striking apparent proximity on 15 and 16 November. According to astronomer Gideon Bendavid-Walker at the Royal Observatory Greenwich, their angular separation, the angle between their positions in the sky, will be just 1.2 degrees. He compares that interval with the width of a finger held at arm’s length. Near Regulus, the two planets may resemble a group of three stars. While Uranus will be setting in the west before sunrise, Saturn and Neptune will be visible for a time in the evening. Observers should choose a location away from artificial skyglow with a good view of the eastern horizon, allowing around twenty minutes for their eyes to acclimatise to darkness. The recommended viewing period for the planetary parade is approximately 45–60 minutes before sunrise.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. configuration (noun): The arrangement of several objects in relation to one another, considered as a whole.
2. discern (verb): To manage to see or recognise something that is difficult to distinguish from its surroundings.
3. solar glare (noun phrase): Intense light from the Sun that makes nearby objects difficult to see.
4. confined to (adjective phrase): Restricted to a particular situation, place or period; here, a limited lighting condition.
5. unobstructed (adjective): Having nothing in the way that blocks the view.
6. conspicuous (adjective): Readily noticeable because something stands out strongly from its surroundings.
7. reflectivity (noun): The capacity of a surface to send incoming light back rather than absorb it.
8. apparent proximity (noun phrase): Closeness as it appears to an observer, without establishing the actual distance between the objects.
9. angular separation (noun phrase): The angle between the directions in which two objects are seen, rather than the distance between them in space.
10. acclimatise (verb): To become accustomed to different surrounding conditions; here, for the eyes to become accustomed to darkness.

Advanced Grammar: Pseudo-cleft sentences for explanatory emphasis
A pseudo-cleft uses a “what” clause followed by “be” to emphasise the information that follows. Here, the opening clause establishes what needs explaining, while the clause after “is” foregrounds the reason Venus stands out.
Example from the text: "What makes Venus especially conspicuous is that only the Moon appears brighter in the night sky."

Observers should choose a location away from artificial skyglow with a good view of the eastern horizon, allowing around twenty minutes for their eyes to acclimatise to darkness. 📅 November 15, 2026 - SpaceX Catches Starship’s Upper Stage in Recovery Breakthrough
SpaceX’s first capture of Starship’s upper stage advances its pursuit of full rocket reuse, although combining both recoveries remains an unresolved challenge.

Read the story

SpaceX recovered Starship’s upper stage for the first time after a flight lasting twenty-two and a half hours. The returning vehicle hovered beside its launch tower before enormous mechanical arms, nicknamed chopsticks, gripped it. The booster triggered an automated abort, a cancellation initiated by its own systems, before diverting to the ocean, hovering above the water and exploding. During its journey into space, the upper stage released 26 satellites belonging to the Starlink constellation, a satellite system providing high-speed internet. The Ship completed fourteen circuits of Earth before returning to the launch tower. Before launch, SpaceX said an effort to catch the Ship would be contingent on a careful assessment of the vehicle in space. Had problems emerged, the Ship would have been brought down in the ocean instead. Developing a fully reusable rocket remains a long-held ambition for SpaceX. The company needs dependable recovery of both Starship sections to enable repeated flights and sharply lower costs. Falcon 9 already illustrates the financial value of partial reuse: its rockets fly more than 100 times annually, and reusing the lower section has substantially reduced launch costs. SpaceX had already caught the Super Heavy booster on three earlier test flights. Prof Malcolm Macdonald, president of the Royal Aeronautical Society, said landing an entire Starship would represent a major advance in spaceflight. In his assessment, the next milestone is to combine the two recovery achievements in one flight and demonstrate that the process works dependably, repeatedly and economically. Starship’s progress has direct implications for Nasa’s ambition to land humans on the Moon in 2028, a highly ambitious timetable. SpaceX’s Nasa contract requires it to configure the Ship as a lander that will eventually carry astronauts to the lunar surface. Starship’s development has proceeded more slowly than SpaceX intended, and considerable progress in the coming months is necessary to meet its commitments.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. automated abort (noun phrase): A cancellation of a planned operation triggered by a control system rather than a direct human decision.
2. diverting (verb, present participle): Changing course towards an alternative destination instead of continuing along the intended route.
3. constellation (noun): A group of satellites organised to operate together as a system.
4. contingent on (adjective phrase): Dependent on a particular condition being satisfied before something can proceed.
5. long-held ambition (noun phrase): An important goal maintained over an extended period, without implying that it has been achieved.
6. milestone (noun): A significant achievement that marks progress towards a larger objective.
7. economically (adverb): In a way that keeps costs low enough for an activity to be financially practical.
8. implications (plural noun): Possible consequences or wider effects that a development has for another undertaking.
9. configure (verb): Arrange or adapt a vehicle’s design and equipment so that it can perform a particular function.
10. commitments (plural noun): Responsibilities or promised undertakings that an organisation is expected to fulfil.

Advanced Grammar: Inversion in past counterfactual conditionals
To express an unreal past condition in a formal register, place “had” before the subject and omit “if”. Use “would have” plus a past participle for the imagined consequence; here, the condition concerns problems emerging before recovery.
Example from the text: "Had problems emerged, the Ship would have been brought down in the ocean instead."
