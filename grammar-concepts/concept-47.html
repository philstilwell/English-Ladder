Don't or doesn't?

Choose don't or doesn't and keep the following verb in its base form.

Level ranges are study guidance. Start with the examples and choose your own pace.

Try the practice →Learner workbook PDFTeaching guide PDF

I / YOU / WE / THEY: DON'T

I don’t drive. They don’t work on Sundays.

“Don’t” means “do not”. The following verb stays in its plain form.

HE / SHE / IT: DOESN'T

She doesn’t drive. This shop doesn’t open on Sundays.

“This shop” is singular, so it follows the same pattern as “it”.

KEEP THE BASE VERB

She drives. / She doesn’t drive. He has a ticket. / He doesn’t have a ticket.

“Doesn’t” carries the subject agreement. The next verb is “drive” or “have”, without a third-person ending.

Understand

The useful distinction

For present-simple negatives with verbs such as “drive”, “work”, and “open”, use “don’t” with “I”, “you”, “we”, and “they”. Use “doesn’t” with “he”, “she”, and “it”. A singular noun follows the “it” pattern: “This shop doesn’t open on Sundays”. A plural noun follows “they”: “These shops don’t open on Sundays”. These are the standard-English forms taught in this lesson.

After “don’t” or “doesn’t”, use the base verb: the plain form, such as “drive”, “work”, or “open”. Compare “She drives” with “She doesn’t drive”. Do not add -s or -es to the main verb after “doesn’t”. The same pattern gives “doesn’t have” and “doesn’t go”, not “doesn’t has” or “doesn’t goes”. The full forms are “do not” and “does not”.

This pattern does not apply to every negative sentence. With “be”, put “not” after “am”, “is”, or “are”: “I am not late”, “The printer isn’t ready”, “They aren’t tired”. Compare “The printer doesn’t work” with “The printer isn’t ready”. The first uses the verb “work”; the second uses “be” with the describing word “ready”.
