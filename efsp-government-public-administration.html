Your goal

Explain a useful distinction in plain English.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A resident mistakes draft eligibility guidance for a final rule. The office has published current guidance and a separate proposal.

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

policy
Formal rule or standard that guides decisions and behavior.
eligibility
Rules that determine whether a person, account, provider, service, or request qualifies for a program, benefit, or action.
implementation
Putting an agreed plan, product, or process into operation.
public guidance
Official information explaining how a public program or process operates.

03 · Notice the language

Make a complex point clear

Start with the reader's question. Explain one unfamiliar term with familiar words, then give a relevant example or contrast. Check understanding without asking only 'Do you understand?'.

  • In this context, ... means ... .
  • The difference is that ... .
  • How would you explain that distinction to a colleague?

Improve this: We need to operationalize the implementation.

See a clearer version

We need to put the plan into practice.

The revised sentence names the action in familiar words. Specialist vocabulary is useful when it adds precision, but repeating abstract nouns can hide the meaning.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
  2. B. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
  3. C. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.
  2. B. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.
  3. C. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. The proposal is not current eligibility guidance

The lesson's office has current guidance and a separate proposal; a constituent-facing team needs to explain the difference after a resident confuses them.

  1. Constituent services officer: A resident read the proposal and thought it changed eligibility immediately. How should I explain the distinction?
  2. Policy officer: Explain that current public guidance describes the present process, while the separate proposal describes a possible change.
  3. Constituent services officer: So publishing a proposal doesn't itself establish that its eligibility provisions are now in effect?
  4. Policy officer: Correct. We should direct the resident to the current guidance without deciding their individual eligibility here.
  5. Constituent services officer: Could I describe the proposal as the next version of the policy, to make it simpler?
  6. Policy officer: That would suggest its adoption is certain. Keep the proposed status explicit instead.
  7. Constituent services officer: I'll say the proposal is under consideration, not a replacement for the current published guidance.
  8. Policy officer: Good. Also use the actual document labels so the resident can distinguish the two sources.
  9. Constituent services officer: And if they ask about their own application, I'll refer the question through the relevant case process.
  10. Policy officer: Exactly. A clear explanation of document status helps without inventing a decision about their circumstances.
2. An effective date and a training date

Separate fictional case: an office's approved service procedure begins on Monday; staff training occurred last Thursday. A supervisor confuses the training date with implementation.

  1. Front-desk supervisor: Staff learned the new procedure last Thursday. Does that mean implementation began when the training finished?
  2. Program officer: No. In this fictional program, the approved start date is Monday; training prepared staff for that change.
  3. Front-desk supervisor: Then a training session and an effective date answer different questions about the policy?
  4. Program officer: Yes. One concerns preparation, while the other tells us when the approved procedure starts.
  5. Front-desk supervisor: What should we say about cases received before Monday? The training notes don't explain that.
  6. Program officer: Identify that as an unresolved transition question and ask the policy owner for the applicable guidance.
  7. Front-desk supervisor: I shouldn't assume the new procedure applies retrospectively simply because the staff have been trained.
  8. Program officer: Correct. Explain what we know, and don't create a transition rule to fill the gap.
  9. Front-desk supervisor: I'll brief the team on Monday's start date and flag earlier cases for clarification.
  10. Program officer: I'll obtain the transition explanation so public guidance and staff practice use the same confirmed information.
3. Meeting a condition versus receiving a decision

Separate fictional case: a fictional program's guidance lists several eligibility conditions, and a service team is discussing a caller who mentions only one condition.

  1. Service adviser: The caller meets one listed condition. Could I tell them that means their application will be approved?
  2. Program coordinator: Not from that information. Meeting one condition is different from completing the program's eligibility assessment.
  3. Service adviser: How can I explain that without sounding as though I am discouraging them from applying?
  4. Program coordinator: Say that the guidance lists several conditions and the responsible team assesses the required information.
  5. Service adviser: So I can acknowledge the information they provided without announcing an outcome we haven't determined.
  6. Program coordinator: Exactly. Public guidance explains the process; an individual decision applies that process to a particular case.
  7. Service adviser: Would it help to ask which part of the guidance they want explained next?
  8. Program coordinator: Yes. Answer within your role and refer questions requiring a case decision to the authorized team.
  9. Service adviser: I'll distinguish understanding the conditions from confirming eligibility, and avoid predicting an approval date.
  10. Program coordinator: Good. That keeps the explanation useful while preserving the limits of what we actually know.

Scenario 2 · A summary leaflet and the full guidance

In a fictional program, a short leaflet omits a condition that appears in the current full guidance. No decision has been made on a caller's case. Discuss how the service team should explain the difference and seek correction of the leaflet.

Role A: You are the service supervisor. Explain why a shortened leaflet is not an individual eligibility decision.

Role B: You are the policy coordinator. Clarify the authoritative internal source and arrange review of the omission.

Possible opening: “The leaflet is a summary, and it omits a condition that appears in the current full guidance.”

Success checks
  • Explain summary guidance versus an individual decision.
  • Acknowledge the confusing omission without inventing a remedy.
  • Agree an authorized clarification and leaflet-review next step.
Add a complication

Vocabulary for this lesson

public comment
Input submitted by members of the public in a defined consultation process.
stakeholder
Person or group with an interest, risk, authority, or dependency in the work.
public record
A record held by a public body that is subject to applicable records rules.
facilitation
Guiding a discussion so people can contribute, understand differences, and reach useful next steps.

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
  2. B. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
  3. C. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.
  2. B. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.
  3. C. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  2. B. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
  3. C. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  2. B. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.
  3. C. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.

Vocabulary for this lesson

jurisdiction

memorandum of understanding

Making data available to another person or organization under appropriate conditions.

Named person or role accountable for a decision, action, deliverable, or risk.

03 · Notice the language

Transfer information and responsibility

State the current situation, relevant background, open task, and receiving role. Ask for acknowledgment or repeat-back of the critical item. Sending a message and transferring responsibility are not always the same thing.

  • The current status is ...; the outstanding item is ... .
  • The record shows ... .
  • Please confirm who has accepted ... .

Improve this: Someone should follow up on this.

See a clearer version

Please confirm which team has accepted the follow-up task.

The revised sentence asks for an identifiable receiving team instead of leaving responsibility with an unspecified 'someone'.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence checks that responsibility has been accepted?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

  1. A. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check. It asks for an identifiable person or team responsible for the task.
  2. B. I mentioned the check in my earlier email. Mentioning a task does not show that anyone accepted it.
  3. C. The check appears somewhere in the notes. A written record alone does not identify who will act.
2. Which response best checks a critical handoff detail?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

  1. A. I assume all results are final, so we can finish. It changes pending information into a final result.
  2. B. I saw the message, so no clarification is needed. Receiving a message is not the same as confirming its meaning.
  3. C. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct? This repeats the status and responsibility and invites correction.

Your goal

Acknowledge a communication problem and give a corrected message.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A resident has been redirected between offices without an explanation of who can address the issue.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

constituent
A person served or represented by a public official or institution.
casework
Work performed to help an individual navigate a service, issue, or administrative process.
appeal
A request for a decision to be reviewed through an established process.
authority
The recognized power to decide, act, or provide an authoritative basis for a conclusion.

03 · Notice the language

Repair a misunderstanding

Name what was unclear or wrong, acknowledge its effect, and correct it. A brief apology can help, followed by a practical next step. Avoid explaining away the other person's reaction or promising an outcome you cannot control.

  • My earlier message did not make ... clear.
  • I'm sorry for ... . The correct information is ... .
  • Let me check that the revised explanation addresses ... .

Improve this: I'm sorry if you failed to understand.

See a clearer version

I'm sorry my message did not explain the timing clearly.

The revision takes responsibility for the message without blaming the listener. It identifies what needs to be corrected.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which apology takes responsibility for unclear wording?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear.

  1. A. I'm sorry the process took longer than expected. This acknowledges duration, but does not take responsibility for the unclear wording.
  2. B. I'm sorry we could not meet the original date. This acknowledges a missed date, not the communication problem in the question.
  3. C. I'm sorry my earlier wording was unclear. This acknowledges the speaker's contribution to the problem.
2. What should follow an apology for an incorrect date?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status.

  1. A. A new guaranteed date even if it is unverified. An unsupported new promise can create a second misunderstanding.
  2. B. The corrected date and the current confirmation status. A repair needs usable corrected information, including any uncertainty.
  3. C. A longer explanation that never states the correct date. The reader still lacks the information they need.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A records request involves material that may require review for an exemption. An initial note says all material will automatically be released.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

public records
Records held by public bodies and governed by applicable access and retention rules.
exemption
A defined exception to a rule or requirement under the applicable process.
conflict of interest
A situation where another interest may improperly influence a person's duties or judgment.
ethics
Principles and standards used to judge responsible conduct.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  2. B. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  3. C. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  2. B. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  3. C. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Located records are not cleared records

The lesson's public-records request may involve material requiring exemption review; an initial note incorrectly promises automatic release of everything.

  1. Records officer: The initial note promises automatic release of all material, but some records may require exemption review.
  2. Service communications lead: Can we say those records are exempt now, since a review has been identified?
  3. Records officer: No. A possible exemption is not a determination. We need the authorized review under the applicable process.
  4. Service communications lead: Then the correction should avoid both guaranteed release and a premature refusal.
  5. Records officer: Exactly. State that the request is being handled and distinguish located material from material cleared for release.
  6. Service communications lead: Should I add a standard deadline from another office's public-records webpage to reassure the requester?
  7. Records officer: Not without checking applicability. We must use the actual process and approved timing for this request.
  8. Service communications lead: I'll remove the automatic-release claim and obtain the responsible team's wording before responding.
  9. Records officer: Please preserve the uncertainty clearly rather than implying that review itself decides the outcome.
  10. Service communications lead: Understood. The response will describe the current status, not invent a release decision.
2. A declared connection is not a misconduct finding

Separate fictional case: a committee member discloses a prior professional connection with a bidder; the ethics review has not assessed it.

  1. Committee administrator: A member disclosed a prior professional connection with a bidder. Should the minutes call this a conflict of interest?
  2. Ethics coordinator: Record the disclosure accurately, but don't present a pending assessment as a concluded finding.
  3. Committee administrator: So the known fact is the disclosed connection, while its implications remain for authorized review?
  4. Ethics coordinator: Correct. Follow the applicable ethics process for participation questions rather than inventing a determination here.
  5. Committee administrator: Could we describe the member as having acted improperly because they raised the issue during the meeting?
  6. Ethics coordinator: No. The timing and disclosure do not establish misconduct. Keep allegations and verified facts distinct.
  7. Committee administrator: I'll use neutral wording and ask the responsible reviewer what next procedural action is required.
  8. Ethics coordinator: Good. Also avoid circulating unnecessary personal detail beyond the appropriate record and review channels.
  9. Committee administrator: The minutes will state that a connection was disclosed and that assessment remains pending.
  10. Ethics coordinator: That preserves transparency about the event without prejudging the ethics review.
3. A search result does not establish completeness

Separate fictional case: a records search returned files from the shared drive, but archived email has not yet been searched.

  1. Records search coordinator: The shared-drive search is complete, but archived email remains unchecked. We should not call the collection complete.
  2. Department liaison: The retrieved folder is large. Could we say all relevant records were found based on that volume?
  3. Records search coordinator: No. Volume doesn't establish completeness, especially while a known source has not been searched.
  4. Department liaison: I'll describe which source was searched and identify archived email as the outstanding part.
  5. Records search coordinator: Please also distinguish finding a file from assessing whether it belongs within the request's scope.
  6. Department liaison: And scope assessment is separate from any exemption or release review, correct?
  7. Records search coordinator: Correct. Those are distinct questions, and none should be treated as settled by a search result alone.
  8. Department liaison: I'll avoid promising a complete release package until the responsible team verifies those stages.
  9. Records search coordinator: Thank you. I'll coordinate the remaining search and report any access limitation rather than hide it.
  10. Department liaison: Then our status update can be specific about progress without claiming a finished public-records response.

Two short language checks

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
  2. B. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
  3. C. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.
  2. B. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.
  3. C. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Activities delivered versus public outcomes

The lesson's budget proposal lists delivered activities but does not show the intended public outcomes.

  1. Budget analyst: The proposal lists activities delivered, but what public outcome is the requested appropriation intended to support?
  2. Program manager: We have described what staff did. We haven't yet connected those activities to a measured public result.
  3. Budget analyst: That is the distinction to explain: activity counts show work performed, while outcome measures show intended changes.
  4. Program manager: Could we call a higher activity count improved public value without collecting any other information?
  5. Budget analyst: Not automatically. More activity may matter, but it doesn't establish whether the intended benefit occurred.
  6. Program manager: I'll explain the intended result separately and identify the evidence we have not yet collected.
  7. Budget analyst: Please also consider equity: an overall total can hide differences in who can access the service.
  8. Program manager: I can raise that measurement question without claiming that the current program has solved access disparities.
  9. Budget analyst: Good. The justification should distinguish the purpose of funding from evidence of achieved outcomes.
  10. Program manager: I'll bring the activity information, intended outcomes, and open measurement questions to the budget discussion.
2. More appointments, unchanged waiting time

Separate fictional case: a service office delivered 1,200 appointments this quarter, up from 1,000, while measured average waiting time remained ten days.

  1. Program director: We delivered twelve hundred appointments instead of one thousand. Can we describe that as faster access?
  2. Performance analyst: Not from these figures. Average waiting time remained ten days, so the access-speed measure did not improve.
  3. Program director: Then appointment volume is the output, while waiting time reflects a different aspect of service performance?
  4. Performance analyst: Yes. One counts activity, while the other measures how long people waited for it.
  5. Program director: The extra appointments still represent work, but they don't support the specific claim that access became faster.
  6. Performance analyst: Exactly. We can recognize higher volume without substituting it for the outcome measure.
  7. Program director: Should the appropriation discussion consider whether demand also increased during the same quarter?
  8. Performance analyst: Yes, as a relevant question. We haven't supplied demand figures, so don't invent an explanation yet.
  9. Program director: I'll present both measures and ask what else is needed to understand the public value of the increase.
  10. Performance analyst: That will give decision makers a clearer account than calling every higher count an improvement.
3. A citywide average hides access questions

Separate fictional case: a fictional digital-service pilot reports a citywide completion rate but has not examined outcomes for people using assisted access.

  1. Service transformation lead: The citywide completion rate is our main outcome measure. Does that fully describe whether the pilot improved access?
  2. Equity analyst: It describes the measured overall result, but we haven't examined people using assisted access separately.
  3. Service transformation lead: Could you explain why that matters without assuming the pilot disadvantaged that group?
  4. Equity analyst: An average combines experiences. Equity analysis asks whether access or outcomes differ across relevant groups.
  5. Service transformation lead: So it raises a question about distribution of benefit, not an automatic finding of unfairness.
  6. Equity analyst: Exactly. We need appropriate data and definitions before drawing a conclusion about any group.
  7. Service transformation lead: I'll avoid saying the overall rate proves equally successful access for everyone in the city.
  8. Equity analyst: Good. Explain what the measure covers and what remains unknown in the budget justification.
  9. Service transformation lead: Then we'll ask for an appropriate comparison of assisted and unassisted access within the pilot.
  10. Equity analyst: That can make the public-value discussion more informative while preserving the limits of the current evidence.
