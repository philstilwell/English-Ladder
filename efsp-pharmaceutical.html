03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. A laboratory signal is not patient benefit

Fictional development-slide review: a laboratory result is promising, but no clinical benefit has been established.

  1. Development lead: This slide says our candidate will benefit patients. What evidence supports that wording?
  2. Research scientist: We observed the intended response in the laboratory system, not in patients.
  3. Development lead: Could we describe that as confirmation of the proposed mechanism of action, or MOA?
  4. Research scientist: That is still too strong. The result supports our hypothesis under these experimental conditions.
  5. Development lead: Then how should we connect it to the target product profile, the TPP?
  6. Research scientist: Present the TPP as a development target, with clinical benefit listed as unestablished.
  7. Development lead: Should the indication remain on the slide as the proposed patient population?
  8. Research scientist: Yes, provided we label it proposed and avoid implying an approved use.
  9. Development lead: I'll replace the benefit claim and separate evidence from the development objective.
  10. Research scientist: I'll check that the laboratory conditions and remaining evidence gaps stay visible.
2. Two indications, one evidence package

Separate fictional portfolio discussion: candidate P7 has laboratory data for indication A; indication B has only a biological rationale.

  1. Portfolio manager: Can we put indications A and B at the same development stage?
  2. Translational scientist: Not on this evidence. P7 has laboratory data for A and a rationale for B.
  3. Portfolio manager: The proposed MOA is similar. Doesn't that support the same conclusion for both?
  4. Translational scientist: It supports asking the question, not assuming that the results will transfer.
  5. Portfolio manager: How do we explain the difference without making B sound irrelevant?
  6. Translational scientist: Call B an exploratory opportunity and identify the experiment needed to assess it.
  7. Portfolio manager: Would a separate TPP make that distinction clearer for the committee?
  8. Translational scientist: Possibly, but its targets must not appear to be measured product characteristics.
  9. Portfolio manager: I'll show observed evidence for A beside the unanswered questions for B.
  10. Translational scientist: Good. The committee can discuss priorities without confusing potential with demonstrated performance.
3. Readiness for discussion versus readiness for filing

Separate fictional project check-in: an IND planning discussion is scheduled, but the supporting evidence inventory is incomplete.

  1. Project manager: The calendar says IND readiness. Can I report that the package is ready?
  2. Regulatory scientist: No. That is the meeting topic; our evidence inventory is still incomplete.
  3. Project manager: I understood the title to mean an investigational new drug submission was imminent.
  4. Regulatory scientist: We're reviewing gaps before proposing a filing schedule, not confirming a submission date.
  5. Project manager: Which part of the update can I state with confidence today?
  6. Regulatory scientist: The planning discussion is scheduled. The package assessment and timing remain open.
  7. Project manager: Should I include the proposed indication and TPP in that discussion?
  8. Regulatory scientist: Yes. They explain what we intend to develop and why particular evidence matters.
  9. Project manager: I'll correct the milestone wording and avoid treating a meeting as a completed assessment.
  10. Regulatory scientist: I'll bring the inventory and clearly distinguish available documents from expected ones.

Scenario 2 · An unmet-need claim without a source

In a fictional portfolio meeting, a slide says all existing options fail patients. The team has interviewed six clinicians, but no comparative evidence review is available. Discuss wording that preserves the development rationale without claiming universal failure.

Role A: Development strategist: explain what the interviews support and propose a qualified unmet-need statement.

Role B: Medical reviewer: challenge the universal wording and ask which evidence is still needed.

Possible opening: “The six interviews identify concerns, but they don't establish that every existing option fails.”

Success checks
  • Distinguish reported concerns from a general clinical conclusion.
  • Connect the proposed indication or TPP to an unanswered evidence question.
  • Agree on a spoken claim that retains the limitation.
Add a complication

The presenter wants to keep the absolute claim because the speaking slot is only one minute.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
  2. B. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  3. C. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.
  2. B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  3. C. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A trial slide highlights a secondary endpoint while the primary endpoint result needs explanation. The audience may mistake the highlighted result for the main study conclusion.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

Primary endpoint
Main outcome measure used to evaluate the primary objective.
Secondary endpoint
Additional outcome measure supporting other objectives.
Estimand
Precise description of the treatment effect being estimated.
Confidence interval
An interval produced by a method with a stated long-run coverage rate under its assumptions.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

Vocabulary for this lesson

AE
Adverse event; unfavorable medical occurrence after product use, regardless of causality.
SAE
Serious adverse event meeting criteria such as death, life-threatening event, hospitalization, disability, or birth defect.

Process for identifying information that may suggest a new or changed risk.

Integrated assessment of benefits, risks, uncertainty, disease severity, and alternatives.

03 · Notice the language

Transfer information and responsibility

State the current situation, relevant background, open task, and receiving role. Ask for acknowledgment or repeat-back of the critical item. Sending a message and transferring responsibility are not always the same thing.

  • The current status is ...; the outstanding item is ... .
  • The record shows ... .
  • Please confirm who has accepted ... .

Improve this: Someone should follow up on this.

See a clearer version

Please confirm which team has accepted the follow-up task.

The revised sentence asks for an identifiable receiving team instead of leaving responsibility with an unspecified 'someone'.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence checks that responsibility has been accepted?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check.

  1. A. I mentioned the check in my earlier email. Mentioning a task does not show that anyone accepted it.
  2. B. The check appears somewhere in the notes. A written record alone does not identify who will act.
  3. C. Please confirm who will complete the outstanding check. It asks for an identifiable person or team responsible for the task.
2. Which response best checks a critical handoff detail?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct?

  1. A. I saw the message, so no clarification is needed. Receiving a message is not the same as confirming its meaning.
  2. B. So the result is pending and your team will follow up; is that correct? This repeats the status and responsibility and invites correction.
  3. C. I assume all results are final, so we can finish. It changes pending information into a final result.

Your goal

Distinguish completed work from pending work and explain its impact.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A quality review identifies missing documentation in a batch record. A manager asks whether the batch can be described as fully reviewed.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

CMC
Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls information supporting product quality.
Batch record
Documented record of manufacturing steps, controls, and results for a batch.
OOS
Out of specification result requiring investigation.
CAPA
Corrective and preventive action addressing root cause and recurrence prevention.

03 · Notice the language

Give a useful status update

Lead with the current state, then the important change or open item. Use completed-action language only for completed work. Add a next update point when it is known; do not invent a date to make the message sound complete.

  • We have completed ...; ... remains open.
  • As of ..., the status is ... .
  • The next update will cover ... .

Improve this: Everything is progressing well.

See a clearer version

Three checks are complete; two remain open and are being reviewed.

The revision gives observable progress. A positive label alone does not tell the reader what is complete or still needed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence says a review is finished?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The team has completed the review.

  1. A. The team plans to complete the review. 'Plans to complete' describes an intention, not completion.
  2. B. The team has completed the review. 'Has completed' states that the action is finished.
  3. C. The team is completing the review. 'Is completing' presents the work as in progress.
2. Which update distinguishes a plan from a confirmed fact?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it.

  1. A. Delivery is planned for Friday; the carrier has not confirmed it. This explicitly labels the date as planned and identifies the missing confirmation.
  2. B. Delivery is confirmed for Friday because we planned it. A plan does not establish carrier confirmation.
  3. C. The delivery plan proves that arrival will be Friday. A plan cannot prove a future arrival time.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. A batch review with missing documentation

Fictional quality meeting: documentation is missing from a batch record, and a manager asks whether review is complete.

  1. Production manager: Can I describe the batch as fully reviewed in today's supply update?
  2. Quality reviewer: Not yet. The batch record review identified missing documentation that remains unresolved.
  3. Production manager: The manufacturing steps are complete. Isn't fully reviewed a reasonable shorthand for that?
  4. Quality reviewer: Those are different statuses. Completed production doesn't establish completed quality review or authorized disposition.
  5. Production manager: What wording will explain the situation without implying a confirmed product defect?
  6. Quality reviewer: Say production is complete and document review remains open because supporting information is missing.
  7. Production manager: Should I call this OOS in the update to emphasize its importance?
  8. Quality reviewer: No. An out-of-specification result is not established by the documentation gap described here.
  9. Production manager: I'll keep the supply forecast conditional and request the next review-status checkpoint.
  10. Quality reviewer: I'll provide that checkpoint and identify what remains outstanding without predicting the disposition decision.
2. An OOS investigation is not a release decision

Separate fictional quality exercise: one test result is recorded as OOS, the investigation is open, and no disposition is authorized.

  1. Supply planner: The investigation has started. Does that mean the batch will be available this week?
  2. Quality investigator: No. One result is out of specification, and the investigation and disposition remain open.
  3. Supply planner: Can I remove the delay from the supply dashboard while you repeat the test?
  4. Quality investigator: We haven't agreed a repeat test here. Don't assume a particular investigation step or outcome.
  5. Supply planner: What completed action can I report to the commercial planning team today?
  6. Quality investigator: The OOS result has been logged and assigned for investigation. Availability is not confirmed.
  7. Supply planner: Would describing it as a CMC quality issue help explain the dependency?
  8. Quality investigator: Yes, with the specific status. Broad terminology shouldn't hide the unresolved batch decision.
  9. Supply planner: I'll show an unconfirmed supply date and keep any alternative plan separate.
  10. Quality investigator: I'll update you when the authorized review changes status, not merely when an activity is scheduled.
3. CAPA implementation versus demonstrated effectiveness

Separate fictional CAPA review: a revised batch-record checklist is in use, but the planned effectiveness check has not occurred.

  1. Quality manager: The new checklist is in use. Can we close the corrective and preventive action?
  2. CAPA owner: Implementation is complete, but the planned effectiveness check hasn't happened yet.
  3. Quality manager: Staff say the checklist is clearer. Doesn't that demonstrate that the issue is resolved?
  4. CAPA owner: It is useful feedback, but it doesn't replace the agreed evidence for effectiveness.
  5. Quality manager: How should the progress report distinguish those two stages of the work?
  6. CAPA owner: List the revised checklist as implemented and the effectiveness assessment as pending.
  7. Quality manager: Could we describe the batch-record problem as eliminated across every production line?

  9. Quality manager: I'll keep that scope visible and ask when the assessment results can be reviewed.

Your goal

State a concern respectfully and propose a practical route forward.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A draft promotional slide uses stronger benefit language than the approved material supplied for review.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

Prescribing information
Approved product information describing indication, dosing, warnings, adverse reactions, and other use information.
Fair balance
Balanced communication of benefits and risks so the message is not misleading.
MLR
Medical, legal, and regulatory review of materials.
Scientific exchange
Non-promotional scientific communication, usually handled within medical affairs boundaries.

03 · Notice the language

Disagree with a useful reason

Acknowledge the goal without pretending agreement. Name the specific problem and its implication, then ask a focused question or propose a next step. Be direct when a safety or ethical boundary requires it.

  • I understand the goal. My concern is ... .
  • That statement goes beyond ... .
  • Could we ... before confirming ...?

Improve this: That idea is ridiculous.

See a clearer version

My concern is that the proposal assumes capacity we have not confirmed.

The revised sentence identifies a testable concern about the proposal. It leaves room for a constructive response.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which response identifies a specific assumption and evidence against it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open.

  1. A. The proposal assumes the review is complete; the record shows it is still open. It connects the concern to a specific assumption and record.
  2. B. The proposal appears optimistic, and I would prefer a slower approach. This expresses a preference, but does not identify the assumption or the record that contradicts it.
  3. C. I would like another discussion before we proceed with the proposal. This requests discussion without stating the specific assumption or evidence.
2. Which follow-up makes disagreement actionable?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan?

  1. A. Could we confirm the plan and discuss the missing approval later? This confirms the plan before resolving the stated approval condition.
  2. B. Could we ask for general feedback before sending the next update? General feedback does not directly address the missing approval.
  3. C. Could we resolve the missing approval before confirming the plan? It names the unresolved condition and a practical next step.

Three conversations at work

1. A stronger adjective than the reviewed material

Fictional promotional review: a draft slide makes a stronger benefit claim than the supplied approved material.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
  2. B. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
  3. C. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.
  2. B. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.
  3. C. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Trial findings beside observational evidence

Fictional market-access rehearsal: trial findings and observational data appear together without study-design labels.

  1. Market access manager: The presentation combines these results. Why do we need separate study-design labels?
  2. HEOR scientist: Trial findings and observational analyses have different conditions and limitations.
  3. Market access manager: Does that mean the observational information has no place here?
  4. HEOR scientist: No. Health economics and outcomes research can use it, with its source and limits explained.
  5. Market access manager: How do I distinguish real-world data, RWD, from real-world evidence, RWE?
  6. HEOR scientist: RWD are routinely collected healthcare and patient data. RWE is clinical evidence about use, benefits, or risks derived from analyzing those data.
  7. Market access manager: Can I present both sources as equally establishing that the product caused the outcome?
  8. HEOR scientist: Not without examining the designs. Putting results together doesn't make their causal interpretations equivalent.
  9. Market access manager: I'll label each source and separate findings from any formulary recommendation.
  10. HEOR scientist: I'll check that our explanation of the limitations matches the actual analyses.
2. A formulary decision is not a universal access promise

Separate fictional launch meeting: one organization's formulary listing is confirmed, while patient-level access conditions have not been reviewed.

  1. Launch coordinator: We have a formulary listing. Can the field update say access is now unrestricted?
  2. Access specialist: No. The listing is confirmed, but we haven't reviewed the patient-level access conditions.
  3. Launch coordinator: I thought formulary meant everyone covered by that organization could obtain the product immediately.
  4. Access specialist: It identifies inclusion on a medicine list; the actual terms need separate checking.
  5. Launch coordinator: What distinction should the update make for colleagues planning launch activities this week?
  6. Access specialist: State the confirmed listing and flag that operational access details remain under review.
  7. Launch coordinator: Could HEOR results tell us those administrative conditions without obtaining the listing documents?

3. Database size versus a usable comparison

  1. Business development lead: Fifty thousand records sounds persuasive. Can we say the evidence package is already strong?
