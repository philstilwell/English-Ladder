5. Finish with two new four-choice collocation questions for later review, with a clearly separate answer key. Do not claim to schedule a reminder.

Lesson 01: Clarifying the visit at check-in
Original fictional case: The schedule lists an annual review for patient Alexandra, whose verified record has already been matched through the clinic process. The patient prefers Alex and expected to discuss a recent concern. Assistant Sam cannot decide whether the appointment type is suitable. Nurse Dana is available to clarify the visit plan. No decision about the concern, billing, urgency, or an additional appointment has been supplied. Sam must acknowledge the expectation, preserve accurate identification, use Alex in conversation, and ask Dana to clarify the clinical plan.
Language workshop: The schedule and the expectation
Communication goal: Clarify a mismatch between a scheduled visit and a patient concern while respecting the preferred name.
Language guidance: An appointment label describes a scheduled category, not a clinical judgment about every concern raised that day. Do not promise that the concern will be resolved, reject it automatically, or invent a billing outcome from the schedule alone.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): Thank you, Alex; I will use that name. / The schedule lists an annual review. / You expected to discuss a recent concern. / The recorded visit type and your expectation are not the same. / I cannot decide whether this appointment type meets that need. / Nurse Dana can clarify the visit plan. / We will keep the verified record linked correctly. / Let us clarify the plan before assuming how the mismatch happened. / I cannot determine charges from this appointment label alone. / I will include your stated concern in the handoff. / We can use the appropriate setting for personal details. / I cannot promise a particular clinical outcome. / Dana, can you clarify the visit plan with Alex? / The verified record says Alexandra; the preferred spoken name is Alex. / We should use the correctly matched existing record. / I will explain the mismatch to Dana so you do not have to start from nothing.
Vocabulary: check-in: Arrival process confirming a patient and the scheduled visit. / appointment type: Category describing the scheduled purpose or format of a visit. / annual review: Periodic review visit recorded as occurring yearly. / presenting concern: Issue the patient brings to the current visit. / chief complaint: Clinical-record term for the main reason for seeking care. / visit agenda: Topics planned for discussion during the encounter. / preferred name: Name a person asks others to use when addressing them. / recorded name: Name shown in the relevant patient record. / patient identifier: Information used to match a person with the correct record. / demographic details: Administrative identity and contact information in a record. / registration: Creation or updating of required administrative patient information. / encounter: A particular interaction or visit involving healthcare services. / appointment slot: Reserved time in a schedule. / scheduled purpose: Reason for a visit as recorded in the booking. / patient expectation: What a patient believes will happen during a service or visit. / clinical suitability: Whether a plan or appointment is appropriate for the clinical need. / scope of practice: Activities permitted within a role under applicable requirements. / delegation: Assignment of an activity under the applicable professional rules. / supervision: Required oversight of work by an appropriate professional. / clinical handoff: Transfer of relevant information to a receiving clinical professional. / visit plan: Intended approach to the current appointment. / billing category: Classification used for charging or reimbursement purposes. / identity match: Confirmation that a person corresponds to the correct record. / name pronunciation: The way a person's name is spoken.
Speaking task: Read Alex and Sam's corrected exchange. Stress annual review, recent concern, and Dana; keep the patient concern separate from the booking label. Switch roles. Repeat the privacy request and preferred-name exchange without changing the verified identity facts. Complete Morgan's four-line exchange, check the key, then read it aloud with follow-up and initial consultation clearly contrasted.
Partner: Alex: Patient / Sam: Medical assistant
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: The schedule says follow-up. Patient Morgan expected an initial consultation. Assistant Lee cannot decide suitability. Nurse Inez is available to clarify the plan.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Alex: Thank you. I do not need you to change the appointment yourself; I need someone qualified to clarify what today's visit can include.
Sam: That is the visit plan question for Dana. I will not turn the appointment label into a refusal or a guarantee before that discussion.
Alex: All right. Can you let Dana know I am here? I would like to settle the visit plan before I explain everything again.
Sam: Correct. I will record the appointment type accurately and pass on your expectation. We will keep the two facts clear while Dana clarifies the next step.

Lesson 02: Rooming and factual patient reports
Original fictional case: During rooming, patient Pat says, "I have felt tired since Monday." Assistant Rosa observed Pat answering questions while seated. Rosa has not assessed the cause or severity, and no measurement values are supplied. Nurse Dana is receiving the report. The conversation must preserve the onset as reported by Pat, state the limited observation, and avoid filling missing fields with normal values. Laboratory clerk: Please keep the reference attached, so a reply about T18 isn't applied to another request.
Assistant: Agreed. I'll also avoid calling either description incorrect until we receive the clarification.
Laboratory clerk: That leaves a clear open question: what the current order requests, not what we think the abbreviation probably means.
Vocabulary in this exchange: specimen: A sample of material from a patient submitted for examination or testing. / requisition: A document or electronic request specifying the laboratory testing requested by a clinician.
