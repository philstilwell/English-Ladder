Read the story

TV presenter Alison Hammond experienced a health scare during a live broadcast last week. She quickly left the programme, clutching her chest, and went to the hospital for a check-up. Doctors confirmed she had suffered a panic attack, not a heart attack, which she described as "probably one of the most scariest things I've ever been through". Hammond explained she couldn't catch her breath and genuinely believed she was having a heart attack on air. She added that the experience felt very real, like "a wave" of adrenaline overwhelming her body. Upon returning to her weekly slot, she expressed gratitude, saying the event had given her a new perspective on life. She even compared the feeling to entering a place, like the "Upside Down world" from the TV show Stranger Things. She admitted she was anxious about returning to the show, fearing that if she didn't come back immediately, she might never do so. Panic attacks are an extreme anxiety response that can cause both physical and mental symptoms. These attacks can mimic heart attack symptoms such as chest pain, but heart attack pain often spreads to the arm or jaw. To manage a panic attack, experts advise focusing on the present moment, one's surroundings, and steadying one's breathing.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. health scare (noun phrase): a sudden feeling of fear or worry about your health.
2. broadcast (noun): a program on television or radio.
3. clutching (verb, present participle): holding something tightly.
4. suffered (verb, past participle): experienced something bad or unpleasant.
5. genuinely (adverb): truly and sincerely.
6. perspective (noun): a particular way of thinking about something; a point of view.
7. admitted (verb, past tense): accepted that something is true or that you have done something wrong.
8. extreme (adjective): very great in degree or intensity.
9. mimic (verb): to copy the way someone or something acts or behaves; to imitate.
10. manage (verb): to succeed in dealing with or controlling something.

Grammar Focus: Reporting Verbs (Past Simple with 'that' clause)
Reporting verbs, like 'explained' or 'said,' are used to share what someone else has communicated without using their exact words. When reporting past events, the verb in the reported clause often changes tense. For instance, a present action might become past, or a past action might become past perfect. The word 'that' often introduces the reported clause, but it can sometimes be omitted.
Example from the text: "Hammond explained she couldn't catch her breath and genuinely believed she was having a heart attack on air."
