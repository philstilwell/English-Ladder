01Confirming openings and measurement referencesAsk what the dimension describes · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Distinguish a client's usable-space requirement from a drawing dimension.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

The hall drawing labels a door opening 900 millimeters. The client assumes this is clear passage width. The note does not say rough or finished opening; no door has been ordered. The designer can clarify the reference.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

rough opening
The framed space intended to receive a door or window unit before surrounding finishes are complete.
finished opening
The opening measured between specified completed surrounding surfaces; this is not automatically the usable clear passage with a door installed and open.
jamb
A side or top member of a door or window frame.
datum
An agreed reference point or line from which project dimensions are measured.

Useful word combinations

  • verify the opening: confirm which opening and measurement basis are meant
  • measure between finished faces: state the surfaces that bound a dimension
  • allow for the jamb: include the frame in the dimensional discussion
  • establish a datum: agree the reference used for dimensions

C Notice the language

Ask what the dimension describes

An opening dimension needs units and reference surfaces. Rough opening, finished opening, door-leaf width, and usable clear passage are different quantities. Even a finished opening measurement may not establish passage with the door and hardware present. Ask which requirement matters before discussing an order size.

  • Is ___ measured between ___ or ___?
  • When you say clear width, do you mean ___?
  • We have the dimension, but still need ___.

Improve this: "The door is nine hundred, so you have nine hundred clear."

See a clearer version

The note says 900 millimeters; it does not establish the clear passage width.

The revision preserves the number while avoiding an unsupported promise about usable space.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: I need 900 millimeters for a cabinet to pass with the door open. Which dimension matters?
Carpenter: We must confirm the ____; the finished opening alone may not establish it.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. usable clear passage

  1. A. usable clear passage Correct. This is the unobstructed space available with the door in its relevant position.
  2. B. rough opening The rough opening is the framed space, not the unobstructed passage through a completed doorway.
  3. C. jamb thickness Jamb thickness describes part of the frame, not the remaining usable passage.
  4. D. door leaf width Door leaf width describes the door panel, not necessarily the unobstructed passage when open.
2. Designer: What is missing from the dimension?
Carpenter: We need to know ____ measured from.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. which reference line it is

  1. A. which reference line is it Direct-question word order does not follow 'know' here.
  2. B. which it reference line is The noun phrase 'reference line' must stay together.
  3. C. which reference line does it 'Does' does not form this passive phrase; use 'it is measured'.
  4. D. which reference line it is Correct. The embedded question uses subject before verb: it is.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Nine hundred between which faces?

At the hall doorway, the carpenter and client clarify the fictional 900-millimeter note.

  1. Carpenter: The hall drawing says nine hundred millimeters. Were you expecting that much clear passage?
  2. Client: Yes, our cabinet needs that space, and I thought the number guaranteed it.
  3. Carpenter: The note doesn't say whether it measures the rough opening or finished opening.
  4. Client: I hadn't realized those were different. Does the jamb take up some space?
  5. Carpenter: The frame and open door affect usable passage; we need the selected unit's dimensions.
  6. Client: We haven't chosen the door yet. Can you still clarify the drawing today?
  7. Carpenter: I can ask the designer which surfaces the number refers to before ordering.
  8. Client: Please mention the cabinet, because usable width is the requirement that matters most.
  9. Carpenter: I'll separate your passage requirement from the unresolved drawing dimension in that question.
  10. Client: That helps. Let's leave the order pending until those two things agree.
2. The floor that is not finished yet

A carpenter checks a cabinet-height reference with the site coordinator.

  1. Carpenter: Your cabinet sketch gives a height, but which floor surface is the datum?
  2. Site coordinator: The finished floor should be the reference, not the exposed subfloor visible today.
  3. Carpenter: Thanks. The supplier's survey note says existing floor, which could mean something different.
  4. Site coordinator: That survey happened before the floor finish was selected. I see the problem.
  5. Carpenter: Could you confirm the current floor build-up with the designer before fabrication release?
  6. Site coordinator: Yes, and I'll ask them to identify the finished floor reference on the sketch.
  7. Carpenter: Please keep the earlier survey attached so the supplier can see what changed.
  8. Site coordinator: Should I describe the old height as wrong, or simply as needing reconciliation?
  9. Carpenter: Needing reconciliation is more accurate; the two documents may use different reference surfaces.
  10. Site coordinator: I'll request a coordinated dimension, with fabrication release still pending that response.
3. A width without its units

A carpenter and supplier resolve an ambiguous shelf enquiry by telephone.

  1. Carpenter: Your quotation lists the alcove shelf as thirty, but it doesn't show units.
  2. Supplier: That's thirty inches of nominal width. Were you reading the number as centimeters?
  3. Carpenter: I was checking because our site measurements are recorded in millimeters throughout this job.
  4. Supplier: Understood. Is your figure the wall opening or the finished shelf length requested?
  5. Carpenter: It's the opening measurement; the shelf size still needs confirmation against the detail.
  6. Supplier: Then I shouldn't simply convert thirty inches and treat that as your order.
  7. Carpenter: Exactly. Please label the quotation dimension and wait for the confirmed fabrication size.
  8. Supplier: I can revise the description now without releasing any material to the workshop.
  9. Carpenter: Please also retain the unit labels when you send the revised quotation over.
  10. Supplier: Done. It will say nominal width in inches, with fabrication dimensions awaiting confirmation.

Scenario 2 · The alcove narrows at the back

An alcove survey records front width 1,210 millimeters and rear width 1,190 millimeters. A client calls it a 1,200-millimeter opening. No shelf fabrication size is approved.

Role A: Carpenter: distinguish the two measured locations from the shorthand description.

Role B: Client: you want full-depth shelves and can show the survey sketch.

Possible opening: “Is twelve hundred the front measurement, the back measurement, or a rounded description?”

Success checks
  • Name both locations and dimensions aloud.
  • Explain why an average is not a confirmed fabrication size.
  • Agree who will clarify the shelf detail before ordering.
Add a complication

The client says a single average would be simpler for the order.

Check your reasoning

02Selecting timber, trim, and visible finishesCompare the properties that matter · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare two finish choices without treating one feature as an overall verdict.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

For a fictional study remodel, sample A is solid oak trim with pronounced grain and a ten-day quoted lead time. Sample B is oak-veneered trim with a more uniform appearance and a four-day quoted lead time. Both match the requested profile. No sample is selected.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

grain
The direction and visible pattern of fibers in wood.
veneer
A thin layer of wood bonded to a supporting material to provide a wood surface.
profile
The cross-sectional shape of a molding or trim piece.
finish sample
A physical example used to compare a proposed surface color, sheen, or texture.

Useful word combinations

  • match the existing profile: compare trim shapes, not just overall width
  • approve a finish sample: accept a particular reference for appearance
  • follow the grain: describe the visible direction of wood fibers
  • compare like for like: use the same dimensions and finish conditions

C Notice the language

Compare the properties that matter

A profile is shape; grain and sheen describe different aspects of appearance. Use the same criteria for both samples. A quoted lead time is supplier information, not a promise about the entire remodeling schedule.

  • Both samples ___, whereas only ___ .
  • The difference in appearance is ___ .
  • The quoted lead time is ___; the site date remains ___.

Improve this: "The quicker trim is better."

See a clearer version

The quicker option matches the profile but has a more uniform grain appearance.

The revised comparison names a tradeoff instead of declaring a universal winner.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: Are the bead and curved edge the same shape on both samples?
Carpenter: Yes, they match the same ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. profile

  1. A. grain direction Grain direction concerns wood fibers rather than the molding's curved shape.
  2. B. surface sheen Surface sheen describes reflected appearance rather than the shape of the edge.
  3. C. veneer species Veneer species identifies the wood layer's type rather than the molding's shape.
  4. D. profile Correct. Profile identifies the cross-sectional shape of the molding.
2. Client: How do the quoted times compare?
Carpenter: A takes ten days, ____ B is quoted at four.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. whereas

  1. A. because 'Because' supplies an unsupported causal relationship.
  2. B. despite 'Despite' needs a noun phrase or an -ing form here, not a full clause.
  3. C. whereas Correct. 'Whereas' introduces a contrast.
  4. D. therefore 'Therefore' implies that A's timing causes B's timing.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Choosing the study trim reference

At a sample review, a carpenter and client compare the two study trim options.

  1. Carpenter: Both samples match your trim profile. Which difference catches your eye in this light?
  2. Client: Sample A has stronger grain. Is sample B a different shape around the edge?
  3. Carpenter: No, the shape matches. B has an oak veneer and a more uniform appearance.
  4. Client: I prefer A's grain, but the room is needed soon. What about delivery?
  5. Carpenter: The supplier quotes ten days for A and four for B at present.
  6. Client: Does choosing B mean the entire study will be finished six days earlier?
  7. Carpenter: Not necessarily. Those figures concern supply; the site schedule also includes other work.
  8. Client: Then keep A as my preferred sample while you check the scheduling effect.
  9. Carpenter: I'll label it preferred, with the timing decision open, rather than as an order.
  10. Client: Yes, show me that effect before we make the final selection for the room.
2. White is not a complete finish description

A carpenter and decorator compare a trim sample with an existing painted surface.

  1. Carpenter: The client asked for white trim, but this sample looks glossier than the existing skirting.
  2. Decorator: The color reference matches. The sheen is different, which changes how light reflects.
  3. Carpenter: Could we show both together before calling this a successful finish match today?
  4. Decorator: Certainly. Is the client asking for the same color or the same overall appearance?
  5. Carpenter: Their note says match existing, so I think we need to clarify both.

03Reading details and resolving drawing conflictsMake a drawing question answerable · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Request a specific clarification with references, location, and consequence.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

Cabinet elevation C4 revision B shows a 20-millimeter reveal. Section S2 revision B shows 15 millimeters at the same end panel. Fabrication release is due for review at 14:00. The designer has not clarified which dimension applies.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

elevation
A drawing showing a vertical face of a room, wall, or assembly.
section detail
A drawing showing a cut-through view of an assembly and its relationships.
scribe allowance
Extra material specified so a component can be fitted to an adjoining irregular surface.
reveal
An intentionally visible strip, gap, or setback between adjoining finished components.

Useful word combinations

  • check the latest revision: confirm the current drawing issue
  • raise a drawing query: ask for clarification of a specific conflict
  • maintain an even reveal: describe a consistent visible spacing
  • reconcile the detail: obtain one consistent interpretation of conflicting information

C Notice the language

Make a drawing question answerable

A useful query lets the designer find the conflict without guessing. Name both documents and their revisions, describe the same physical location, and ask for the intended relationship. A review deadline is not permission to choose a dimension.

  • On ___ revision ___, the dimension is ___ .
  • Could you reconcile ___ with ___?
  • This affects ___; please confirm ___.

Improve this: "The cabinet drawings are confusing."

See a clearer version

C4 and S2, both revision B, give different reveal dimensions at the end panel.

The revised statement locates the disagreement and avoids criticizing the whole drawing set.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Designer: Is the discrepancy on different issues?
Carpenter: No, ____ show revision B.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. both drawings

  1. A. neither drawing 'Neither' denies the shared revision and also requires 'shows'.
  2. B. each drawing 'Each drawing' requires the singular verb 'shows'.
  3. C. both drawings Correct. 'Both drawings show' confirms the two matching revision labels.
  4. D. either drawing 'Either drawing' normally takes 'shows' and does not directly state both labels.
2. Designer: Is the query about panel size or the intentionally visible strip between finished faces?
Carpenter: The ____ is shown as 20 millimeters on C4 and 15 on S2.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. reveal

  1. A. edge profile Edge profile describes the edge shape, not the dimension of the visible strip.
  2. B. reveal Correct. Reveal names the intentionally visible strip or setback at this junction.
  3. C. scribe allowance Scribe allowance is extra material for fitting, not the intended visible strip.
  4. D. overall panel width Overall panel width describes the entire panel, not the strip between finished faces.

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

04Discussing concealed conditions without guessingDescribe a finding and its limits · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Report a concealed-condition concern without diagnosing damage or minimizing it.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

An existing exposed subfloor has dark staining beside the laundry doorway. The client asks whether the joists are rotten. The carpenter has not assessed structural condition and the joists are not visible. The project lead can arrange a qualified assessment.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

subfloor
The supporting floor layer beneath the finished floor covering.
joist
A horizontal structural member that supports a floor or ceiling.
blocking
Short framing pieces between other members, used for specified support or restraint.
concealed condition
An existing site condition that was not visible during the initial assessment.

Useful word combinations

  • report visible staining: describe appearance without naming a cause
  • identify the affected area: locate the observation precisely
  • separate evidence from inference: distinguish what is seen from what is assumed
  • request a condition assessment: ask the appropriate specialist to evaluate the finding

C Notice the language

Describe a finding and its limits

Location, visibility, and source matter. A stain is an observation; decay and structural adequacy are conclusions requiring appropriate assessment. Keep urgent site concerns within the project's safety arrangements rather than treating this exercise as an inspection procedure.

  • I can see ___ at ___ .
  • That does not establish whether ___ .
  • The assessment needs to address ___ before ___.

Improve this: "The dark patch means the joists are rotten."

See a clearer version

There is dark staining on the subfloor; the joist condition has not been assessed.

The revision avoids turning surface appearance into a structural conclusion.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: Can you see the joists from here?
Carpenter: No, they remain ____ beneath this area.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. concealed

  1. A. directly observable Directly observable would claim that the joists can be seen from here.
  2. B. concealed Correct. Concealed means the joists are hidden from this view.
  3. C. exposed Exposed would mean they are uncovered, contrary to the stated condition.
  4. D. fully visible Fully visible contradicts the limited view described in this case.
2. Project lead: What does the stain prove?
Carpenter: It ____ the cause of the discoloration.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. does not establish

  1. A. does not establish Correct. Appearance alone does not identify cause.
  2. B. has established This overstates the information available.
  3. C. is caused by This would claim a cause, and the remaining phrase does not name one meaningfully.
  4. D. has resulted from This treats the unknown cause as already identified and makes the sentence circular.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. A stain is not a joist assessment

At the laundry doorway, a carpenter answers the client's question about staining.

  1. Carpenter: There's dark staining on the exposed subfloor beside the laundry doorway in this area.
  2. Client: Does that mean the joists underneath are rotten and the whole floor needs replacing?
  3. Carpenter: I can't conclude that. The joists aren't visible, and their condition hasn't been assessed.
  4. Client: We had a washing-machine spill years ago. Could that explain what you're seeing now?
  5. Carpenter: It could be relevant history, but I can't confirm the cause from the stain.
  6. Client: Please include the spill when you speak to the project lead about the assessment.
  7. Carpenter: I will describe it as your recollection, separately from the visible staining we see.
  8. Client: Will that assessment also tell us whether any change to the agreed work is needed?
  9. Carpenter: The assessment can clarify this concealed condition before we discuss a revised carpentry scope.
  10. Client: All right. I'd rather understand the finding than approve replacement based on appearance alone.
2. The photograph does not show the support

A carpenter and project lead discuss an incomplete site photograph.

  1. Carpenter: The client's photograph shows the new shelf location, but not what's behind the wall.
  2. Project lead: They're asking whether the existing blocking can support the cabinet at that position.
  3. Carpenter: We don't have a verified record of blocking there, so the photograph cannot answer.
  4. Project lead: Is there an earlier framing record we can request from the original contractor first?
  5. Carpenter: Yes, I'll ask for that record and identify which wall and cabinet position we mean.
  6. Project lead: Please don't describe the missing record as evidence that no blocking exists at all.
  7. Carpenter: Agreed. I'll say the support arrangement remains unverified, rather than absent or inadequate.
  8. Project lead: The client also wants a fixed installation date. What can we tell them today?
  9. Carpenter: We can give the current planning status and explain which assessment the date depends on.
  10. Project lead: I'll handle that conversation and keep the support question separate from our scheduling preference.
3. A small gap with no agreed tolerance

A carpenter discusses a reported trim gap with the client's representative.

  1. Carpenter: You noted a gap at the stair trim. Which joint are you referring to?
  2. Client representative: The upper corner beside the landing. I photographed it this morning from the hallway.
  3. Carpenter: Thank you. Do we have an agreed sample or acceptance detail for that joint?
  4. Client representative: There's an approved detail, but I haven't compared the photograph with it yet.
  5. Carpenter: Then let's identify the location and request a comparison before calling it acceptable or defective.
  6. Client representative: The owner asked me whether every gap of that size is always a defect.
  7. Carpenter: I wouldn't give a universal answer. The detail and actual condition matter for this review.
  8. Client representative: I'll send the photograph with the drawing reference, without presenting my impression as a finding.
  9. Carpenter: Please also note the viewing location so the reviewer understands what the image shows.
  10. Client representative: Done. We'll describe it as a reported finish concern pending comparison with the agreed detail.

Your goal

Repair an overstatement while agreeing a precise finish-review description.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

At a wardrobe review you said all fronts were flush. The client points to drawer D3, whose left edge projects beyond the neighboring front. You have not assessed the cause or remedy. Correct your earlier statement and add the specific observation for follow-up.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

flush
Aligned in the same plane as an adjoining surface, without an intended step.
plumb
Vertical relative to gravity; distinct from level, which describes a horizontal reference.
snag list
A list of observed unfinished or unsatisfactory items requiring agreed follow-up; commonly called a punch list in U.S. usage.
acceptance sample
An agreed physical reference against which specified aspects of finished work are compared.

Useful word combinations

  • check against the sample: compare the specified feature with the agreed reference
  • identify a proud edge: describe an edge projecting beyond its neighbor
  • agree a snag description: settle the exact location and observed issue
  • confirm the review outcome: distinguish accepted work from items still open

C Notice the language

Correct the claim and specify the item

Use location and relationship rather than a vague judgment such as bad. Acknowledge an inaccurate statement directly. Recording a snag does not by itself identify its cause, the remedy, or the contractual outcome. Snag list and punch list name the same type of follow-up list here.

  • My earlier statement about ___ was too broad.
  • At ___, the edge is ___ relative to ___ .
  • The recorded observation is ___; the remedy remains ___.

Improve this: "You're looking at it from the wrong angle."

See a clearer version

I overlooked D3's projecting edge; that specific item needs review.

The correction addresses the observation instead of dismissing the client's view.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Client: This edge projects beyond the adjacent face, rather than sharing its plane.
Carpenter: Then I should not have described those faces as ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. flush

  1. A. level Level concerns horizontal orientation rather than whether adjacent faces share a plane.
  2. B. square Square concerns a right-angle relationship, not whether the faces share a plane.
  3. C. flush Correct. Flush describes adjoining faces aligned in the same plane.
  4. D. plumb Plumb concerns vertical orientation, which this observation does not establish.
2. Client: Did you mean every drawer was aligned?
Carpenter: Yes, and my earlier statement ____ too broad.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. was

  1. A. being 'Being' needs a finite verb to complete this sentence.
  2. B. was Correct. The singular subject 'statement' takes 'was'.
  3. C. were 'Were' does not agree with this singular subject in an ordinary factual statement.
  4. D. have been Use 'has been' with a singular subject, not 'have been'.

Your goal

Give the owner usable records and an identifiable follow-up contact.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

The owner receives cabinet drawings and the correct finish care sheet. A replacement shelf pin cover is due but not delivered. The joinery lead, Mina, accepts supplier follow-up and will call Friday. The return-visit date is not confirmed; warranty terms remain those in the supplied document.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

as-built record
A record intended to describe the work as actually completed, including documented changes.
care sheet
Product-specific information about looking after a finished material or item.
warranty document
A document stating the provider's warranty terms and conditions for identified work or products.
return visit
A planned later attendance to review or complete identified outstanding work.

Useful word combinations

  • hand over the care sheet: provide the correct product-care information
  • match the product reference: link a document to the item actually supplied
  • schedule a return visit: agree a later attendance for defined work
  • close an outstanding item: record completion after the relevant review

C Notice the language

Hand over what is complete and what remains open

A handover is more useful when each document matches the supplied product and each outstanding item has an owner. A promised update date is not necessarily a delivery or return-visit date. Do not paraphrase warranty terms as guaranteed coverage.

  • This document covers ___, not ___ .
  • ___ has accepted follow-up for ___ .
  • The update is due ___; the visit date is ___.

Improve this: "Mina will sort everything out Friday."

See a clearer version

Mina will call Friday about the cover; the return visit is not booked yet.

The revision separates an update commitment from an unconfirmed attendance.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Owner: Will the cover be fitted Friday?
Carpenter: Friday is the agreed ____ date, not a confirmed return visit.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. update

  1. A. completion An update does not establish completion.
  2. B. update Correct. Mina has agreed to call with status.
  3. C. installation Installation on Friday is not established.
  4. D. delivery The cover's delivery date is unknown.
2. Owner: Which instructions apply to this finish?
Carpenter: Use the care sheet ____ the product actually supplied.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. for

  1. A. for Correct. 'For' links the sheet to its intended product.
  2. B. during 'During' expresses time, not the product reference.
  3. C. among 'Among' concerns membership within a group.
  4. D. toward 'Toward' expresses direction rather than applicability here.
