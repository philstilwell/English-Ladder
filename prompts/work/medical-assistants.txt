TASK: Retrieve occupational vocabulary
1. Select four terms from the reference. Explain each in plain English, give two natural collocations and quote or create a short workplace example. Label invented examples. Contrast each term with a plausible confusable term; acknowledge regional variation.
2. Present one role-labelled exchange with one missing word or phrase and four choices A-D. Exactly one choice must fit the stated facts and grammar. Stop and wait for my answer before revealing it.
3. Explain all four options, then invite me to read the completed exchange aloud. Offer a sentence frame for optional spoken practice, never an open-ended writing assignment.
4. Repeat for the other three terms, one question at a time. Vary the correct letter and the situation; preserve the reference facts. If a choice is ambiguous, replace the item.
5. Finish with two new four-choice collocation questions for later review, with a clearly separate answer key. Do not claim to schedule a reminder.

Lesson 01: Clarifying the visit at check-in
Original fictional case: The schedule says annual review. Patient Alex expected a visit about a recent concern. The assistant cannot decide whether the appointment type is suitable. Nurse Dana can clarify the visit plan. Alex prefers Alex, while the verified record says Alexandra.
Language workshop: Acknowledge the expectation before checking the booking
Communication goal: Describe a visit mismatch without dismissing the patient's reason for attending.
Language guidance: A booking label and the patient's chief concern serve different purposes. Ask what the patient expected, preserve their wording, and leave decisions about the visit's clinical suitability to the team.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The schedule says ___; you expected ___. / How would you describe your main concern today? / I can address you as ___ while verifying the record under ___.
Vocabulary: chief concern: The main reason a patient gives for seeking care at the current visit. / intake form: A clinic document used to gather patient information before or during a visit. / patient identifier: A detail used with the clinic's verification process to distinguish one patient from another. / preferred name: The name a patient asks staff to use when addressing them.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Alex: explain the different expectation and ask staff to use your preferred name.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: Ask whether changing the display name also changes the verified identity record.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Alex, the schedule describes an annual review, but you expected to discuss a recent concern. I will tell Dana about that difference so she can clarify the visit plan with you. I can use Alex when addressing you while keeping the verified record linked to Alexandra. I haven't confirmed a change to the appointment type.

Lesson 02: Rooming and factual patient reports
Original fictional case: During rooming, patient Pat reports tiredness since Monday. The assistant observed Pat answering questions while seated but has not assessed the cause or severity. Nurse Dana is receiving the report. No measurement values or clinical conclusions are supplied.
Language workshop: Separate a report from an interpretation
Communication goal: Attribute symptom language accurately without diagnosing or minimizing.
Language guidance: Use 'reports' for the patient's experience and 'I observed' for what you directly noticed. Neither phrase establishes a diagnosis. Time words such as 'since Monday' and 'on Monday' convey different durations.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The patient reports ___ since ___. / I observed ___ during ___. / The cause and ___ have not been established in this account.
Vocabulary: rooming: Preparing a patient for the clinician's visit through the clinic's delegated intake activities. / vital signs: Basic physiological measurements, such as temperature and pulse, recorded for clinical review. / reported symptom: A bodily experience described by the patient, not necessarily observed by staff. / onset: The time or circumstances in which a reported symptom began.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: Nurse Dana: ask which parts came from Pat and which were directly observed.
Guided response task: Complete a workplace dialogue by selecting one of four supplied options. Compare the feedback and say the completed exchange aloud. Do not write an open-ended message.
Optional second-round challenge: Ask whether answering questions proves the reported concern is minor.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Pat reports feeling tired since Monday. During our conversation, Pat answered questions while seated. That observation does not explain the tiredness or establish its severity. Dana, I am passing on the reported onset and what I directly observed; Pat wants to discuss the change with the clinician. I have not offered an explanation for it.
END REFERENCE

Language workshop: Separate a report from an interpretation
Communication goal: Attribute symptom language accurately without diagnosing or minimizing.
