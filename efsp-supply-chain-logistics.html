01Demand Planning and Forecast BiasMatch certainty to evidence · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

A forecast exceeds actual demand for three months. Sales expects a new promotion to improve demand but has no order evidence yet.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

forecast
Forward-looking estimate based on assumptions, trends, constraints, and known risks.
forecast bias
A systematic tendency for forecasts to be higher or lower than actual results.
demand signal
Information suggesting what customers may require or purchase.
S&OP
Sales and operations planning: coordinating demand, supply, and business plans across teams.

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
  2. B. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  3. C. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.
  2. B. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  3. C. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Three months of overforecast before a promotion

Fictional demand review: the forecast exceeded actual demand for three months; sales expects a promotion to help, but no supporting orders exist yet.

  1. Demand planner: Our forecast exceeded actual demand for three months. That repeated direction suggests an overforecast pattern to review.
  2. Sales manager: The promotion should improve demand. Can we treat the current forecast as confirmed now?
  3. Demand planner: Not yet. We have an expectation about the promotion, but no supporting order evidence.
  4. Sales manager: Does the recent forecast bias prove that the promotion will fail as well?
  5. Demand planner: No. Historical overforecast and the future promotion are different questions, both requiring clear assumptions.
  6. Sales manager: Then I'll describe the promotion uplift as a planning assumption rather than a confirmed demand signal.
  7. Demand planner: Good. We can take a base view and a clearly labeled promotion scenario into S&OP.
  8. Sales manager: What evidence would let us revisit that assumption during the next review?
  9. Demand planner: Verified orders and relevant demand information, with timing and scope clear rather than an optimistic headline.
  10. Sales manager: I'll bring those when available and keep the current expectation separate from actual demand.
2. A one-time order inside the baseline

Separate fictional planning case: last month's demand included a one-time order of 200 units; whether it will repeat is unconfirmed.

  1. Sales analyst: Last month's actual demand includes the two hundred-unit special order. Should it remain in next month's baseline?
  2. Demand planner: We should identify it separately because repeat demand hasn't been confirmed for that order.
  3. Sales analyst: Could we assume it repeats simply because the customer was pleased with delivery?
  4. Demand planner: Customer satisfaction isn't a confirmed order. Keep any repeat expectation labeled as an assumption.
  5. Sales analyst: Would removing it completely prove that the lower forecast is more accurate?
  6. Demand planner: No. We need a justified planning treatment, not certainty about an outcome that hasn't occurred.
  7. Sales analyst: I'll show the one-time quantity separately and ask sales whether any repeat commitment exists.
  8. Demand planner: That gives S&OP a clearer demand signal and avoids hiding a special event in the baseline.
  9. Sales analyst: I'll distinguish confirmed history from the proposed future treatment in the review.
  10. Demand planner: Good. We'll revisit the assumption when evidence arrives instead of treating one month as a permanent pattern.
3. Orders shifted between months are not necessarily growth

Separate fictional demand analysis: 80 units originally requested for June were moved to May; no additional quantity was ordered.

  1. Commercial lead: May's orders increased by eighty units. Can we report that as new demand growth?
  2. Demand analyst: Those eighty were moved from June into May; the total ordered quantity didn't increase.
  3. Commercial lead: So we have a timing change in the demand signal rather than eighty incremental units?
  4. Demand analyst: Correct. Treating them as new demand could also leave the June forecast overstated.
  5. Commercial lead: Does the move prove the customer will buy less over the whole quarter?
  6. Demand analyst: No. The supplied change is timing only, not evidence of a different quarterly total.
  7. Commercial lead: I'll explain the pull-forward and keep the two months linked in the forecast discussion.
  8. Demand analyst: Please preserve the original and revised dates so we can trace the change.
  9. Commercial lead: Should we take the timing assumption into S&OP rather than celebrate it as a sales increase?
  10. Demand analyst: Yes. Report the confirmed schedule movement and leave any wider growth interpretation dependent on additional evidence.

Scenario 2 · A distributor inquiry mistaken for an order

A fictional distributor asks whether 500 units could be available next month but submits no purchase order. Sales wants to add all 500 as confirmed demand. Discuss the distinction and an appropriately labeled planning scenario.

Role A: Demand planner: distinguish the inquiry from a commitment.

Role B: Account manager: explain the potential opportunity and agree how to obtain a clearer demand signal.

Possible opening: “The five hundred units are an availability inquiry, not a confirmed order yet.”

Success checks
  • Keep inquiry quantity separate from committed demand.
  • Avoid predicting whether the order will materialize.
  • Agree a labeled scenario and a specific confirmation request.
Add a complication

The sales lead says a confident tone from the distributor is enough evidence.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

State the action and the information needed. Explain why it matters and specify a deadline only when one is given or agreed. 'Could you' is polite; repeated apologies can obscure a legitimate request. Urgent situations may require a direct request.

  • Could you provide ... so that ...?
  • Please confirm ... by [agreed time].
  • If that timing is not possible, please let me know ... .

Improve this: Please revert soonest with the necessary.

See a clearer version

Could you send the missing document and confirm when it will be available?

The revision names the document and requested response. It avoids expressions that may be unfamiliar or ambiguous for an international audience.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. What does 'Please send it by Thursday' normally mean?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Send it no later than Thursday.

  1. A. Send it on Thursday, but not earlier. “By Thursday” permits earlier delivery; it is not restricted to that day.
  2. B. Keep sending it regularly until Thursday. This interprets a deadline as a continuing activity, which would use “until”.
  3. C. Send it no later than Thursday. 'By' sets the latest point, while 'after' refers to a later time.
2. Which request gives the recipient enough detail to act?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review?

  1. A. Could you confirm that you received my earlier message? This checks receipt, not which version is required for the review.
  2. B. Could you confirm the document version needed for the review? The requested action and information are explicit.
  3. C. Could you confirm that the review is important? This checks importance rather than identifying the needed document version.

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. An acknowledgment without a shipping date

Fictional procurement call: the supplier acknowledged purchase order 612 but provided no shipping date; the planning review is tomorrow afternoon.

  1. Buyer: Thank you for acknowledging purchase order six twelve. Please confirm the shipping date or current constraint.
  2. Supplier coordinator: The acknowledgment confirms receipt of the order, not a committed dispatch date yet.
  3. Buyer: We need a date for tomorrow afternoon's planning review. Can you check and return a status before noon?
  4. Supplier coordinator: I can check with planning this morning and confirm when a reliable update is available.
  5. Buyer: Please distinguish a firm commitment from an estimated lead time in that response.
  6. Supplier coordinator: Understood. I won't turn the standard lead time into a confirmed shipping promise.
  7. Buyer: If allocation is limiting the order, please identify the affected quantity rather than simply say delayed.
  8. Supplier coordinator: I'll ask for that detail, but the current cause isn't confirmed, so I won't assume allocation.
  9. Buyer: Thank you. I'll keep our downstream schedule provisional until we receive the verified status.
  10. Supplier coordinator: Request accepted. I'll return the planning response or explain the remaining uncertainty before your review.
2. A partial shipment needs a quantity and date

Separate fictional supplier case: order 733 is for 400 units; the supplier offers a partial shipment without specifying quantity or dispatch date.

  1. Procurement specialist: You offered a partial shipment against the four hundred units on order seven thirty-three. Please specify quantity and dispatch date.
  2. Supplier account manager: Those details aren't confirmed yet. I can ask our allocation team for the available quantity.
  3. Procurement specialist: Could you also confirm the balance plan so we can assess the offer before accepting it?
  4. Supplier account manager: Yes. Would you accept any available quantity now and leave the remainder open?
  5. Procurement specialist: We need the actual split and timing first; otherwise we can't evaluate the impact on our schedule.
  6. Supplier account manager: I'll request both the proposed first shipment and the remaining commitment from planning.
  7. Procurement specialist: Please return an initial status today, even if the quantities remain unconfirmed.
  8. Supplier account manager: I can provide that status by the end of our working day without promising shipment.
  9. Procurement specialist: Agreed. We are requesting details, not accepting an undefined partial-delivery arrangement.
  10. Supplier account manager: Understood. I'll label estimates clearly and wait for your reviewed response to the complete proposal.
3. Confirm the OTIF measure before the supplier review

Separate fictional performance meeting: the buyer calculates OTIF by order, while the supplier's presentation calculates it by order line; the joint review is Friday.

  1. Supplier performance analyst: Before Friday's review, please confirm the denominator and delivery basis used in your OTIF presentation.
  2. Supplier representative: Our on-time, in-full figure is calculated by order line. Is your report using the same unit?
  3. Supplier performance analyst: No, ours uses whole orders, so the two percentages aren't directly comparable.
  4. Supplier representative: Would you like us to replace our number with yours before the meeting?
  5. Supplier performance analyst: Please provide the underlying order-line data and definition first so we can reconcile both views.
  6. Supplier representative: I can ask our analyst to prepare that. What timing would support your review?
  7. Supplier performance analyst: An initial data-availability confirmation tomorrow would help us agree a realistic reconciliation schedule.
  8. Supplier representative: I'll confirm availability tomorrow, but I can't promise the reconciled calculation before assessing the work.
  9. Supplier performance analyst: That's fine. Keep the current definition visible rather than present either number as the agreed result.
  10. Supplier representative: Request received. We'll distinguish our existing measure from the common basis still to be agreed.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence explains 'backlog' rather than repeating it?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed.

  1. A. The backlog is the maximum work the team can finish in a period. This describes capacity, not the work waiting to be completed.
  2. B. The backlog is the work still waiting to be completed. This gives the meaning in familiar words.
  3. C. The backlog is the work completed during the last period. Completed work is different from work still waiting.
2. Which follow-up best checks whether your explanation was clear?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. How would you describe the next step in your own words?

  1. A. How would you describe the next step in your own words? An open request lets the listener show their understanding.
  2. B. Would you like me to repeat the explanation? Offering repetition may help, but it does not show how the listener understood the next step.
  3. C. Was the explanation detailed enough for you? This invites an opinion on detail, not a demonstration of understanding.

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. High total stock with two recurring stockouts

Fictional inventory review: total inventory is high, but two important SKUs repeatedly stock out; item-level demand and stock policies need review.

  1. Operations manager: Our total inventory looks healthy. Why are two important items still experiencing stockouts?
  2. Inventory planner: The total combines different SKUs; stock of one item doesn't necessarily satisfy demand for another.
  3. Operations manager: So a large aggregate can hide the wrong mix of inventory across the product range?
  4. Inventory planner: Exactly. We need item-level availability and demand, not only the total value or quantity.
  5. Operations manager: Would increasing safety stock everywhere automatically solve the service-level problem?
  6. Inventory planner: Not necessarily. We should assess the affected items and their replenishment conditions before proposing changes.
  7. Operations manager: Can you explain SKU for the newer colleague joining this review?
  8. Inventory planner: It means stock-keeping unit, the identifier for a particular stocked item in our system.
  9. Operations manager: I'll ask for separate results for the two items rather than treat the headline as reassurance.
  10. Inventory planner: I'll bring the verified item-level picture and keep any policy recommendation tied to the actual evidence.
2. On-hand stock versus stock available to promise

Separate fictional inventory case: SKU Q5 has 100 units on hand, of which 70 are already allocated; no other holds or receipts are included.

  1. Customer service lead: Q5 shows one hundred units on hand. Can I promise all one hundred to this new customer?

03 · Notice the language

Match certainty to evidence

Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.

  • The available evidence shows ... .
  • This may indicate ..., but ... .
  • We have not yet established whether ... .

Improve this: The pilot proves this will work everywhere.

See a clearer version

The pilot worked in the tested setting; performance elsewhere remains untested.

The revision states the finding and its scope. It avoids turning limited evidence into a universal claim.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence preserves uncertainty about an unconfirmed cause?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. The change may have contributed to the delay.

  1. A. The change must have caused the delay. 'Must have' expresses a strong inference that the evidence may not support.
  2. B. The change definitely caused the delay. 'Definitely' presents the cause as certain.
  3. C. The change may have contributed to the delay. 'May have contributed' presents a possibility without claiming the cause is established.
2. A result comes from a small pilot in one location. Which phrase accurately limits the claim?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. In this pilot, the observed result was ...

  1. A. Across the service, the observed result was ... A pilot in one location does not establish a service-wide observation.
  2. B. In this pilot, the observed result was ... This locates the finding in the setting actually studied.
  3. C. In similar locations, we can now expect the same result ... Similarity alone does not establish that the result will transfer.

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which indirect question has the usual word order?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Could you confirm when the review starts?

  1. A. Could you confirm when starts the review? Place the subject 'the review' before 'starts'.
  2. B. Could you confirm when the review starts? The embedded question uses subject then verb: “the review starts”.
  3. C. Could you confirm when does the review start? Remove 'does' and use statement word order after 'confirm'.
2. Someone says the report is 'ready.' Which question best clarifies its approval status?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review?

  1. A. Has the report been approved, or is it ready for review? This question distinguishes two meanings of 'ready' that affect the next action.
  2. B. Can you send the report to me this afternoon? This asks about delivery timing, not whether approval has happened.
  3. C. Who will present the report at the meeting? This asks about the presenter, not the report's approval status.

07Supply Risk and Business ContinuityExplain a fair comparison · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Compare options using the same criteria and state the tradeoff.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

One supplier has a shorter lead time; a second has available backup capacity. The team is considering supply continuity options.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

business continuity
Planning to maintain or restore important operations during disruption.
dual sourcing
Obtaining a product or service from two suppliers to support supply needs or continuity.
allocation
The assignment of available resources, stock, or capacity to particular uses.
constraint
A limit that restricts the available choices or amount of work possible.

03 · Notice the language

Explain a fair comparison

Use the same time period, scope, and measurement basis for both options. Explain a difference with 'whereas' or 'while'. State what improves and what is given up; do not hide the weaker side of a recommendation.

  • Both options ..., but ... .
  • Option A ..., whereas option B ... .
  • The comparison depends on ... .

Improve this: This option is more better.

See a clearer version

This option is better on cost, but slower to deliver.

'Better' already has comparative meaning, so it does not need 'more'. Naming the criterion and tradeoff makes the comparison useful.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which sentence expresses a clear contrast?

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less.

  1. A. Option A is faster, whereas option B costs less. 'Whereas' marks a contrast between two options.
  2. B. Option A is faster because option B costs less. 'Because' claims a causal connection that is not given.
  3. C. Option A is faster, so option B costs less. 'So' implies one fact causes the other.
2. A rate increases from 6% to 7%. What is the absolute difference?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. An increase of one percentage point.

  1. A. An increase of one percent relative to the original rate. A one-percent relative increase on 6% would be 6.06%, not 7%.
  2. B. An increase of seven percentage points. The new rate is 7%; the difference between the rates is one percentage point.
  3. C. An increase of one percentage point. Subtracting 6% from 7% gives one percentage point. The relative increase is about 16.7%.

08Executive S&OP DecisionsHelp a group reach a clear next step · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Include relevant voices and clarify the decision process.

01 · Read the situation

A moment at work

Sales and operations propose different monthly plans. The meeting has ten minutes left and no agreed assumption about the promotion.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

02 · Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

S&OP
Sales and operations planning: coordinating demand, supply, and business plans across teams.
consensus plan
A plan developed from an agreed set of assumptions across relevant teams.
scenario
A description of possible conditions used to explore decisions or practice responses.
owner
Named person or role accountable for a decision, action, deliverable, or risk.

03 · Notice the language

Help a group reach a clear next step

Summarize the issue neutrally, invite missing perspectives, and distinguish discussion from decision. State who can decide and record any unresolved question. Do not assume silence means agreement.

  • Let's hear ... before we decide.
  • The point we still need to resolve is ... .
  • Who will make this decision, and what do they need?

Improve this: Nobody objected, so everyone agrees.

See a clearer version

Before I record agreement, does anyone have a concern or need more time?

The revised question gives people a way to raise a concern. Silence can have several meanings, especially in unfamiliar or unequal-power situations.

04 · Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Which phrase invites a missing perspective?

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view?

  1. A. The remote team has been quiet, so they must agree. Silence does not establish agreement.
  2. B. We can record their agreement without asking them. Recording agreement without checking may misrepresent the team's view.
  3. C. We haven't heard from the remote team yet; what is your view? It invites participation without interpreting silence as consent.
2. Which closing statement records an unresolved decision accurately?

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options.

  1. A. Everyone spoke, so the decision must be final. Participation alone does not establish a decision.
  2. B. Approval is pending; the sponsor will review the two options. This distinguishes discussion from approval and identifies the next reviewer.
  3. C. We discussed the options, so approval is complete. Discussion does not itself constitute approval.

05 · Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Ten minutes to surface the promotion assumption

Fictional S&OP meeting: ten minutes remain. Sales proposes 1,200 monthly units after two retailer inquiries suggesting 200 promotional units, but neither retailer has ordered. Operations has confirmed capacity for 1,000 units; overtime capacity is unconfirmed. Both teams can check their assumptions before tomorrow's review. Director Maya approves the monthly plan.

  1. S&OP chair: We have ten minutes. Sales proposes twelve hundred units; operations plans one thousand.
  2. Sales planning lead: Two retailers asked about promotion stock, so we expect two hundred additional units, but neither has ordered.
  3. S&OP chair: Operations, what constrains the higher scenario, and which capacity is actually confirmed?
  4. Operations lead: We have capacity for one thousand units. Overtime capacity for the extra two hundred is unconfirmed.
  5. S&OP chair: Then inquiries support an opportunity, not confirmed demand. Sales, will you verify the proposed uplift?
  6. Sales planning lead: Yes. I'll ask both retailers for quantity and timing confirmation before tomorrow's review.
  7. S&OP chair: Operations, will you check additional capacity against that same monthly horizon?
  8. Operations lead: I'll own that check and report verified capacity tomorrow, keeping any overtime assumption explicit.
  9. S&OP chair: We have two scenarios and two verification owners. Director Maya retains approval of the monthly plan.
  10. Sales planning lead: Agreed. I'll bring the retailer responses; neither scenario is a consensus commitment yet.
2. A plan labeled consensus before finance speaks

Separate fictional planning meeting: sales and operations support a proposed volume, but finance has not assessed the cash assumption; the chair must clarify decision status.

  1. S&OP facilitator: Sales and operations support the volume, but finance hasn't assessed the cash assumption. This isn't yet a complete consensus plan.
  2. Finance partner: Thank you. I need the purchase timing before I can evaluate the cash implication.
  3. S&OP facilitator: Please explain that dependency, then we'll ask procurement to confirm the proposed timing.
  4. Finance partner: The volume alone doesn't tell us when commitments or payments occur in this scenario.
  5. S&OP facilitator: Can we record your review as pending instead of treating your silence as agreement?
  6. Finance partner: Yes. I'm not rejecting the plan; I'm identifying missing information needed for my assessment.
  7. S&OP facilitator: I'll ask procurement to own the timing clarification and confirm when you can review it.
  8. Finance partner: Once the timing is available, I can give a realistic assessment schedule rather than promise an immediate conclusion.
  9. S&OP facilitator: We'll keep the plan proposed and confirm the decision owner after the relevant inputs are heard.
  10. Finance partner: Agreed. Today's next step is the timing clarification, not an invented finance approval.
3. Different planning horizons in the same vote

Separate fictional executive meeting: sales proposes a one-month plan while operations presents a three-month plan; participants are about to vote without aligning the horizon.

  1. Planning chair: Before we vote, sales is discussing one month and operations is presenting three months.
  2. Operations lead: You're right. Our capacity assumptions cover the quarter, so the proposals aren't aligned.
  3. Planning chair: Let's agree the decision horizon first, then compare the relevant monthly scenario on the same basis.
  4. Operations lead: Could the group simply choose the larger number and treat that as the consensus plan?
  5. Planning chair: That would hide the different time frames rather than resolve the planning question.
  6. Operations lead: I'll separate our first-month view from the remaining two months for the discussion.
  7. Planning chair: Thank you. We'll then invite sales to confirm its demand assumption for that same month.
  8. Operations lead: Who should retain ownership of the quarterly questions that remain outside today's decision?
  9. Planning chair: I'll confirm a named follow-up owner with the group and record those questions as unresolved.
  10. Operations lead: Good. We can reach a bounded planning outcome without treating unmatched horizons as comparable options.

Lesson 01: Demand Planning and Forecast Bias
Original fictional case: A forecast exceeds actual demand for three months. Sales expects a new promotion to improve demand but has no order evidence yet.
Language workshop: Match certainty to evidence
Communication goal: Separate observations, assumptions, and conclusions.
Language guidance: Say what the evidence shows, identify what is still unknown, and limit the conclusion to that evidence. Words such as 'may', 'suggests', and 'in this sample' are useful when the uncertainty is real. Do not soften an urgent, verified safety concern.
Useful frames (complete the gaps with case facts): The available evidence shows ... . / This may indicate ..., but ... . / We have not yet established whether ... .
Vocabulary: forecast: Forward-looking estimate based on assumptions, trends, constraints, and known risks. / forecast bias: A systematic tendency for forecasts to be higher or lower than actual results. / demand signal: Information suggesting what customers may require or purchase. / S&OP: Sales and operations planning: coordinating demand, supply, and business plans across teams.
Speaking task: Prepare for two minutes. Speak for 45-60 seconds using the case facts, then respond to your partner's question. Switch roles and repeat without reading the model response.
Partner: You need to tell a colleague what is known. Ask which part is confirmed and which part is an assumption. Challenge one statement that sounds more certain than the case supports.
