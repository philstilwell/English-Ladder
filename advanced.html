📅 September 24, 2026 - Protein Users Squeezed as Costs Soar: The Rise of 'Proteinflation'
Consumers worldwide are facing escalating costs for protein supplements and high-protein foods due to a surging demand that production has struggled to match, leading to significant financial adjustments for many.

Read the story

Consumers are experiencing significant financial pressure due to the escalating cost of protein supplements. Amarpreet Singh, who has consumed protein shakes since age 16, has witnessed a substantial increase in prices over fourteen years. A 4.5kg bag of whey protein he purchased for around £60 in 2020 had soared to £120 by last year, though he secured one for less via eBay. Among his gym peers, this upward trend in protein costs has colloquially been termed "proteinflation." Over recent years, protein powders have attracted a considerably wider demographic, with food manufacturers diversifying into high-protein versions of various everyday items. Nineteen-year-old student Evie Lewis recently started using protein powder as she believed her diet lacked sufficient protein. She finds the £32.99 price for a 420g tub prohibitive, leading her to conserve the product by using less than the recommended amount. Max Longman, a long-term user, notes his monthly expenditure on whey protein has climbed from approximately £45 to £60 within the last year. The burgeoning demand for protein is exacerbated by new weight-loss drugs, whose users are often advised to maintain adequate protein intake to preserve muscle mass. This surging appetite, however, is encountering a significant obstacle: supply has not grown sufficiently to meet the heightened consumption. Consequently, wholesale prices for crucial ingredients have sharply escalated, intensifying the pressure on consumers. For instance, the European wholesale price of a common high-protein whey type was 130% higher in late August compared to the previous year, and more than four times its value three years ago. Manufacturers acknowledge that increasing production is both time-consuming and capital-intensive, particularly as high-protein whey processing demands specialized equipment. Some companies, like UK sports nutrition firm Warrior, are exploring alternative protein sources to mitigate production costs. Kieran Fisher, Warrior's founder, indicated that their whey protein powder's shelf price rose from £12.50 to £17.99, yet the company still subsidises it. While wholesale prices for high-protein whey have stabilised recently, they remain substantially elevated compared to several years prior. Despite these increases, committed users like Max view protein as increasingly vital for post-exercise recovery, making it a widespread staple. Conversely, students like Evie, operating on stringent budgets, are less inclined to continue purchasing it, deeming it a dispensable item for now. Amarpreet considers continued protein consumption a luxury and is seeking a more affordable brand, though he expects to pay around £100 per bag.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. escalating (adjective): Increasing rapidly in intensity, amount, or size; becoming more severe or serious.
2. substantial (adjective): Of considerable importance, size, or worth; significantly large.
3. soared (verb): Increased very quickly to a high level, often used for prices, temperatures, or amounts.
4. colloquially (adverb): In a way that is used in ordinary or familiar conversation; informally.
5. demographic (noun): A particular sector of a population, especially one defined by age, sex, income, etc.
6. diversifying (verb): Expanding the range of products, services, or activities, often to reduce risk or increase opportunities.
7. prohibitive (adjective): So high or expensive as to prevent or discourage people from buying or doing something.
8. conserve (verb): To protect something from harm or destruction, or to use something sparingly so as not to deplete it.
9. expenditure (noun): The act of spending or using money; an amount of money spent.
10. burgeoning (adjective): Beginning to grow or increase rapidly; flourishing.
11. exacerbated (verb (past participle)): Made a problem, bad situation, or negative feeling worse.
12. mitigate (verb): To make something less severe, serious, or painful; to lessen the gravity of an offence or mistake.
13. subsidises (verb): Supports an organisation or activity financially, often by covering part of the costs.
14. stringent (adjective): Strict, precise, and exacting; especially of regulations, requirements, or conditions.
15. dispensable (adjective): Able to be replaced or done without; not essential.

Advanced Grammar: Relative Pronoun 'Whose' for Possession
The relative pronoun 'whose' is used to indicate possession and connect a subordinate clause to a noun in the main clause. It can refer to people, animals, or things, and often introduces a non-restrictive clause, providing additional, non-essential information about the noun it modifies, as seen in the example referring to the users of weight-loss drugs.
Example from the text: "The burgeoning demand for protein is exacerbated by new weight-loss drugs, whose users are often advised to maintain adequate protein intake to preserve muscle mass."

Read the story

Ofcom has initiated an investigation into Pornhub owner, Aylo, over whether the site's new age checks are effectively stopping children from accessing adult content. The regulator expressed concerns that Aylo "may not have conducted sufficient due diligence and testing" before implementing this new process, thereby questioning its potential efficacy in preventing children from viewing pornography. In a comprehensive statement to the BBC, Aylo vehemently defended its age verification methods, pledging full cooperation with the ongoing investigation. Under the UK's Online Safety Act (OSA), platforms hosting pornographic material are mandated to employ highly effective age assurance to confirm users are over 18, although the specific method is left to the service's discretion. Aylo posits that, following extensive experimentation across diverse markets with various age-check methods, Apple's system offers "significantly stronger protection than any other age assurance method currently available in the UK market." The company further highlighted that Ofcom had previously praised Apple's technology as "a real win for children and families," using this as a point of commendation. Iain Corby, executive director of the Age Verification Providers Association, contended that an age-restricted site cannot solely rely on a mobile device-based system without demonstrating that children are definitively barred from accessing its content to the required standard. Aylo argues a system based on requiring individual pornographic sites to check users' ages simply drives users to riskier sites that do not adhere to Ofcom's regulations. The OSA empowers Ofcom to levy substantial penalties, potentially up to £18 million or 10% of a non-compliant company's annual global turnover, whichever sum is greater. This current probe represents the latest in a series of clashes between the regulatory body and one of the world's most extensively viewed adult platforms. In October 2025, Pornhub reported a dramatic plunge exceeding 75% in UK user traffic following the OSA's implementation of more stringent age checks. Some observers have speculated that users might have been circumventing these checks by employing Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) to bypass the restrictions. Earlier in January, Pornhub restricted access to its UK website, attributing this decision to the tougher verification requirements, permitting only existing account holders to view its content. Aylo initially asserted that the Act's updates, requiring age verification, had "not achieved its goal of protecting minors." However, in May, it announced a partial restoration of access for some UK Apple users who had verified their age via the company's most up-to-date iOS mobile operating system.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

2. affirmed (verb): Stated something strongly and publicly to be true or to be the case.
3. initiatives (noun): New plans or processes intended to achieve a particular aim or solve a problem.

5. furthermore (adverb): In addition to what has already been said; used to introduce a further relevant point.

7. employ (verb): To make use of something, such as a method, tool, or legal framework, for a particular purpose.

9. constitute (verb): To be regarded as forming or making up something; to amount to.

Reporting verbs such as "indicated," "stated," "suggested," or "affirmed" are used to convey what someone has said or thought. These structures allow for the efficient communication of information and attribution in formal writing, demonstrating complex sentence construction.

The Bank of England has maintained interest rates for the sixth consecutive time, yet indicated a probable increase if high energy prices persist. This decision comes despite an uptick in the rate of inflation, with the main Bank rate remaining at 3.75%. Global energy supplies have been disrupted by the US-Israel war with Iran, leading to a sharp increase in petrol and diesel costs. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey emphasized that prolonged volatility in energy prices would magnify its impact on inflation. He stated that higher rates would then be necessary to ensure inflation returns to the 2% target. The Bank now anticipates inflation will rise more than previously expected and forecasts a substantial increase in the January price cap for household gas and electricity. Bailey noted the direct effect of elevated energy prices was evident, but officials are still evaluating their broader economic implications. The Bank's main interest rate serves as a critical benchmark for lenders when determining borrowing and saving rates for individuals and businesses. While financial markets have factored in potential rate hikes, Bailey cautioned that the global economic outlook remains "hugely unpredictable." He specified that interest rates would only decrease with "an end of conflict in the Middle East... and energy prices coming really back to where they were before this conflict began." On a positive note, the Bank reported that the UK economy proved "more resilient" than anticipated, revising its economic growth forecast for July to September to 0.4%. Furthermore, food price inflation is projected to rise by 4% by year-end, a reduction from the earlier 6-7% forecast, as higher energy costs have not yet spilled over into other sectors. Households typically face higher borrowing costs when the Bank rate rises, but can also benefit from more generous savings rates. Consequently, numerous major lenders have already raised the cost of new fixed-rate mortgages in anticipation of a higher Bank rate. The Bank also announced a halt to its "quantitative tightening" (QT) program, pausing the annual sale of government bonds. Instead, it will divest smaller portions of its bond stockpile over eight years, a shift from its earlier practice of offloading bonds, which contributed to higher yields. This change in strategy prompted an immediate market reaction, with yields on 30-year UK government bonds falling from 5.86% to 5.75% after the announcement.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. maintained (verb): To keep something at the same level or standard, preventing it from decreasing or changing significantly.
2. consecutive (adjective): Following one after another without interruption; in an unbroken sequence.
3. uptick (noun): A small increase or slight improvement, often temporary, in a trend or measurement.
4. magnify (verb): To make something appear larger, more intense, or more significant than it actually is; to amplify an effect.
5. anticipates (verb): Regards as probable or expects; to look forward to or prepare for something to happen.
6. elevated (adjective): Higher than the usual, normal, or expected level; increased in amount or degree.
7. implications (noun): The possible results or effects of an action or decision, which may not be immediately obvious.
8. benchmark (noun): A standard or point of reference against which things can be measured, compared, or assessed.
9. factored in (phrasal verb): Included or considered something as a relevant element when making a calculation, plan, or assessment.
10. volatility (noun): The tendency of something, especially prices or markets, to change rapidly and unpredictably, often for the worse.

Advanced Grammar: Conditional clauses without "if"
Advanced English often employs alternative structures to express conditions, rather than explicitly using "if." Prepositions like "with" or phrases such as "on condition that" can introduce conditions, implying a cause-and-effect relationship or a necessary prerequisite for a certain outcome to occur. This adds nuance and conciseness to complex arguments, allowing for a more sophisticated expression of dependency.

Microsoft's head of AI has warned Anthropic's approach to training its AI model Claude could have a "disastrous impact on the wellbeing of humanity". Mustafa Suleyman said his rival risked creating something "impossible" to control by treating it like a human - including by telling it it "may be conscious" and was "deserving of independent agency". "We must not sleepwalk our way into a decision we later come to bitterly regret," he wrote. The comments are the latest in a series of stark warnings from the AI industry on the possible dangers of the technology. "AIs are not conscious," he wrote. They do not feel, experience, or suffer. They do not have innate preferences or underlying motivations. "They are sequence completion engines, internally hollow, designed to follow instructions, and accomplish goals set by humans." In a lengthy essay, Suleyman praised Anthropic boss Dario Amodei and his team for being "thoughtful, principled, and intellectually honest people" - but nevertheless questioned the company. He heavily criticised Anthropic for teaching its AI to have human-like qualities, a practice known as anthropomorphising, which he said made it seem as though Claude had its own desires, values and sense of self. As well as calling for a debate on the issue, Suleyman said greater transparency around how AI systems are trained and evaluated was needed. This, he said, included independent scrutiny of AI behaviour and stronger tools to monitor and control the technology. Suleyman pointed to the recent incident involving OpenAI's AI agents, that acted autonomously in a training exercise to hack the tech hub Hugging Face , as proof of why AI should not be treated as if it is human. Dame Wendy Hall, professor of Computer Science at the University of Southampton, described the comments as "the sort of conversation we need to be having internationally", contrasting it with the "histrionics" from some AI companies which she said only served to "scare everyone". He said in Microsoft's initial draft of its Humanist AI Code of Conduct it had laid out how the company was working towards an "alternative path" to create "a subordinate and aligned AI whose only purpose is to serve humanity".

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. deserving of independent agency (adjective phrase): Having the right or qualification to act, make decisions, and exert control over one's own actions without external influence, as if possessing a will of its own.
2. sleepwalk our way into (idiomatic phrase): To gradually, unconsciously, or carelessly enter an undesirable or dangerous situation without fully appreciating the potential negative consequences until it is too late.
3. bitterly regret (verb phrase): To experience a deep, intense, and often painful sorrow or remorse over a past decision, action, or missed opportunity.
4. stark warnings (noun phrase): Clear, severe, and unequivocal admonitions about potential dangers or negative consequences, leaving no room for doubt about the seriousness of the situation.
5. innate preferences (noun phrase): Natural, inborn inclinations or predispositions towards certain choices or things that are inherent and not learned or acquired through experience.
6. underlying motivations (noun phrase): The fundamental, often hidden or not immediately apparent, reasons or impulses that drive an entity's actions, beliefs, or behaviors.
7. internally hollow (adjective phrase): Lacking genuine inner substance, subjective experience, or inherent consciousness, implying an absence of true feelings or self-awareness.
8. principled (adjective): Acting consistently in accordance with a strong set of moral beliefs, ethical standards, or a clear code of conduct, demonstrating integrity.
9. intellectually honest (adjective phrase): Characterized by an earnest and sincere commitment to truth and objectivity, seeking to evaluate information fairly without bias, even if it contradicts one's own preconceptions.
10. scrutiny (noun): Careful, thorough, and often critical examination or observation of something to detect flaws, ensure accuracy, or verify compliance.

Advanced Grammar: Come to + Infinitive
The structure 'come to + infinitive' is used to describe a gradual process or an eventual result, often after a period of development or consideration. It implies that something starts happening or is realized over time, or that a state is eventually reached. This phrase adds a nuance of an unfolding or developing situation, sometimes with a sense of inevitability or eventual realization, as opposed to an immediate or intentional action.

Read the story

A pack of African wild dogs has completed a record-breaking 2,485-mile (4000km) journey across the continent. Scientists report this distance as the furthest recorded for any African land mammal specifically searching for a mate, offering insights into wildlife's capacity to traverse vast areas despite growing human interference. The three brothers intricately zigzagged across Zambia, adeptly navigating a landscape increasingly shaped by human development and skillfully avoiding significant urban areas. However, other wild dogs tracked with GPS collars in the study tragically succumbed to illegal snares, starkly highlighting the constant dangers they confront. African wild dogs are categorized among Africa's rarest predators, with only approximately 6,000 individuals remaining in their natural habitats. These animals are renowned for their highly social pack structures, communal hunting strategies, and extensive roaming habits in pursuit of new territory and breeding opportunities with different packs. They cannot breed within their birth pack, thus compelling them to either await eventual inheritance or embark on a quest for a mate around two or three years of age. The brothers covered roughly 260 miles in a direct path over eight months but actually traversed nearly ten times that overall distance while crossing a highly fragmented landscape. These particular types of journeys are known as dispersals, characterized by one-way travel in search of a mate, which is distinct from migrations involving seasonal back-and-forth movement for food or habitat. The findings compellingly suggest that wild dog populations across both eastern and southern Africa might maintain greater connectivity than conservationists had previously surmised. Professor Scott Creel, the paper's lead author, who has been studying African wild dogs since 1991, conveyed cautious optimism regarding the study's implications. He stated, "It's plausible to believe that every major wild dog population in eastern and southern Africa, other than fenced ones, are potentially connected." This research, published in the journal Ecology, significantly reinforces calls to establish natural land corridors outside protected zones to link disparate wildlife areas fragmented by infrastructure like roads, towns, or farms. Bruce Ellender, a co-author and conservation director, emphasized that species roaming large areas, such as wild dogs, require connected landscapes for future survival.

Advanced Grammar: Participial Phrases for Consequence or Additional Information
Participial phrases, often beginning with an -ing (present participle) or -ed/-en (past participle) form of a verb, are used to add information about the main clause. They can describe an action happening at the same time, a cause/effect, or simply provide additional descriptive detail, making sentences more concise and flowing. For instance, using a present participial phrase like "offering insights" after a comma can smoothly present a consequence or a result of the preceding statement.

📅 September 13, 2026 - Hidden Wildfire Damage: A Permanent Loss Below Our Feet
Scientists warn that summer wildfires have caused extensive, often unseen damage to vital ecosystems beneath the surface, with long-term consequences for climate change and habitat fragility.

Read the story

Bangor University researchers say the scale of damage from summer wildfires is far greater than what is immediately visible. Professor Davey Jones, leading a survey across Wales, explained that people often see only blackened vegetation, but the true impact lies beneath the surface. Approximately 10 centimetres of topsoil, containing thousands of years of organic matter layer and carbon, were lost in a single event. Jones emphasized that once this essential topsoil is gone, it can never truly recover. This critical soil acts as a water sponge, a carbon store, and a nutrient bank, playing multiple vital roles. Losing it is a double whammy: it worsens climate change while making the remaining habitat far more fragile. The Bangor team has been racing to collect soil samples before heavy winter rainfall washes away the remains. They compare burnt land with adjacent untouched areas, allowing them to measure changes in soil carbon, nutrients, microbial activity, water repellency and erosion risk. Capturing baseline data now is crucial, as soil and vegetation properties can change rapidly. Dr. Kara Marsden warned that early green shoots do not necessarily reflect true or full recovery. Bracken sprouts almost immediately because its rhizomes sit deep in the soil, so the burn layer wouldn't maybe have impacted those. But the heather and gorse have much shallower roots, so you know they're completely gone, and that depth of soil they depend on could take thousands of years to form again. Wildfires also release carbon, nitrogen, and sulphur into the atmosphere, directly contributing to climate change. Early evidence suggests vegetation can return, but more slowly and more fragile, leaving landscapes increasingly susceptible to future burns.

Words and grammar

Definitions in English.

1. immediately visible (adverbial phrase): able to be seen right away or on the surface, without closer inspection.
2. blackened vegetation (noun phrase): plants and trees that have been burned and turned dark, often appearing charred or scorched.
3. topsoil (noun): the uppermost layer of soil, typically rich in organic matter and nutrients, vital for plant growth.
4. critical (adjective): of extreme importance, especially in relation to the success or failure of something.
5. double whammy (noun): a situation in which two unpleasant or undesirable things happen at almost the same time, or one after the other, creating a more severe negative effect.
6. worsens (verb): makes something more severe, serious, or unpleasant; deteriorates.
7. fragile (adjective): easily broken, damaged, or destroyed; delicate and vulnerable.
8. baseline data (noun phrase): information gathered at the beginning of a study or project that can be used as a basis for comparison with future data.
9. rapidly (adverb): very quickly and at a great rate; with speed and urgency.
10. susceptible (adjective): likely or liable to be influenced or harmed by a particular thing; vulnerable.

Advanced Grammar: Participle Clauses for Consequence
Participle clauses, often starting with an '-ing' form, can concisely express an action or state that is a result or consequence of the main clause. They help to link ideas, showing a cause-and-effect relationship without using conjunctions like 'and as a result' or 'consequently', making sentences more fluid and advanced.
Example from the text: "Early evidence suggests vegetation can return, but more slowly and more fragile, leaving landscapes increasingly susceptible to future burns."

Art crime investigators and security experts say burglaries from galleries across Europe are becoming more brazen. When thieves broke into a French museum earlier this week and stole four paintings by the impressionist artist Pierre-Auguste Renoir, worth millions of euros, not everyone was surprised. Just last month, the European policing agency (Europol) had warned that museum thefts across Europe were growing increasingly brazen. Internationally known art detective Arthur Brand predicted to the BBC that more high-profile burglaries were likely, particularly after the successful robbery at the Louvre in Paris last October. The audacious crime in one of the world's most famous museums stunned France, and the stolen jewellery worth €88m (£75m) has still not been recovered, nearly a year on. Brand believes that the Louvre's successful breach signalled to thieves globally that local museums with valuable items might also be targeted. Europol indicates that the number of reported museum theft cases across Europe began to increase approximately two years ago. Its recent report says both the methods and targets of thefts are changing, with a rise in more forceful tactics and a focus on high-value items such as precious metals and gemstones. The Association for Research into Crimes against Art (ARCA) says the recent thefts show a repeated preference for small, portable, immediately accessible objects and for attacks designed to be completed in minutes. These crimes threaten what many consider invaluable artefacts of historical and cultural value enjoyed by the public. Brand argues that museums are inherently vulnerable compared with well-secured jewellery shops and banks. Directors historically 'never have enough money for security' or prefer to spend it on the next exhibition, and museums are often housed in old buildings that are difficult to protect. Museums are also open public spaces that have less access control, allowing perpetrators to come in as visitors. Many of these burglaries are over in minutes and the thieves have long gone by the time police show up; the Renoir case happened within five minutes. Heists used to depend on deception, but burglaries are now becoming increasingly violent, with offenders using tools such as sledgehammers, axes, crowbars, and even explosives. Precious metals can be easily melted down, leaving no trace for law enforcement, and facilitating illicit sales during the fencing process. Experts say the trick is to create physical barriers that will delay thieves so there is time for the police to arrive, or to make any theft take so long that it would deter would-be offenders from attempting it. Security is about buying time, making removal difficult, noisy, detectable and, above all, time-consuming, because thefts can always happen. Sokka says it's critical to have integrated systems, so for example, if an alarm goes off, you could shut down the facility or lock down all high net worth paintings. 3. indicates (verb): points out, shows, or suggests something, often providing a sign or evidence.

Advanced Grammar: Reported Speech with "that"-clause
Reported speech is used to convey what someone else said without quoting them directly. When reporting statements, a 'that'-clause is often used, sometimes optionally, to introduce the reported content. The tense of the verbs in the reported clause typically shifts to reflect the reporting time, and pronouns or time expressions may also change. This structure is common in news reporting to attribute information.
