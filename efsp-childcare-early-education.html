01Arrivals and family partnershipsClarify arrival expectations with the family · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Resolve a settling-in mismatch while including the child in the conversation.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

It is Mina's third morning. Her parent expected to stay for ten minutes, but the arrival note says a brief goodbye. Key educator Rosa is present and can clarify the settling-in plan with the parent. Mina has a familiar cloth; its use follows the center's arrangements.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

key educator
The staff member designated to support a child's continuity of care and family communication; called a key person or primary caregiver in some settings.
settling-in period
The time during which a child and family become familiar with a new early-education setting.
family handover
An exchange of relevant information between a family member and staff when care responsibility changes.
comfort item
A familiar object that a child finds reassuring, used according to the setting's arrangements.

Useful word combinations

  • greet the child by name: address the child directly at arrival
  • share a morning update: pass relevant information from home to the educator
  • agree a goodbye routine: establish a predictable parting arrangement with the family
  • check the settling-in plan: review the current agreed arrival support

C Notice the language

Clarify arrival expectations with the family

Acknowledge the parent's expectation and address the child warmly. Agree what happens today without making a guarantee about tears or separation. Do not treat one difficult arrival as evidence about the family. Some settings call the key educator a key person or primary caregiver; check the center's own role names.

  • You expected ___; our note says ___.
  • Let's check today's settling-in plan with ___.
  • What helped ___ feel familiar yesterday?

Improve this: "You need to leave; the note says so."

See a clearer version

The note and your expectation differ. Let's ask Rosa to clarify today's goodbye plan.

The revision creates a shared understanding rather than blaming the parent.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Parent: Who is our main contact for Mina's settling-in plan?
Educator: Rosa is her ____ here.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. key educator

  1. A. visiting specialist A visiting specialist is not Rosa's stated ongoing role.
  2. B. emergency contact An emergency contact is listed for urgent contact, not this educator role.
  3. C. relief educator A relief educator provides cover rather than the named continuity role.
  4. D. key educator Correct. Rosa is the designated continuing family contact here.
2. Parent: Will she definitely stop crying immediately?
Educator: I can explain our support, but I can't ____ exactly how she will feel.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. guarantee

  1. A. notice Notice means observe, not promise an outcome.
  2. B. ask about Ask about seeks information rather than predicting feelings.
  3. C. describe Describe reports something; the question asks about certainty.
  4. D. guarantee Correct. Guarantee would promise an outcome we cannot establish.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. Mina's third morning

Fictional arrival conversation using the lesson case.

  1. Parent: I expected ten minutes together during this settling-in period. Why does the note say brief goodbye?
  2. Educator: I can see the difference. Rosa, Mina's key educator, is here to clarify today's settling-in plan.
  3. Parent: Mina brought her familiar cloth. Yesterday she wanted it while looking around the room.
  4. Educator: Thanks. I'll include that comfort item in the family handover, without assuming Mina wants it all morning.
  5. Parent: Could you promise me she won't cry once I leave? That is what worries me.
  6. Educator: I can't promise her feelings, but Rosa can explain how we'll support her and communicate with you.
  7. Parent: Please ask Rosa before I say goodbye, so Mina doesn't hear two different plans.
  8. Educator: Yes. Mina, your parent and I are checking today's plan with Rosa together.
  9. Parent: I'd like consistent goodbye words, even if today's arrangement needs clarification.
  10. Educator: Let's agree those words with Rosa now, then we can all give Mina one clear message.
2. The morning update is from home

Fictional arrival handover; parent reports a disrupted night without requesting medical advice.

  1. Parent: Owen woke earlier than usual and seems quieter to me this morning. I wanted you to know.
  2. Educator: I'll identify that as your home observation, not a conclusion about the day.
  3. Parent: Please don't assume he is upset with the classroom. He hasn't told me that.
  4. Educator: I won't assign a reason. Is there anything else you want his key educator to hear?
  5. Parent: He asked for the book corner when we arrived. That's his familiar place here.
  6. Educator: I'll tell her about that request and the earlier waking, keeping the two facts separate.
  7. Parent: Could you share actual observations later, rather than just saying he was fine?
  8. Educator: Yes. We can share concrete observations in the family handover, within our usual contact arrangements.
  9. Parent: Concrete observations would help me understand the morning without guessing from his quiet arrival.
  10. Educator: I'll give his key educator the update now and mention his request for the book corner.
3. A name pronounced correctly

Fictional first-week family conversation; educator checks the child's name.

  1. Educator: I want to say Leila's name correctly. Could you model how your family pronounces it?
  2. Parent: Of course. The first sound is longer than the way you said it yesterday.
  3. Educator: Thank you. Let me try it again, and please correct me if I miss it.
  4. Parent: That's closer. Leila notices when people rush the name, though she may not correct adults herself.
  5. Educator: I'll take time with it and ask colleagues to listen to your pronunciation too.
  6. Parent: Could you also use the same name, rather than shortening it without asking her?
  7. Educator: Yes. We can use Leila and check her preference instead of choosing a nickname.
  8. Parent: She has been quiet at arrival, but she told me she likes hearing her name properly.
  9. Educator: That's useful to know. I'll greet her directly and give her time to answer.
  10. Parent: Thank you. I'll help her hear the same pronunciation when we introduce ourselves to other staff.

Try a harder second round

Scenario 2 · A different adult at drop-off

Amir arrives with his grandparent instead of his parent. The grandparent can share the morning update but does not know the agreed settling-in plan. Key educator Rosa is present and has the plan.

Role A: Educator: welcome Amir and clarify the plan with Rosa.

Role B: Grandparent: you were asked to bring Amir but cannot confirm a change to the goodbye routine.

Possible opening: “I usually don't do drop-off. Should I stay, or say goodbye now?”

Success checks
  • Use Amir's own agreed plan, not another family's arrangement.
  • Include the grandparent's useful morning information.
  • Check uncertainty with Rosa without criticizing anyone.
Add a complication

A nearby family offers its own goodbye arrangement as the rule for everyone.

Studying alone? Speak both roles aloud. For each scenario, prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then answer a follow-up. Switch roles and repeat using fewer notes. Use only the supplied facts; identify missing information instead of inventing it.

Compare scenario 1 with a model response
You expected ten minutes together, while our arrival note says a brief goodbye. Let's check that difference with Rosa, Mina's key educator, before describing today's plan. Mina, you brought your cloth. Let's talk to Rosa together before you say goodbye.

This is one possible spoken response, not the only acceptable wording. Preserve the case facts when you try your own version.

Apply it to this case

Check your reasoning

Compare with the case above and the model response. Can you point to the words that support your factual statement? Is your question about something the case leaves open? If you introduce a possible outcome, clearly label it as a possibility.

Review, then try again

  • Keeps the case facts accurate; clearly separates confirmed and unknown information.
  • Uses understandable sentences and explains specialist terms when the listener needs it.
  • Responds to the other person's question, checks understanding, and uses an appropriate tone.
  • Makes the requested action or unresolved question clear without inventing authority or facts.

Revise one sentence and repeat the response. A useful response can be clear even with a few grammar errors; judge meaning and task completion, not accent.

02Play-based learning and useful promptsInvite thinking without taking over the play · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Use prompts that help children explain and compare their own ideas.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

In fictional block play, Eva and Jo each want their bridge design used. Eva wants a wide bridge; Jo wants a taller opening. The educator can invite both explanations and suggest trying two designs using available blocks. No winner has been chosen.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

child-initiated play
Play whose idea or direction begins with the child rather than being assigned by an adult.
guided play
Play that retains children's active choices while an educator offers prompts or support toward a learning purpose.
scaffolding
Temporary support that helps a child do or understand something without taking over the task.
open-ended question
A question inviting explanation, exploration, or several possible answers rather than one fixed response.

Useful word combinations

  • follow the child's interest: build a conversation from what the child is exploring
  • offer a small prompt: give limited support without taking over
  • extend the play: invite a new idea that builds on the child's activity
  • leave room for an answer: allow time for the child to respond in their own way

C Notice the language

Invite thinking without taking over the play

An open-ended question invites more than a guessed adult answer. Scaffolding offers enough help to continue while leaving meaningful choices with children. Adult language can be clear without directing every action.

  • What would you like your ___ to do?
  • How could you test ___?
  • You suggested ___; what does your partner think?

Improve this: "Build my bridge; this is the right design."

See a clearer version

You have two designs. What would each let you try, and which will you test first?

The new question retains the children's ideas and gives discussion a practical purpose.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Colleague: Which prompt invites several possible ideas?
Educator: ____

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. What might make the bridge steadier?

  1. A. What might make the bridge steadier? Correct. It invites suggestions rather than one fixed answer.
  2. B. Did you use two blocks? This checks a specific count rather than exploring ideas.
  3. C. Is the tower tall? This invites a yes-or-no response about height.
  4. D. Is that a bridge? This checks a label with a likely short answer.
2. Colleague: You gave a temporary hint so the child could continue independently; you didn't score or demonstrate the whole task.
Educator: That support was ____.

Answer and explanations

Answer: B. scaffolding

  1. A. a retrospective observation A retrospective observation describes earlier events rather than providing support now.
  2. B. scaffolding Correct. A temporary hint supports the child's own continuing effort.

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

transition
The change from one activity, place, or part of the day to another.
visual schedule
A sequence of pictures or symbols showing planned parts of the day.
transition cue
A consistent signal that an activity change is approaching or beginning.
self-help skill
An everyday action a child is learning to manage with increasing independence and suitable support.

Useful word combinations

  • give a transition cue: signal that the next activity is approaching
  • check the visual schedule: refer to the pictures showing the day's sequence
  • offer a bounded choice: provide a small set of real options within the routine
  • allow time to finish: give the agreed opportunity to bring an activity to a stopping point

C Notice the language

Offer a real choice inside a clear routine

A bounded choice gives meaningful options while keeping the known routine explicit. Use before and after carefully. Do not threaten, shame, or promise unlimited extra time.

  • First ___, then ___.

04Co-regulation and peer disagreementsDescribe the disagreement without choosing a villain · 45-60 minutes+

Your goal

Help children express needs and agree a concrete next step.

A Read the situation

A moment at work

Two children reach for the same large scoop. The educator saw Ari holding it, then Bea reaching; motive is unknown. A smaller scoop is available. Ari wants to finish one container. Bea wants the large scoop next. No injury or ongoing danger is stated.

Before you look at the model: what is confirmed, what is missing, and who needs a response?

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

co-regulation
Supportive adult interaction that helps a child manage feelings and behavior while developing self-regulation.
turn-taking
Sharing participation or access through successive turns.
peer conflict
A disagreement between children that needs understanding and appropriate support.
repair attempt
An action or message intended to address the effect of a disagreement or mistake.

Useful word combinations

  • name the feeling tentatively: offer a possible feeling word without claiming to know the child's mind
  • describe the observed action: say what happened without labeling the child's character
  • support turn-taking: help children agree and understand whose turn comes next
  • invite a repair attempt: support a practical response to the effect on another child

C Notice the language

Describe the disagreement without choosing a villain

Use observed actions, not claims about motives or character. Co-regulation includes calm language and time to respond. A repair attempt should address the effect; a forced apology alone may not clarify the problem.

  • I saw ___; I don't yet know ___.
  • You wanted ___, and your friend wanted ___.
  • What could help us put ___ right?

Improve this: "You're always selfish with the toys."

See a clearer version

You both want the large scoop. Let's agree whose turn it is and what happens next.

The revision focuses on the current disagreement and a workable next action.

D Check your understanding

Two short language checks

These language patterns recur across courses so you can retrieve and reuse them.

1. Colleague: Which statement describes an observed action rather than a motive?
Educator: ____

Answer and explanations

Answer: C. Ari held the scoop when Bea reached for it.

  1. A. Ari wanted to annoy Bea. Wanting to annoy attributes an unverified intention.
  2. B. Ari dislikes sharing. This generalizes a character trait from one event.
  3. C. Ari held the scoop when Bea reached for it. Correct. This names the actions actually observed.
  4. D. Ari planned to upset the group. Planning to upset others invents a motive.
2. Child: I want the scoop after Ari finishes.
Educator: You are asking for the ____ turn.

Answer and explanations

Answer: A. next

  1. A. next Correct. Next identifies the following turn.
  2. B. simultaneous Simultaneous means happening at the same time.
  3. C. cancelled Cancelled means a turn will not take place.
  4. D. previous Previous refers to a turn before the current one.

E Conversations

Three conversations at work

Original fictional training conversations. Each line is one speaking turn.

1. The large scoop's next turn

Fictional educator and Bea resolve the lesson's shared-scoop disagreement.

  1. Educator: Bea, I saw you reach for the scoop Ari was holding. What did you want?
  2. Child: I wanted the large scoop. The little one takes longer to fill my container.
  3. Educator: Ari wants to finish this container, and you want the next turn with the large scoop.
  4. Child: Yes, but I don't want everyone else to get it before I do.
  5. Educator: Let's agree a turn-taking order: Ari finishes this container, then you have the large scoop.
  6. Child: Could I use the small scoop while I wait, without losing my turn?
  7. Educator: Yes, it is available. Using it doesn't change the order we just discussed.
  8. Child: I thought Ari was being mean, but maybe Ari didn't know I wanted it next.
  9. Educator: We don't know Ari's thoughts yet. You can say, I'd like the next turn, please.
  10. Child: I'll say that now and use the small scoop while this container is finished.
2. A fallen structure and a repair

Fictional accidental bump during play; no injury or disciplinary procedure.

  1. Educator: Your elbow touched the blocks, and part of the structure fell. What happened from your view?
  2. Child: I was reaching for the box. I didn't see the corner sticking out.
  3. Educator: Your friend looks upset about the fallen part. What could you offer to help put it right?
  4. Child: I could ask which pieces belonged on top, then help rebuild that part.
  5. Educator: Ask your friend where those pieces go before moving them. They might want to show you.
  6. Child: Should I say sorry even if I didn't mean to knock it down?
  7. Educator: You can say you're sorry it fell and explain the bump without pretending you meant it.
  8. Child: I'll say, I'm sorry I knocked that part down. Would you like help rebuilding it?
  9. Educator: That names the effect and offers help. Give your friend time to answer.
  10. Child: I'll wait for their answer instead of deciding how the whole structure should look myself.
3. A feeling word is a possibility

Fictional educator-colleague reflection on co-regulation language.

  1. Educator: I called the child angry, but they said they were sad about the unfinished picture.
  2. Colleague: Their correction matters. We can offer a feeling word without treating our first guess as fact.
  3. Educator: Could I tentatively say you seem disappointed, then invite the child's own words?
  4. Colleague: Yes, if it fits the moment. Keep the invitation open instead of demanding agreement with your label.
  5. Educator: I also spoke quickly because the group was waiting for the next activity.
  6. Colleague: A calmer pace may give them room to respond. Co-regulation isn't just finding the perfect word.
  7. Educator: I'll acknowledge their word sad and ask what part of the picture they wanted to finish.
  8. Colleague: Then help identify a practical next step within today's routine, without promising unlimited time.
  9. Educator: The picture can stay in their tray under our current arrangement. I can offer that.
  10. Colleague: That connects the feeling conversation to a real option and respects the child's account.

B Find the words

Vocabulary for this lesson

observation note
A factual record of what an educator saw or heard in a stated context.
learning portfolio
A collection of selected observations and work showing a child's experiences and learning over time.
developmental milestone
A commonly described skill or behavior associated with development, not a diagnosis or universal timetable for an individual child.
developmental screening
A structured process used by appropriately qualified people to identify whether closer developmental review may be needed.

Useful word combinations

  • anchor an observation in context: state when and where an action occurred
  • quote the child's words: preserve a short accurate example of what the child said
  • compare observations over time: look at dated evidence rather than a single impression
  • raise a developmental question: invite the appropriate review without diagnosing a child

C Notice the language

Two short language checks

1. Colleague: Did you score a screening tool or set a future target?

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. observation note

  1. A. assessment score An assessment score expresses a rating, not this factual quotation.
  2. B. individual target An individual target sets a future aim rather than recording speech.
  3. C. interpreted screening outcome A screening outcome comes from a screening process, which did not occur.
  4. D. observation note Correct. The note records the child's words in the observed context.
2. Parent: Does this single activity establish his overall development?
Educator: It gives evidence ____ this activity, not a complete developmental assessment.

Answer and explanations

Answer: D. within

  1. A. regardless of Regardless of would remove the important context.
  2. B. prior to Prior to would place the evidence before the activity.
  3. C. instead of Instead of would replace rather than define the activity's scope.
  4. D. within Correct. Within limits the evidence to its observed context.
