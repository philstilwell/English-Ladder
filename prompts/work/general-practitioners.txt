Lesson 01: Agreeing on a consultation agenda
Original fictional case: Dr Shah sees Morgan for a planned long-term-condition review. Morgan also wants to discuss tiredness and a form for work. No clinical urgency assessment has yet been made. Morgan says the tiredness matters most. Dr Shah will first clarify that concern and assess priorities, then explain what can be covered and what requires follow-up. No symptom is declared safe to postpone merely because the appointment was booked for another purpose.
Language workshop: Three concerns, one visit
Communication goal: Negotiate priorities without dismissing a newly disclosed concern.
Language guidance: A booked review does not determine the urgency of a newly raised symptom. Agree on the agenda after appropriate clinical assessment, not before it.
Useful expressions (preserve the supplied facts): What else were you hoping we could cover? / The tiredness is the main concern for you today. / I will clarify that concern before we agree on the order. / I am not setting it aside because it was not on the booking. / The work form is still on our list. / Let us agree how to handle anything we cannot complete today. / First I will ask about the tiredness; then we will review the plan. / Have I captured the concerns in the right order? / Please stop me if I have misunderstood. / In everyday language, that means the following. / That remains uncertain; it is not a confirmed finding. / Let me explain that another way. / We can pause before moving to the next point. / Let us confirm who will follow up. / I will distinguish what was reported from what was observed. / We have agreed on the next step, not on an outcome.
Vocabulary: consultation agenda: Concerns and tasks agreed for a clinical encounter. / presenting concern: Problem the patient brings to the current encounter. / continuity of care: Connected care over time with an ongoing clinical relationship. / comorbidity: Another condition occurring alongside a particular condition. / multimorbidity: Presence of multiple long-term health conditions in one person. / problem list: Organized record of a patient's identified health problems. / acute concern: A new or recently changing health concern. / long-term condition: Health condition requiring ongoing management or follow-up. / patient-centered care: Care attentive to the person's needs, priorities, and circumstances. / functional limitation: Restriction affecting a person's usual activities or abilities. / follow-up visit: Later encounter to review progress or unfinished care. / priority: Issue judged to require attention before other issues. / shared agenda: Visit priorities agreed through discussion with the patient. / visit length: Time allocated or used for an encounter. / care coordination: Organizing related care across people, services, or settings. / concern elicitation: Inviting the patient to describe what needs attention. / signposting: Explaining where a conversation is going next. / safety-netting: Explaining what changes require further help and how to obtain it. / qualified interpreter: Trained language professional supporting accurate communication between speakers. / teach-back: Checking an explanation by inviting the listener to restate its meaning. / patient preference: A person's stated wishes about care or communication. / clinical handoff: Transfer of relevant care information and responsibility between professionals. / documented plan: Agreed next steps recorded for the people involved. / source attribution: Identification of who reported, observed, or established a fact.
Speaking task: Read the corrected agenda exchange. Stress tiredness, review, and work form as three separate concerns. Swap roles. Repeat the signposting lines and the distinction between a proposed agenda and assessed priorities. Complete the four-line follow-up with the supplied words. Check every explanation, then read the corrected exchange twice, changing roles.
Partner: Dr Shah: General practitioner / Morgan: Patient
Guided response task: Select supplied answers for the dialogue gaps. Check the explanations, then read the completed exchange aloud; no original writing is required.
Optional second-round challenge: Patient Lee attends a blood-pressure review and raises a new sleep concern. Dr Chen will clarify the sleep concern before agreeing on priorities. A travel form remains unfinished.
Model for comparison AFTER my attempt, not a response to give me first: Dr Shah: Correct. We also need to clarify the form's requirements before promising what I can certify.
Morgan: Then our shared agenda begins with the tiredness, while the review and form remain visible.
Dr Shah: That captures your preference. I will assess the concern before we settle the clinical order and next steps.
Morgan: Good. I want continuity of care, not three disconnected conversations in which the details get lost.

Lesson 02: Taking a precise symptom history
Original fictional case: Ari uses the word dizzy for episodes that began on Tuesday. Ari describes feeling about to faint, not the room spinning. Episodes are reported after standing, and their duration is not yet clear. Dr Cole has not established a cause. The task is to clarify the account and record relevant unknowns, not diagnose from one trigger or assume unmentioned symptoms are absent.
Language workshop: Dizziness means different things

Morgan: Good. I want continuity of care, not three disconnected conversations in which the details get lost.

Professional roles: Dr Cole / Ari

Ari: More like I might faint than like the room is spinning. The onset was Tuesday.
Dr Cole: Thank you. Did you feel it continuously from Tuesday, or have there been separate episodes?
Ari: Separate episodes. I suppose episodic describes it, but I cannot time them accurately yet.
Dr Cole: That is useful to know. How often have you noticed it, and what are you doing when it starts?
Ari: Standing up seems to be a trigger, although I have not kept a record of every episode.
Dr Cole: I will record that as your observation. What happens next, and what makes the feeling ease?

Dr Cole: Please do not guess. I will ask about other symptoms separately rather than treating silence as an answer.
Ari: So an associated symptom is something else happening during the same episode?
Dr Cole: Yes. I also need to understand any changes and how the episodes affect what you can do.
Ari: The frequency may be increasing, but I should explain what I actually remember.
Dr Cole: Exactly. Start with Tuesday and describe the later events in order, including anything that is uncertain.
Ari: That will keep the chronology straight instead of mixing the first episode with the most recent one.
Dr Cole: I will summarize your account, then assess it. The standing association alone does not establish a diagnosis.
Ari: I understand the diagnostic uncertainty. You are listening to the pattern, not promising an explanation yet.
Dr Cole: Correct. Any relevant absence I record will come from a question or assessment, not from an empty field.
Ari: Then a pertinent negative is different from something I simply have not mentioned.

Dialogue 04: Outside the range is not the diagnosis
Original fictional setting: Casey sees an out-of-range flag on a blood-test report before speaking with Dr Vega. No numerical value or diagnosis is supplied in this case. Dr Vega will review the result alongside symptoms, previous results, and sample context. A repeat test is being considered, not yet ordered. Casey fears that the flag means a serious diagnosis is certain.
Professional roles: Casey / Dr Vega
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Casey: The portal highlighted my blood result. I saw the warning before anyone explained what it meant.
Dr Vega: I understand why the out-of-range flag worried you. Let us separate the marker from its clinical meaning.
Casey: Does outside the range mean that I definitely have the condition I read about online?
Dr Vega: Not by itself. The reference interval helps comparison, but we must interpret the result in context.
Casey: What context do you need before you can tell me more about this particular result?
Dr Vega: Your symptoms, earlier results, and the sample circumstances help us assess its clinical significance.
Casey: I remember having the same test previously. Will you compare those reports rather than only this one?
Dr Vega: Yes, where they are comparable. A trend can add information that one isolated result cannot provide.
Casey: I do not know whether the earlier value was typical for me or just another unusual day.
Dr Vega: We should not assume it is your baseline without reviewing the relevant history and circumstances.
Casey: Could something about the sample affect the result, or would that just be an excuse to ignore it?
Dr Vega: Sample context can matter. Reviewing preanalytical variation does not mean declaring that the laboratory made an error.
Casey: Are you ordering the test again now, or is that only something we might discuss?
Dr Vega: A repeat sample is under consideration. I have not ordered it in this discussion.
Casey: So there is no new result waiting somewhere that I have failed to look at?
Dr Vega: Correct. A pending result would be a different situation from considering whether to repeat the test.
Casey: I would rather hear that something is uncertain than be reassured today and surprised tomorrow.
Dr Vega: That is reasonable. A working diagnosis must be described as provisional while we evaluate the evidence.
Casey: I understand the difference now: the flag needs review, but it is not the diagnosis itself.
Dr Vega: Exactly. We will explain the plan, including whether a confirmatory test is appropriate and who will communicate any subsequent result.
Vocabulary in this exchange: reference interval: Range used by a laboratory for comparison with a result. / out-of-range flag: Indicator that a result lies outside a stated comparison range. / trend: Pattern of change across comparable observations over time. / baseline: Earlier or usual state used for comparison. / preanalytical variation: Differences arising before a sample is analyzed. / repeat sample: New specimen obtained to repeat an investigation.

Dialogue 05: A recommendation without a dismissal
Original fictional setting: After an assessment, Dr Rao has not identified an indication for an antibiotic in this fictional visit. Jules expected one because a previous illness improved after antibiotics. The case does not provide diagnostic findings, a named infection, or medicine instructions. The conversation explains the current recommendation, distinguishes sequence from proof of benefit, and includes an individualized follow-up and safety-net discussion with the actual clinician.
Professional roles: Jules / Dr Rao
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Jules: Last time I felt like this, antibiotics helped. I expected to leave with the same treatment today.
Dr Rao: I understand that treatment expectation. Based on today's assessment, I am not recommending an antibiotic.
Jules: Are you saying the improvement last time was not real? I remember feeling much better afterward.
Dr Rao: The improvement was real to you. But improvement afterward does not establish the medicine's expected benefit in this situation.
Jules: I mainly need to feel better and get back to work. I do not want to be dismissed.
Dr Rao: Your need for symptom relief matters. Not recommending this medicine is not the same as offering no care.
Jules: Then please explain what separates my request from a clinical reason to prescribe the medicine.
Dr Rao: A treatment indication depends on the current assessment and evidence, not only on an earlier prescription.
Jules: Is the concern just about avoiding unnecessary cost, or can an unnecessary medicine cause problems too?
Dr Rao: There can be potential harm, as well as a lack of benefit. We should discuss that honestly without overstating either.
Jules: I have heard people say their bodies become resistant. Is that the right way to describe it?
Dr Rao: Antimicrobial resistance concerns microbes that withstand medicines. It is not your body becoming resistant to treatment.
Jules: I follow that distinction. I still want to know what happens if the illness does not develop as expected.
Dr Rao: That needs a clear contingency plan, including the appropriate route for further help and reassessment.
Jules: Please make that specific to me rather than just saying to come back if I am worried.
Dr Rao: We will discuss individualized return precautions and check that the instructions and contact route are clear.
Jules: Then a later review would not mean that I had failed to follow today's recommendation?
Dr Rao: Correct. Reassessment responds to the clinical situation; it is not a judgment about you.
Jules: I can accept a different recommendation when I understand the reasoning and know the next step.
Dr Rao: That is the aim of antibiotic stewardship alongside good care: explain the decision, address your needs, and keep follow-up clear.
Vocabulary in this exchange: antibiotic stewardship: Responsible use of antibiotics to preserve benefit and limit avoidable harm. / antibiotic: Medicine used against susceptible bacterial infections. / symptom relief: Reduction of an uncomfortable symptom. / antimicrobial resistance: Ability of microbes to withstand medicines intended to act against them. / treatment indication: Clinical reason supporting use of a particular treatment. / return precautions: Instructions about changes requiring further medical attention.

Original fictional setting: Dr Byrne referred Fin to a specialist for an unresolved clinical question. The receiving office confirms receipt, but has not confirmed an appointment. Practice coordinator Mina is checking the status and will update Fin on Friday. Friday is a status-update commitment, not a clinical waiting instruction or appointment date. New or worsening concerns must use the practice's actual clinical route.
Professional roles: Fin / Dr Byrne
Published dialogue (reference script, not my own performance):
Fin: I was told the referral had gone through. Does that mean the specialist has booked me in?
Dr Byrne: We have referral receipt, but an appointment has not yet been confirmed.
Fin: Thank you for separating those. I heard gone through and assumed there was a date somewhere.
Dr Byrne: Appointment confirmation is a separate step. Mina is checking the status with the receiving office.
Fin: What exactly is the specialist being asked to address? I do not want to arrive without understanding that.
Dr Byrne: We will review the consultation question with you and make sure the relevant records accompany the request.
Fin: I can explain my concern, but I may not remember every detail that is already in your notes.
Dr Byrne: Appropriate record transfer helps the receiving team. It does not replace listening to your current account.
Fin: Mina mentioned Friday. Is that when the consultation will happen, or when someone will contact me?
Dr Byrne: Friday is a status update. The pending action is checking progress, not attending a confirmed appointment.
Fin: Who should deal with questions before the specialist sees me? I do not want to fall between teams.
Dr Byrne: We must clarify interim care and the outstanding responsibilities rather than assume everything transferred when we sent the request.
Fin: If something changes clinically before Friday, should I wait for Mina's administrative update to mention it?
Dr Byrne: No. Use the actual clinical escalation route for new or worsening concerns. The update date is not advice to wait.
Fin: I also need calls at a time I can answer privately. Can we agree on how the update reaches me?
Dr Byrne: Yes, we will verify your contact preference and use the practice's approved communication process.
Fin: Once the appointment happens, will the specialist's advice come back here, or do I have to relay everything?
Dr Byrne: We will track the consultant letter and review the responsibilities it identifies, rather than assume attendance alone closes every question.
Fin: That is clearer: receipt now, a status update Friday, and no appointment date promised yet.
Dr Byrne: Exactly. A closed-loop referral keeps the stages and responsibilities connected until the relevant follow-up is complete.
Vocabulary in this exchange: referral: Request for another professional or service to assess or assist. / consultation question: Specific issue the referring clinician asks the receiving service to address. / referral receipt: Confirmation that the receiving service has obtained the referral. / appointment confirmation: Verification that a specific appointment has been arranged. / closed-loop referral: Referral process tracking receipt, follow-up, and information returned to the referrer. / consultant letter: Communication from a specialist summarizing an assessment or advice.
